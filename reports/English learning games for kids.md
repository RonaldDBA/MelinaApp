# Build English by ear, then by eye

The app should add English the way the evidence and Québec's own program both point: **listening first, a small set of words taught intensively over several days, spaced retrieval rather than re-exposure, prompts before answers when the child errs, and reading only after an oral base exists**. For a 2e année francophone, the school target is the end of Cycle One, which is oral-only and roughly equivalent to CEFR Pre-A1. That makes the first two of six app levels "school-aligned", while levels 3 to 6 climb through Cycle Two and Three content (word decoding, sentences, spelling, simple past) toward the A2 edge. The existing adaptive engine fits this well, with three changes. It needs a guessing floor for multiple-choice items. It should track each word in separate modalities (heard, read, spelled), because recognition is easier than recall. And it needs hard gates so its "explore one level up" behaviour cannot push English reading onto a child who is still consolidating French decoding. The highest-value first release is two listening games, picture choice and an action-command game. Minimal-pair sound games come next for the traps francophones reliably hit (/h/, "th", ship/sheep), then story listening. Phonics and spelling come last. The weakest links are audio and evidence. Synthetic speech is measurably harder for child and bilingual listeners, so core words and every minimal pair need adult-checked audio. And almost none of the research is on 7-year-old francophones learning English through an app, so the app's own retention data has to become the final judge.

## Small word sets and spaced retrieval beat songs and passive exposure

The strongest evidence for young L2 vocabulary comes from the IES/WWC practice guide for English learners. It rates "teach a set of academic vocabulary words intensively across several days using a variety of instructional activities" as **Strong**: six studies met its standards, and the how-to calls for a small set of words taught across writing, speaking and listening, plus word-learning strategies including cognates ([IES/WWC English learners guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)). Its second Strong recommendation is to anchor instruction with visuals and short videos. The same guide rates structured writing only **Minimal**, which justifies keeping writing at the top of the ladder ([IES/WWC guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)). Repetition alone is weaker than folk wisdom suggests. A meta-analysis of 26 studies (N = 1,918) found a medium correlation between the number of encounters and incidental word learning, **r = .34** ([Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa 2019](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12343)). A secondary summary reports that the effect is smaller for primary-age learners than for university students ([reference-global summary](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/eip-2023-0001.pdf)). Young children therefore need deliberate, varied retrieval, not just more exposure.

Spacing is the most reliable lever, and its shape matters less than its amount. A spaced-practice meta-analysis of 48 experiments found a **medium-to-large** effect. Short gaps did as well as long gaps on immediate tests but worse on delayed ones ([Western University thesis record of the Kim & Webb meta-analysis](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)). Nakata found that the *amount* of spacing had a far larger effect than expanding versus equal intervals ([Nakata 2015, SSLA](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-expanding-and-equal-spacing-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/D1D796306985C52F9BE7A1200AC50DB9)). His later review concluded that expanding schedules have limited pedagogical value ([Nakata review](https://kansai-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/10256/files/KU-1100-20181000-02.pdf)). The practical consequence is that a plain day-level review ladder is enough. The app does not need a finely tuned SRS curve before it has data.

Two cheap multimodal tricks have direct support with French-speaking children. Tellier taught 8 English words to 20 French children averaging 5;5 years. Children who **reproduced a gesture** while repeating the word recalled more words productively than children who only saw pictures ([Tellier 2008, Gesture](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00375251/file/3420.pdf)). Songs, by contrast, are a wrapper, not a method. Three song-based lessons with 25 Spanish 5-year-olds were **insufficient to build productive knowledge** in most children ([Coyle & Gómez Gracia 2014](https://catalogosiidca.csuca.org/Record/CR.UNA01000275527/Description)). Another study found no recall difference between words met in songs and words met in stories ([IES REL West summary of Pichette & Leśniewska](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/edlabs/regions/west/Ask/Details/80)). Original chants should therefore recycle a unit's words and always be followed by a retrieval game.

How the app reacts to errors matters as much as what it teaches. In Québec-area French immersion classes, teachers mostly used recasts (simply reformulating correctly), and these produced little learner repair ([Lyster & Ranta 1997, McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/0p096b851)). A meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies (N = 827) found **prompts beat recasts**, and **younger learners benefited more from corrective feedback** than older ones ([Lyster & Saito 2010, SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)). The child-specific twist comes from literacy-game research. Children benefited from simple outcome feedback, but struggled to understand verbal explanatory feedback, including EFL learners ([Vasalou et al., BJET](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10129142/7/Vasalou_bjet.13145.pdf)). The right "prompt" for a 7-year-old is therefore sensory: replay the word, contrast the two sounds, narrow the choices. It should not be a grammar explanation.

Digital delivery itself is not the active ingredient. Technology-assisted vocabulary meta-analyses report sizeable effects: **d = 0.845** across 45 studies ([Hao et al., WSU](https://showcase.wsu.edu/2020/04/22/technology-assisted-vocabulary-learning-for-efl-learners-a-meta-analysis/)) and **d = 0.64** across 34 studies, where college students gained more than K–12 learners ([ReCALL meta-analysis](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/08A549A6CFD1078406E6A4F8AFE28184/S0958344021000239a.pdf/a-meta-analysis-examining-technology-assisted-l2-vocabulary-learning.pdf)). For games, a between-groups effect of **d = 0.50** was found, and entertainment-designed games outperformed education-designed ones ([Dixon, Dixon & Jordan 2022, LLT](https://www.lltjournal.org/item/10125-73464/)). Game design features moderated the effect, but learner age did not ([Chen, Tseng & Hsiao 2018, BJET](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/24BG25I9)). The lesson for this app is that the existing garden narrative and stage are worth reusing. The gains will come from the retrieval, spacing and feedback built into it, and parent-facing expectations for a young child should stay modest.

## Québec asks a 2e année child to listen and join in, not to read

Québec runs two different elementary ESL programs. The 2006 Cycle One program, mandatory in grades 1–2, is oral, listening-first and built on songs, rhymes and stories. It has two competencies: acting on understanding of texts heard (the "cornerstone") and communicating orally. It sets **no reading or writing expectations** ([PFEQ Cycle One, 2006](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)). Its pedagogy translates almost directly into game mechanics. Action songs and predictable stories with refrains come first. Children respond physically before speaking. Pictures carry meaning without falling back on French. Children are taught to make "intelligent guesses" from visuals and cognates. "Quiet activities" such as matching pictures to words are explicitly encouraged. And, in grade 2 only, children make "personalized versions" by swapping their own known words into a song or story template ([PFEQ Cycle One](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)). The Ministry's evaluation framework weights Cycle One **60% comprehension of heard texts and 40% oral communication**. It counts non-verbal reactions as evidence of understanding, and it says strategies get feedback but are not marked ([ESL Evaluation Framework 2011](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/cadres-evaluation/primaire/anglais/PFEQ-cadre-evaluation-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)).

Cycles Two and Three (grades 3–6) switch to a communicative approach with explicit focus on form and add reading and writing. Theme vocabulary becomes a formal target from Cycle Two: family, sports, pastimes, food, animals, clothing, special events, colours, shapes, numbers, the alphabet, prepositions and time. Formal language conventions also appear: adjective before noun, regular and common irregular plurals, articles, and tenses for tasks ([Progression of Learning ESL, 2009](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)). The Ministry's own summary chart puts it bluntly: Cycle One has no expectations for functional language, reading or writing. Cycle Two starts targeted functional language and model-based writing. Cycle Three broadens topics and writes short texts from open models ([Evolution of the ESL Programs, MEES 2016](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/ressources-pedagogiques/anglais-langue-seconde/Evolution-ESL-Programs-English-Second-Language-Elementary.pdf)). These documents were still current on the Ministry's page in late 2025, with no announced revision ([Québec.ca ALS primaire](https://www.quebec.ca/education/prescolaire-primaire-et-secondaire/programmes-formations-evaluation/programme-formation-ecole-quebecoise/primaire/anglais-langue-seconde)).

School time is thin, and that is exactly why the app matters. PIRLS reports about **1 hour of ESL per week** from grade 1 ([PIRLS 2021 Québec](https://pirls2021.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Québec.pdf)). One school service centre's 2025–2026 grid allots 108 minutes per 9-day cycle in Cycle One ([CSS de Portneuf grid](https://cssportneuf.gouv.qc.ca/uploads/documents/bourdons/Repartition-matieres-grille-horaire-du-primaire-2025-2026.pdf)). That is below the 1.5–2 hours a week the Cycle One pilot used when it reported important comprehension gains ([PFEQ Cycle One](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)). Québec's own researchers add a sobering point. School-setting studies "have failed to find long-term advantages for an early start" when instruction is a couple of hours a week ([Lightbown & Spada, Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0261444819000454/type/journal_article)). In a study of about 700 students, concentrated time beat distributed time ([TESL Canada Journal, intensive ESL in Québec](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/view/557)). Muñoz's Barcelona work likewise found that, after similar input, starting age stopped predicting outcomes ([Muñoz 2011, IRAL](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral.2011.006/html)). The program's own critical-period rationale therefore overstates what age alone delivers. A 7-year-old's advantage is realized only through added, meaningful input. Short daily sessions that roughly double her weekly contact hours are the app's most defensible contribution.

The international ladder gives the levels external names. The CEFR Companion Volume defines **Pre-A1** as a band halfway to A1 where the learner "has not yet acquired a generative capacity" and relies on words and formulas ([CEFR Companion Volume 2020](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)). That is a near-perfect description of Cycle One's end-of-cycle child. The Council of Europe also collated age-appropriate can-do descriptors for **learners aged 7–10**. Examples include performing the actions to a song as it is heard and understanding a short illustrated animal story. The collation is explicitly not a ready-made curriculum ([CoE Young Learners 7–10, 2018](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688)). Cambridge's Pre A1 Starters, A1 Movers and A2 Flyers form a no-fail "bridge" from beginner to A2, with cumulative thematic vocabulary lists and shield-based results ([Cambridge YLE Handbook 2024](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)). A publisher estimates roughly **100, 175 and 250 study hours** for the three exams; this is not an official Cambridge figure ([Macmillan YLE teacher's notes](https://www.macmillan.pl/files_catalog/07_egzaminy/yle_tests/YLE_Starters_eTeachers_Notes_sml.pdf)). At about one school hour a week, a Québec child gets roughly 70 hours over Cycle One. Pre-A1 by the end of grade 2 is therefore a realistic school ceiling, and Starters-level reading and spelling sits beyond it. No official alignment between Québec ESL and the CEFR exists. The mapping in the next section is this report's own, and parent reports should present it as the app's indicators, not a certification.

One tension needs a deliberate decision. The Cycle One program wants texts "written for English-speaking children" and a teacher who speaks only English. The repository's rule 5 requires original content, and a solo app has no teacher's gestures and face to carry meaning. The resolution is to write original chants and stories *in the authentic style*: predictable sequences, a recurring refrain, actions tied to words. Instructions and feedback stay in Canadian French at level 1. English classroom routines ("Listen!", "Look!", "Touch the…", "Your turn!") are taught as content, so that the instruction language itself shifts to English level by level, with French as a fallback.

## Six levels climb from 60 heard words to about 850

Vocabulary targets have to be derived, because no official Québec or Cambridge word counts exist. Milton's review reports that effective classrooms yield about **3–4 words per contact hour** and that learners later recall about half of the words presented. It also notes that knowing 500–800 words keeps a learner at A1 ([Milton 2022, Language Learning Journal](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2022.2045680)). A widely repeated rule of thumb puts children's ceiling at about 500 words a year in good conditions ([Pearson GSE young learners report, citing Nation via Cameron](https://www.pearsoncanadaschool.com/content/dam/websites/pearson-canada-school/assets/documents/literacy/gse/developing-gse-vocabulary-for-young-learners.pdf)). School plus about 15 minutes a day in the app gives roughly 100 contact hours a year, or 300–400 words a year. Six levels over about two school years therefore land near **850 receptive words**, inside A1 and touching A2. Productive vocabulary runs at roughly half. Milton also argues that word choice should mix high-frequency words with topical "content" words, because very small lexicons are dominated by concrete nouns ([Milton 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2022.2045680)). That supports picture-friendly nouns first, with verbs and abstract time words later.

The theme lists are an *inventory*, not a teaching order inside a unit. Learning semantically related new words together (all the colours, all the fruits) interferes with learning, while thematic clusters around a scene help ([Frontiers in Psychology 2022, review and experiment](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.997951/full)). Introducing a related word only once the first is largely known reduces that interference ([Nakata & Suzuki 2018, SSLA](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10)). A unit should therefore be a *scene* that draws 6–10 words from several inventories. "At the pond" might teach frog, duck, green, jump, swim and fish. The next colour arrives only after the previous colour is recognized reliably.

The phonics order follows systematic synthetic phonics, which has the strongest reading evidence. The WWC K–3 guide rates sound awareness linked to letters and decoding instruction as **Strong**, and daily connected-text reading as Moderate ([IES/WWC Practice Guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/21)). England's validation criteria ask programs to start with a small high-utility letter set, blend left to right from the start, introduce tricky words gradually, use only decodable practice text, and *not* teach guessing words from pictures ([GOV.UK SSP validation criteria](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/phonics-teaching-materials-core-criteria-and-self-assessment/validation-of-systematic-synthetic-phonics-programmes-supporting-documentation)). The classic first set s, a, t, p was chosen because it builds CVC words immediately ([Letters and Sounds 2007](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7aa7b6e5274a34770e630c/Letters_and_Sounds_-_DFES-00281-2007.pdf)). Phonological awareness also transfers across French and English in Canadian bilingual children ([Comeau et al. 1999, Applied Psycholinguistics record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/longitudinal-study-of-phonological-processing-skills-and-reading-in-bilingual-children/CEFFA121407BB4C7C2F4B355D725DB46)). Oral sound games at levels 1–2 are therefore not wasted on her French reading either. The francophone adaptation in the table is this report's inference, not a tested sequence. English-only codes (short vowels, the pronounced h, th, w, final-e) appear early and are explicitly flagged as "English letters". Reading starts at level 3 and only once the parents confirm French reading is solid. Picture choices appear *after* decoding, never as a guessing cue. Tricky words (I, the, a, to, is, my, you, said, was, are, they, have…) are added two or three at a time, always inside sentences.

Francophone-specific traps deserve their own tags, because they are predictable. In Québec learners, /h/ is both **dropped and inserted**, and the authors place the difficulty in perception and mental representation rather than articulation ([John & Rigoulot 2023, Frontiers in Language Sciences](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/language-sciences/articles/10.3389/flang.2023.1286084/full)). "Th" tends to collapse into nearby French stops, and francophones may not keep *three* and *tree* apart in the mental lexicon even when they can pronounce some "th" words ([Trofimovich 2011](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/lllt.30.09tro)). A Québec study of 50 francophones found these errors **inconsistent rather than systematic** and recommends training perception and production ([Scientifique en chef du Québec](https://www.scientifique-en-chef.gouv.qc.ca/en/research-impact/pronouncing-the-th-and-h-sounds-is-no-easy-task)). Inconsistent errors mean one-off lessons will not work. These contrasts must recur in mixed, spaced review, and a single miss should not be scored as failure. Among grammar traps, *his/her* is the best documented for Québec children. French *son/sa* agrees with the object, English with the owner, and **explicit French–English contrasts sped up acquisition** in Québec ESL learners ([White 2008](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/lllt.23.13whi)). Adjective order ("a car red"), age with *avoir* ("I have seven years"), "the" with general plurals, do-support and third-person -s are standard teacher knowledge with no peer-reviewed source in these notes. They should be treated as hypotheses that the app's tag weights will confirm or dismiss. The IES guide explicitly recommends teaching cognates as a strategy *and* teaching false cognates explicitly ([IES/WWC guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)). True cognates (animal, lion, banana, piano, train) give level-1 quick wins, but each one should be paired with a stress game (AN-i-mal, ba-NA-na), since the meaning transfers but the sound does not. A "tricky cousins" round with false friends (raisin, coin, pain, four, library, sale) waits until level 4.

## Eight game formats turn the receptive ladder into adaptive play

No meta-analysis ranks micro-formats such as picture matching against odd-one-out, so the format set should be chosen to climb the receptive-to-productive ladder. Laufer and Goldstein's study of 435 ESL learners found a stable difficulty hierarchy from passive recognition up to active recall, and **passive recall best predicted classroom performance** ([Laufer & Goldstein 2004](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/testing-vocabulary-knowledge-size-strength-and-computer-adaptiveness/812341984197869569-6763)). One child-specific result shapes the sound games. Greek-speaking 8-year-olds improved on the ship/sheep vowel contrast over **10 sessions** of a computer word game, and children did *better* with one talker than with four ([Giannakopoulou et al. 2017, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/3209)). The games below use original French working titles that fit the garden; each is one engine skill in a new `english` subject.

| Game (skill id) | Levels | What it measures | How difficulty grows | Main tags |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Le potager qui écoute** / listen-and-pick (`en.listen-words`) | 1–6 | Passive recognition by ear: hear a word, tap 1 of 3–4 pictures | 3 → 4 → 6 options; distractors go from unrelated known words, to same-category words, to sound-alikes; word alone → word in a carrier phrase | theme, cognate, distractor type |
| **La jardinière dit** / action commands (`en.commands`) | 1–4 | Understanding instructions, shown by acting (tap the matching action or object; "do the move" moment) | One command → two in sequence → command with a position or colour ("Put the red apple in the basket") | verbs, positions, colour-before-noun |
| **Paires de sons** / sound pairs (`en.sounds`) | 2–6 | Perception of trap contrasts: hear one word of a pair, tap its picture | /h/ vs none (hat/at, hair/air) → th vs t (three/tree, thin/tin) → short vs long (ship/sheep, cap/cape) → final consonants → stress; one voice, then a second voice only after mastery | `en-h`, `en-th`, `en-voyelle`, `en-finale`, `en-accent` |
| **La phrase et l'image** / sentence-picture (`en.listen-sentences`) | 2–6 | Phrase-level listening: hear a sentence, pick the matching scene | Adjective+noun → positions → plurals → there is/are → his/her → negation → past | `en-ordre-adjectif`, `en-pluriel`, `en-his-her`, `en-negation` |
| **Le sentier des histoires** / story path (`en.story`) | 2–6 | Global comprehension, refrain prediction, retelling by ordering 3–5 pictures; in grade-2 style, swap own known words into a template | Longer stories, fewer picture cues, read-along text from level 4, then independent reading at level 5 | story structure, connectors, tenses |

| **Les tuiles de lettres** / spell-it (`en.spell`) | 4–6 | Productive written recall: hear a known word, build it from letter tiles with distractor letters | CVC → digraphs → final-e → vowel teams; distractors move from random letters to the French-habit letters (the letter French would write for that sound) | phonics step, `en-nom-des-lettres`, `en-lettre-muette` |

The current learner model computes the chance of a first-try success as σ(ability − level + 0.85 − Σ tag weights) per skill, and learns tag weights only from her answers (`packages/adaptive-engine/src/learner.ts`). It fits English well, with three adaptations. First, **multiple-choice items need a guessing floor**. With 3 options, a random tap succeeds a third of the time. A 3PL-style form, p = c + (1 − c)·σ(…) with c = 1/options, makes a lucky tap count as weaker evidence and keeps a word from being "mastered" through recognition alone. The BKT literature's conventional guess bound of about 0.3 sits right at that chance level ([Penn learning analytics slides on BKT](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/MOOT/slides/W004V002v6.pdf)). Second, **the unit of knowledge is word × modality**. A word heard, read and spelled is three different strengths in the Laufer–Goldstein sense. So the engine's skills should be the modalities in the table, and a per-word record (derivable from the attempt history, like the existing model, so it needs no new table) should unlock the harder modality only after the easier one succeeds on two separate days. The existing "won on another day" rule (`CONQUER_STREAK`, `almostTags`) already encodes that idea. Third, **the explore rule needs gates**. Today `planRound` offers one level above the official one when it predicts at least 55% success. For listening skills that is a feature. For `en.read-words`, `en.spell` and `en.build-sentence` it must be capped by a parent-area switch, so that the model cannot open English decoding while French reading is still being consolidated.

The word schedule should start as a simple, auditable ladder: two or three retrievals within the session separated by other items, then 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days, demoting one step on an error rather than to the start. This follows the "spaced, not shaped" evidence above. Duolingo's half-life regression, p = 2^(−Δ/h), cut recall-prediction error by **more than 45%** against baselines and lifted daily engagement by 12% ([Settles & Meeder 2016, ACL](https://preview.aclanthology.org/landing_page/P16-1174)). But it was fitted on adult and teen data, and it is worth adopting only once the app has months of her English history. Units should hold 6–10 words with at most 3–5 new words per session. Session size is a practical inference with no peer-reviewed source.

Errors are tagged in two layers. The first layer is the engine's existing kind of tag: *what the item requires*, read from the item itself (`en-th` for a three/tree item, `en-p5` for a word with "sh"). These drive the tag weights and the "reinforce" rounds. The second layer is new: **diagnostic distractors**. Every wrong option is built to stand for one hypothesis (the French-habit spelling, the /h/-less twin, the same-category neighbour, the noun-before-adjective order). The server stores which option she chose in the round snapshot. The model still learns from first-try success, but the parent report can then say *what* she confuses ("choisit *tree* quand elle entend *three*"), and the next minimal-pair round can target that exact confusion. Tag keys follow the repository's style of French labels in `packages/curriculum/src/tags.ts`, for example `en-h` → « le son h anglais », `en-th` → « le son th », `en-faux-ami` → « faux amis ». Following the Québec framework, strategy use (replaying audio, asking for the slow version) is logged as feedback for the parent and never counted against her ([ESL Evaluation Framework 2011](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/cadres-evaluation/primaire/anglais/PFEQ-cadre-evaluation-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)).

The server already allows up to three attempts per round, which maps cleanly onto a prompt-first ladder. After a first miss, the app gives a sensory prompt: it replays the target slowly, or for sound pairs plays both words in contrast, with no failure sound (the current audio policy). After a second miss, it narrows to two options or highlights the key letter or sound. After a third, it models the answer with audio and the picture, then re-asks the item after two or three others before the session ends. Only the first try feeds the model. Any explanation is a few concrete French words ("on entend le *h* : hat"). The one exception is from level 4 on, where a short French–English contrast is justified for his/her by White's results with Québec learners. The distractor difficulty moves up when recent accuracy on a tag exceeds about 85% and down below about 65%. This keeps her near the 70% "challenge zone" the engine already targets. These thresholds are inferences, not sourced constants.

## One checked voice matters more than many voices

Audio is the single point where the English games can fail silently. Synthetic speech is less intelligible for children than for adults ([AJSLP 2010 review](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2010/09-0024))) and poses a **greater challenge for bilingual children** than for monolingual ones ([Axmear et al. 2005, LSHSS](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/0161-1461(2005/024))). Both studies used older synthesizers. Even recently, synthetic speech was the least intelligible and most effortful condition for native and non-native adult listeners ([Simantiraki, Wagner & Cooke 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10568627/)). Quality also varied widely across TTS engines when rated by L2 learners ([research-publishing.net chapter](https://research-publishing.net/publication/chapters/978-2-490057-22-1/817.pdf)). No study tests modern neural TTS with child L2 listeners, so the right stance is to use TTS but verify it.

The requirements follow from that. Use **one** North American voice for all core content: en-CA if a good one is installed, otherwise en-US. Add a second voice only at a later "other speakers" stage, given the single-talker advantage for children. Speak at about 0.85–0.9 of normal rate without distorting vowels. Present each word alone and in a carrier phrase ("a frog", "I see a frog"). Make replay a large button, with a slower replay on the second request. Today's `apps/game/src/audio.ts` deliberately picks only a French voice (preferring Edge's fr-CA voice) and stays silent without one. English needs a separate voice picker, and listening rounds must refuse to start, with a parent-area warning, when no English voice is available. A listening item without sound would teach nothing and would corrupt the model with random taps. Browser voices differ between machines and updates, and the known TTS failure points are homographs, wrong stress, clipped function words and minimal pairs rendered too similarly. So the core vocabulary and **every minimal pair should be pre-generated or recorded once, listened to by an adult, and shipped as files** with a "verified" flag that a content-quality test enforces.

Speech recognition stays out of the first versions. Modern models reach only "acceptable" accuracy on non-native child speech and still need child-specific fine-tuning ([SLATE 2023, arXiv 2306.16710](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.16710)). Rule 4 also forbids sending the child's answers to external APIs, which leaves only on-device recognition, and that would be unreliable for a 7-year-old. Production is practised through "say it with me" moments with no recording, gesture copying, letter tiles and word tiles. Recognition should never be used to mark an answer wrong. Rule 9 means browser tests run headless with speech disabled, so they should check the audio event log and that every English item has an audio key, not listen for sound.

## Ship the listening games first and reading last

Motivation design matters for this age. Among apps used by 160 children aged 3–5, manipulative patterns such as parasocial pressure, fabricated time pressure and lures were near-universal: only about **one in five apps had none** ([Radesky et al. 2022, via Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use)). The English games should reuse the app's existing approach. Progress is cumulative and cannot be lost (plants that grow with mastered words and never wither), with no resetting streaks, timers, sad characters or "one more" prompts. Each session ends naturally, and praise names the specific skill ("tu as entendu la différence entre *ship* et *sheep*"). For parents, the Council of Europe's 7–10 descriptors give the right vocabulary ([CoE Young Learners 7–10](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688)). The report should show original French can-do lines in that spirit, labelled « repères inspirés du CECR ». It should add per-theme counts by modality, the share of words still correct after 7 or more days (the honest learning measure), and one or two concrete things to work on at home. Cambridge's no-fail shields are a good model for showing per-skill progress without percentages ([Cambridge YLE Handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "teaching English early" means for this child. The early start is not itself the advantage. The advantage comes from the added hours, the ear for English prosody, and a positive first experience that Québec's own researchers say the school's single weekly hour cannot deliver alone. That makes the app's job unusually clear. It should be a high-frequency, low-pressure listening companion that teaches a few words deeply and brings them back across days. Reading and spelling should be treated as a later, gated chapter rather than the measure of success. The second shift is in what the learner model should learn. For French and math, tags describe what an exercise requires. For a francophone learning English, the most valuable signal is *which wrong answer* she picks, because the confusions (three/tree, hair/air, "a car red") are predictable and the evidence says they are inconsistent rather than fixed. They need recurring, targeted perception practice, not a one-time correction.

The largest uncertainties are empirical, not technical. No study tests these phonics orders, encounter counts, TTS voices or feedback ladders with 7-year-old francophones using an app. Several francophone traps rest on adult data or teacher lore. The level word targets are extrapolated from older learners. The app is in a position to answer these questions for itself. If English words heard today are still recognized after a week or more, if the sound-pair tag weights fall over successive weeks, and if modality unlocks happen on separate days, the design is working. If they do not, the data will show where to slow down. That retention evidence, more than any external level label, is what should decide when to open the next level.
