# Retrieving, spacing and explaining are worth more than confetti

The learning techniques with the most solid evidence for children aged 7 to 10 are **retrieval practice** (answering from memory, without the model in sight) and **spaced practice** over several days. Close behind, with moderate evidence, come **interleaving** of problem types, **self-explanation**, **worked examples** faded gradually and **explanatory feedback**, which in a computer environment yields about **0.49 SD versus 0.05 for a simple "right/wrong"**. In mathematics, the IES/WWC guides give **strong** evidence to the number line, to concrete representations linked to the symbol, to explicit teaching of word problems and to fact fluency. In reading, the strong evidence is in teaching comprehension strategies: questions, main idea and monitoring. In French, the big obstacle in writing is silent grammatical spelling (-s, -e, -nt), which improves with explicit rules and dictation with justification. Generic brain training does not transfer to grades, so executive functions are better exercised inside school tasks and in games of increasing difficulty. Tangible, expected rewards, given for playing or finishing, reduce intrinsic motivation (d ≈ −0.40 for those given for engagement). Informative feedback and process praise, by contrast, sustain it. Melina's app already gets a lot right: graduated hints, spaced review, a model that learns strengths and weaknesses and a report with trends. The three most valuable corrections are these: confirm mastery with a correct answer in a review done days later; compute the report's trends from the estimated skill, not from the raw percentage, which drops every time the engine raises the level; and turn each round's celebration into short feedback that names the strategy, saving the big celebration for milestones of real mastery.

## Three evidence labels and what was left out

Every claim in this report carries one of three labels. **Strong** marks what an IES/WWC guide classifies as "strong", what Dunlosky et al. (2013) call "high utility" with support from classroom meta-analyses, or what several large meta-analyses confirm together. **Moderate** marks what the WWC classifies as "moderate", what comes from a single meta-analysis, what was studied mainly with older students, what only the team that created the method evaluated, or numbers known only from secondary sources. **Inference** marks the translation into design for the app: extrapolations consistent with the evidence, but that nobody has tested in an app for children aged 7 to 10. The Quebec curriculum (PFEQ, Progression des apprentissages, updated program) appears as **official curriculum**: it prescribes what to teach and is not evidence of effectiveness.

There are limits that need to stay visible. The EEF website refused automated reading (HTTP 403). For that reason, several EEF numbers come from search snippets, from older editions of the Toolkit or from secondary summaries, and each one is flagged in the text. **No OECD document was consulted**: the question mentions the OECD, but there is nothing from it in the notes, and this report attributes no conclusion to the OECD. The full texts of Deci et al. (1999), Hattie & Timperley (2007), Sailer & Homner (2020) and the WWC guides were not read page by page: the findings come from official abstracts and from the guides' pages. In the Quebec curriculum, the PDF conversion lost the symbols that indicate the grade of each content item. For that reason, assigning content to 2e or 3e année is reliable only when the text states it (end-of-cycle expectations, footnotes). In the other cases it is inferred.

## Retrieval and spacing lead; interleaving and explaining come close behind

The review by Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated ten study techniques. Only two received "high utility": **practice testing** (retrieval) and **distributed practice** (spaced). Both benefited learners of different ages and levels across many types of task ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)). Three techniques were rated "moderate": elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving. In part, the moderate rating reflects the scarcity of research on them. Rereading, highlighting, summarizing and the keyword mnemonic were rated "low utility" ([EurekAlert/APS](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/508403)). The 2007 IES guide reaches similar conclusions. It gives **strong** evidence to using quizzes to re-expose the student to the content and to asking deep explanatory questions ("why?", "how?"). It gives **moderate** evidence to spacing learning, to alternating worked examples with exercises, to combining image and text and to linking concrete and abstract representations ([WWC Practice Guide 1](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1)).

The weak point of this base is age. In the classroom, retrieval yields about **g = 0.50** (Yang et al. 2021, 49 effect sizes, n = 5,374), and feedback increases the gain ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10950551)). But in a review of 50 experiments in schools, **only 5 were in primary school** ([WGU](https://guidance.wgu.edu/patterns/resources/retrieval-practice-consistently-benefits-student-learning-a-systematic-review-of-50-experiments-in-schools-and-classrooms)). Direct studies with young children are few, but they point in the same direction. With children aged 5 to 7, spaced lessons produced better generalization of science concepts than massed lessons ([Vlach & Sandhofer 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3399982)). With Grade 4 students, mixing four types of math problem **lowered performance during practice, but doubled the score on a test the next day** ([Taylor & Rohrer 2010](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1760/)). The often-cited figure of "72% versus 38%" comes from another study, with Grade 7 students, and should not be attributed to 9-year-olds ([RetrievalPractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2017/interleaving)).

The Taylor & Rohrer finding matters more for the app than any effect size: **performance within the session is misleading**. Massed and blocked practice looks better in the moment and loses afterwards. The same pattern appears with 2-year-olds, where massed presentation won on the immediate test and lost after 15 minutes ([Vlach, Ankowski & Sandhofer 2012](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Ankowski_Sandhofer-2012.pdf)). From this comes a design rule (inference, but well anchored): the app should **decide mastery and advancement by correct answers in a delayed review**, not by a streak of correct answers in the same session.

Self-explanation has its own evidence in the right age range. Rittle-Johnson (2006) tested it with children aged 8 to 11 on mathematical equivalence ([PDF](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-sub/wp-content/uploads/sites/280/2023/08/04183033/ATME_Rittle-Johnson_2006.pdf)). The 2017 meta-analysis found **small to moderate** gains in procedural and conceptual knowledge and in transfer, but also cases where asking for an explanation **did not help or got in the way** ([ZDM 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11858-017-0834-z)). In other words, it is a seasoning to use sparingly, not something to ask for on every item. Worked examples followed by practice have a moderate label in the IES guide, but the evidence comes almost entirely from older students.

Feedback is the best-measured lever in digital environments. The meta-analysis by Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015), with 40 studies in computer-based environments, found **0.49 for elaborated feedback** (with explanation), **0.32 for showing the correct answer** and **0.05 for only saying right/wrong**. Effects were larger in mathematics and smaller with delayed feedback ([ACER](https://people.acer.org/en/publications/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-s/)). The review by Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), with 435 studies, found d = 0.48 overall. The effect grows with the amount of information in the feedback, and **praise, punishment and rewards have a low effect** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456)). A 2022 network meta-analysis confirms that elaborated feedback is the most effective type, both for recall and for transfer ([IPN](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/effects-of-computer-based-feedback-on-lower-and-higher-order-learning-outcomes)).

In the EEF Toolkit, the largest estimate is for **metacognition and self-regulation: +8 months** of progress per year (May 2025 revision; previously +7) ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/updated-eef-guide-to-metacognition-and-self-regulation)). **Mastery learning** appears with +5 months in earlier editions of the Toolkit ([Sutton Trust–EEF 2014](https://thehub-beta.walthamforest.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2019-07/sutton_trust_toolkit.pdf)). **Individualized instruction** appears with +4 months, with limited evidence, and the EEF says something every app creator should read: **individualization with technology is as effective as without technology**, not more ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/evidence-summaries/teaching-learning-toolkit/individualised-instruction/)). The active ingredient is the method, not the screen. These months come from teacher-led programs, with students aged 4 to 16, and should not be promised for an app.

On calibrating difficulty, the famous "85% rule" (Wilson et al. 2019) is a theoretical result about **machine learning algorithms on binary tasks**, and the author himself advises against applying it to school grades ([Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/); [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0v22s4g4/qt0v22s4g4.pdf?t=sdxgqm)). No source found establishes an ideal success rate for children aged 7 to 10 in educational software. As an engineering heuristic (inference), the notes suggest about 75 to 90% first-try success in reviews and 60 to 75% on new or interleaved items. The ideal spacing intervals for this age were not found either.

## The right exercises: number line, structured problems, inference and agreement

### Mathematics and logical reasoning

The WWC guide for primary mathematics (2021) has six recommendations, and **all have strong evidence**: systematic instruction; precise mathematical language; well-chosen concrete and semi-concrete representations; use of the **number line**; deliberate teaching of **word problems**; and regular **timed activities** to build fluency ([WWC Practice Guide 26](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/26)). The guide was written for struggling students in intervention, which weighs especially on the timer recommendation. The problem-solving guide for Grades 4 to 8 adds **strong** evidence for helping the student **monitor and reflect** on their own process and for teaching **visual representations**, and **moderate** evidence for exposing multiple strategies ([WWC Practice Guide 16](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/16)). The EEF guide for Key Stages 2 and 3 converges: assess in order to start from what the child knows, use manipulatives while explicitly linking them to the mathematical idea so they do not become a crutch, teach problem-solving strategies and develop independence and motivation. The exact wording comes from summaries, because the official page refused the reading ([Third Space Learning](https://thirdspacelearning.com/blog/2017-eef-mathematics-report-close-the-attainment-gap-primary)). The most elegant proof of the power of the number line comes from Siegler & Ramani. A numbered **linear** board game, played in four 15-minute sessions, eliminated differences in numerical estimation between low-income children and their peers. Circular boards or boards with colors instead of numbers did not work. The 2009 study meets WWC standards without reservations ([WWC study 73675](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/study/73675)), but it was done with preschoolers. For ages 7 to 10, the equivalent is estimating positions on a 0 to 100 and a 0 to 1000 line.

The Quebec curriculum asks for exactly these ingredients. The PFEQ organizes mathematics into three competencies: **résoudre une situation-problème, raisonner, communiquer** ([PFEQ](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf)). At the end of the 1er cycle (2e année), the child solves problem situations **with complete data, in one or two steps**, creates personal processes for addition and subtraction and communicates with at least one mode of representation. At the end of the 2e cycle, they deal with **implicit data**, anticipate and validate the result and use more than one mode of representation. The Progression des apprentissages lists for the 1er cycle natural numbers **up to 1000**, the "**droite numérique**", the meaning of operations as **ajout, retrait, réunion and comparaison**, the **missing term** (a + □ = c), the memorized repertoire from 0+0 to 10+10 (which, according to the document itself, "asks for more than memorizing tables"), sequences of at least three terms, even and odd, everyday fractions (demi, tiers, quart), bar graphs and pictographs and qualitative probability (**certain, possible, impossible**) ([Progression, copy read](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf); [official version](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)). One design conclusion stands out: the PFEQ extends the curriculum through **complexity of reasoning** (superfluous or missing data, validation, justification, several representations), not through bigger numbers. It is the natural path to "go further" with Melina without getting ahead of school (inference).

For logical reasoning, the best evidence that training produces gains beyond the task itself is in two families. The first is **inductive reasoning**: series, classification, analogies, finding the odd one out. The meta-analysis by Klauer & Phye (2008), with 74 experiments with children, found **greater transfer to academic learning than to intelligence tests** ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF03172941)). An extended analysis reports d = 0.68 on academic learning, but the authorship was not confirmed ([Hogrefe](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123)). The second is **spatial reasoning**: rotation, visualization, assembly. Uttal et al. (2013), with 217 studies, found g = 0.47, with **durable** gains and transfer to other spatial tasks ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/07/120725120634.htm)). Both families are already in the PFEQ: patterns, classifying numbers by properties, friezes by reflection, nets of solids, location on a grid. The label is moderate, since these are meta-analyses with heterogeneous samples.

### Executive functions without the promise of "brain training"

The Harvard Center on the Developing Child activities guide recommends, for ages 7 to 12, card games that require keeping track of what has already been played, turn-based strategy games, physical games with rules, music and rhythm, crosswords, sudoku and spatial puzzles, always with **progressively increasing difficulty** ([Harvard CDC](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/handouts-tools/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills/); [PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)). The guide is a practical collection, with no effect sizes per activity. It mentions Cogmed and Lumosity, and that mention needs to be read in light of the meta-analyses. Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016) analyzed 145 comparisons and found no convincing evidence that working memory training improves reading, **arithmetic** or reasoning when compared with an active control ([PMC4968033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033)). In typically developing children, the gain close to the trained task is ḡ = 0.46, versus only 0.12 for far transfer ([PMC5724589](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724589)). Chess and music show effects that disappear in the best-controlled studies ([Sala & Gobet 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417712760)). Diamond & Lee (2011) found the common ingredients of the programs that work: **repeated practice and increasing demand**, with the largest gain for those who start out weakest ([PMC3159917](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3159917/)). The conclusion for the app (inference): executive function games are worthwhile as fun that exercises memory, flexibility and control, but the place with the highest return is **inside mathematics and reading**. Examples: holding the data of a problem in mind, switching strategy, resisting the "obvious" operation in a comparison problem.

### Reading, comprehension and analysis

The three WWC reading guides converge. The K–3 comprehension guide (2010) gives **strong** evidence to teaching comprehension strategies and **moderate** evidence to teaching text structure and to a motivating context. Discussions and choice of texts have minimal evidence ([WWC Practice Guide 14](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/14)). The K–3 foundational skills guide (2016) gives **strong** evidence to phonological awareness and to decoding/encoding and **moderate** evidence to daily reading of connected text ([WWC Practice Guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/21)). The Grades 4 to 9 intervention guide (2022) gives **strong** evidence to a package of comprehension practices: building vocabulary and world knowledge, asking and answering questions about the text, a routine for extracting the **central idea** ("gist") and **monitoring comprehension** while reading ([WWC Practice Guide 29](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/29)). The EEF estimates comprehension strategies at **+6 months in primary school**, with high evidence (141 studies) ([EEF, technical appendix](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/reading-comprehension-strategies/technical-appendix)). The EEF KS2 literacy report (ages 7 to 11) teaches these strategies through **modeling followed by supported practice**, with the support faded gradually ([EEF KS2](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/literacy-ks2)).

As for question types, teaching inference works. Rice & Wijekumar (2024) found g = 0.495 on inference skills and on general comprehension, with no significant difference by grade ([record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_3065453949)). According to a secondary summary, the meta-analysis by Elleman (2017) shows a larger effect on inferential comprehension (d ≈ 0.68) than on literal comprehension (0.28) ([Shanahan, via summary](https://www.philippinesbasiceducation.us/2017/04/how-should-we-teach-making-inferences.html)). Teaching the child to **formulate their own questions** about the text also helps: median effect of 0.36 on standardized tests, a figure from an aggregator ([Formapex](https://formapex.com/barak-rosenshine/1209-teaching-students-to-generate-questions-a-review-of-the-intervention-studies)). Writing about what one has read improves comprehension by about 0.40 SD ([Writing to Read](https://core-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/documents/asset/uploaded_file/776383/Synopsis_Writing_to_Read.pdf)). Teaching text structure has large effects in K–12 meta-analyses (0.95; [ASHA](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/61fb58ef-54ff-4a0f-a6f3-ae318f6ea0d5)), but in Grades 4 to 6 the effects were smaller and **did not hold on delayed tests** ([Bogaerds-Hazenberg 2020](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2545476694)). Hence the moderate label.

The Quebec curriculum gives the exact progression. At the end of the 1er cycle, the updated program expects the child to **relève des informations explicites regroupées**, **infère quelques éléments implicites** (especially linking pronouns and substitutes to their referent), build the overall meaning **en effectuant un rappel** and detect and fix their own **bris de compréhension**. At the end of the 2e cycle, they regroup **scattered** information, infer by combining clues and **cause and effect** and **summarize by distinguishing the essential from the secondary** ([Programme actualisé, p. 34](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)). The Progression currently in force already asks for justified interpretation **with excerpts from the text as proof** ([Progression, p. 71–76](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)). The calendar weighs on planning. The updated program, with three competencies (oral, reading, writing), becomes **mandatory in 2027-2028** ([Québec.ca](https://www.quebec.ca/nouvelles/actualites/details/nouveau-programme-de-francais-la-ministre-lebel-accorde-une-annee-de-plus-pour-sy-preparer-68442)), exactly when Melina will be in 3e année.

### Spelling in French: the problem is what you cannot hear

The synthesis by the Conseil scientifique de l'éducation nationale (CSEN, France, 2025) changes the priority of spelling practice. In French, **writing is much harder than reading**, because many morphological markers are not pronounced ("les mouches volent"). Most errors are in **grammatical spelling**, not lexical spelling. The order of acquisition is stable: the -s of the noun, then the -s of the adjective, and last the verbal -nt. Gender is harder than number. From CP to CE2, number markers are acquired **earlier and faster when the rules are taught explicitly and practiced** than through simple exposure (Fayol, Grimaud & Jacquier 2013). The past participle (-é/-ée/-és/-ées) stays at around 50% accuracy at ages 7 and 8. About 70% of nouns end in a silent letter, and the **word family** helps find it (candidat → candidate) ([CSEN 2025](https://www.csen.education.gouv.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8eb4a522-f5fe-4524-b3f3-a52fb82d9907-CSENV2Syntheseorthographe2025web.pdf)). In English, teaching spelling formally yields ES ≈ 0.54 versus no instruction and transfers to word reading (0.44). These numbers come from a secondary summary ([Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/does-spelling-instruction-make-students-better-spellers-readers-and)). In Quebec, the **dictée 0 faute** and the **phrase dictée du jour** were applied in 41 classes, from Grade 3 of primary to Secondary 3: the student justifies each agreement using syntactic manipulations. The result was positive for grammatical spelling, especially for weaker students ([FRQ, Nadeau report 2014](https://frq.gouv.qc.ca/app/uploads/2021/06/pt_nadeaum_rapport-2014_dictee-impact-orthographique.pdf)). The evidence comes from the same team that created the method, without strong independent replication, hence the moderate label.

The curriculum confirms the focus. At the end of the 1er cycle, the child makes the agreement **determiner + noun in simple cases**, writes memorized verbs in the present tense and gets **most of the words on the list** right ([Programme actualisé, p. 37](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)). The Progression in force says that **"dès la 2e année"** the child begins to make the adjective agree with the noun in simple cases. The Liste orthographique prescribes **300 words for 2e année** (260 prescribed and 40 chosen). The chosen words **can come from the child's interests**. The program defines "knowing how to write a word" as knowing its meaning, **all its forms** (ami → amie, amis) and its class ([Annexe 5](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)).

## Rewards expected for playing erode the will to learn

The meta-analysis by Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), with 128 experiments, is the most solid basis on rewards. **Tangible and expected** rewards reduce free-choice intrinsic motivation. The effect is **d = −0.40 when given for engaging**, −0.36 for completing and −0.28 for performance, and the authors report that the harm is greater in children ([PubMed 10589297](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)). The numbers come from the article's abstract. A 2023 meta-analysis reproduces similar values ([Lehtivuori, Turku](https://www.utupub.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/173853/Annales%20E%2099%20Lehtivuori%20DISS.pdf?sequence=1)). Since 2001, even the critics agree that **rewards for engagement** reduce intrinsic motivation ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)). Positive, informative feedback, on the contrary, tends to increase it, because it confirms competence ([Henderlong & Lepper 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12206194/)). But praise can also do harm. Mastery praise increased motivation; praise based on **social comparison** reduced it when the child was unsure how they would do next ([Corpus & Lepper 2007](https://www.reed.edu/psychology/motivation/assets/downloads/Corpus_Lepper_2007.pdf)).

The type of praise matters more than the amount. In the six studies by Mueller & Dweck (1998), with 412 Grade 5 students, children praised for **intelligence** chose easier problems and coped worse with failure. Those praised for **effort** preferred challenges and showed more enjoyment and persistence ([Columbia PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)). In an observational longitudinal study, the **proportion** of process praise that parents gave between ages 1 and 3 predicted, at ages 7 and 8, a preference for challenges and the belief that intelligence can be developed. The **total amount** of praise predicted nothing ([Gunderson et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123)). Hattie & Timperley distinguish four levels of feedback: task, process, self-regulation and self (the person). Feedback directed at the person ("bonne fille") is the least effective ([AITSL](https://www.aitsl.edu.au/docs/default-source/research-evidence/spotlight/feedback.pdf)). It does not follow that "growth mindset lessons" are worthwhile. Sisk et al. (2018) found very small average effects, about d = 0.08 ([MSU Today](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2018/05/study-finds-popular-growth-mindset-educational-interventions-arent-very-effective)). The large EEF trial, with **101 schools and 5,018 Year 6 students**, found no gain at all in reading, spelling or mathematics ([EEF Changing Mindsets](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/changing-mindset-2015)). Process praise, built into feedback, is enough.

Gamification, on average, helps a little. Sailer & Homner (2020) found small positive effects. The cognitive effect was the most stable, and **narrative** and **collaboration combined with competition** worked as favorable moderators ([Augsburg](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)). Bai, Hew & Huang (2020) found g ≈ 0.50 on performance. Students liked **feedback on their own performance** and cited **anxiety and jealousy** as the downside, associated with scoreboards and rankings ([Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full)). The real risk lies in manipulative patterns. In apps used by children aged 3 to 5, **only 20% had none**. The patterns found were characters that pressure the child to continue, fabricated countdowns, lures to play more and navigation restrictions ([Michigan Medicine, Radesky 2022](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use)). The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) criticizes "engagement-based design" that steals sleep, play and family time. It asks for content that puts the child first and **shared use with parents**, and stresses that this shared use is not policing ([HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Helping-Kids-Thrive-in-a-Digital-World-AAP-Policy-Explained.aspx)). Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015) define the truly educational app by four pillars: learning that is **active, engaged (without distractions), meaningful and socially interactive**, within a learning goal ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)). UNESCO's 2023 GEM report points out that good, impartial evidence on educational technology is scarce and that technology should complement in-person teaching, not replace it ([INEE](https://inee.org/resources/2023-gem-report-technology-education-tool-whose-terms)).

For the app, this gives a simple rule (inference anchored in cognitive evaluation theory): **every reward should carry information about competence, and none should work as payment for playing**. A flower that grows because a skill was confirmed in a review says "you know more". A star for participation, accumulated as currency, says "you were paid to be here". Today each correct answer triggers a trumpet "ta-da", sparkles, applause, a spoken «Bravo!» and confetti. The end of the game brings about 4 seconds of audience and 260 pieces of confetti. This is verbal and symbolic feedback, not a tangible prize, and the evidence does not condemn it directly. Even so, there are two problems. The first is that the celebration is **the same for everything**, so it conveys almost no information. The second is that long animations between rounds compete with the goal, which violates Hirsh-Pasek's "engaged" pillar. The better version is short and specific. Examples: « Tu as compté par bonds de 10, bonne stratégie! » or « Tu as relu avant de valider, comme une pro! ». The big celebration is kept for the moments that mean something: a level confirmed days later, a weakness that became a strength, a completed mission. After a mistake, the current path is already aligned with the evidence: a gentle message, a visual hint and, at the end, the answer with an explanation. What remains is to avoid any "failure" sound and never show the child a count of errors. Autonomy (choosing the game, the order or "un niveau un peu plus difficile?") and the garden's original narrative sustain motivation without paying for it.

## A good report compares the child with herself, by skill

Direct evidence on app reports for parents is scarce. No controlled study compares weekly and monthly reports. The best indirect sources all point in the same direction. The EEF guide on working with parents recommends **practical suggestions for learning at home**, communication that neither blames nor stigmatizes, support for **routines** rather than direct help with homework and helping the child **set goals and manage effort** ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/supporting-parents); [PDF](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk//32623/1/EEF_Parental_Engagement_Guidance_Report.pdf)). The EEF itself admits that the evidence on parental engagement is weaker than in other areas, with about +3 months estimated. When 115 parents received both formats, they **overwhelmingly preferred the standards-based report card** to the traditional one ([Swan, Guskey & Jung 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11092-014-9191-4)). But labels such as "Mastery/Proficiency" confused parents in focus groups ([Guskey 2004](https://core.ac.uk/download/232599797.pdf)), and Guskey argues for **keeping the same format** over time ([Corwin](https://corwin-connect.com/?p=16887)). Add to this the harm of social-comparison praise, and the conclusion is clear: the report should be **by skill**, in **plain language**, with **trends relative to herself** and with **one or two concrete actions** for home. It should never include averages, percentiles or other children.

The app's current report already has a good structure. It has indicators with change relative to the previous period, day-by-day bars, a table by competency (first-try success, previous period, level, trend) and lists of « Points forts », « À renforcer », « En amélioration », « En baisse » and « À surveiller ». It also warns that it requires at least 3 attempts and that short periods vary. The problem is **what it measures**, and this is where the evidence about in-session performance comes back in force. The "taux de réussite du premier coup" mixes review, reinforcement and exploration rounds. When the engine raises the level or explores one level above, as it was designed to do, the percentage drops. The report then shows « En baisse » precisely when Melina is advancing. For the same reason, the « À surveiller (entre 50 % et 70 %) » band coincides with the **challenge zone that the model itself aims for** (about 70% predicted probability). The report alarms the parents when the app is working as it should (inference, but direct from the engine design described in `docs/adaptive-engine.md`).

The fix makes use of an asset the app already has. The per-competency logistic model estimates a **difficulty-adjusted skill**, and it is the change in that skill, not in the raw percentage, that should define the five requested categories. The definitions below are design inferences. **Strengths** are the skills with first-try success in **reviews done at least one day later**, or tags with a clearly favorable weight. **Weaknesses** are tags with an unfavorable weight sustained over several answers. **To improve** is the next skill in the curriculum or the weakness with the greatest impact, always described as a "next step". **Improving** is an estimated skill that has risen consistently, or a weak tag that is no longer weak. **Declining** is reserved for a drop in success **in delayed reviews, at the same level**, which is the real sign of forgetting. A drop in percentage caused by a level increase is not a decline and should not appear as one. The mastery state should come in plain language, such as « réussit seule la plupart du temps », « réussit avec un indice », « en découverte », and not as abstract labels. Usage time and practice days remain as **context**, without green and red arrows that turn quantity into a goal.

As for frequency, since there is no evidence comparing weekly and monthly, the reasonable split is functional (inference). The **weekly** report is short: three to five lines, one strength with a concrete example of a strategy, a "next step", **one home activity** (« Comptez les marches par bonds de 2 ») and **one question to talk about with her** (« Qu'est-ce qui était facile? difficile? »). The **monthly** report is more complete: trend of the estimated skill over several weeks, mapping to the PFEQ terms the school uses, grouped error patterns (« confond dizaines et unités », « oublie le -s après les ») and **one small goal** chosen with her for the month. In both, the EEF recommendation and the research on rewards converge on an explicit warning to parents: **the report is not a basis for reward or punishment**, because that would create exactly the performance-contingent reward that Deci's meta-analysis associates with loss of motivation.

### Avoid

Avoid **shops, coins or exchangeable items announced in return for playing or finishing**. The cosmetic garden collection planned in the roadmap should grow through confirmed mastery or through unannounced surprise, never through stars spent as currency ([Deci 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)). Avoid **daily streaks that "break"**, countdowns, a character that complains when she leaves, prize draws and surprise boxes with variable rarity, and invitations to "just one more" ([Radesky 2022](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use)). If there is a count of days, it should only accumulate (« tu as jardiné 12 jours ce mois-ci »), and the session should have a natural end (« le jardin se repose »). Avoid **scoreboards, averages and comparison with other children**, both on her screen and in the report ([Corpus & Lepper 2007](https://www.reed.edu/psychology/motivation/assets/downloads/Corpus_Lepper_2007.pdf)). Avoid **praise for intelligence** (« Tu es trop intelligente! ») and **growth mindset lessons** ([EEF Changing Mindsets](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/changing-mindset-2015)). Avoid selling or designing any part as **"brain training"** ([Melby-Lervåg 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033)). Avoid long single-type rounds as the main mode, "reread the rule" screens in place of practice and **right/wrong-only** feedback ([Van der Kleij 2015](https://people.acer.org/en/publications/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-s/)). Avoid introducing content early that research shows to be unstable at this age, such as the past participle (about 50% accuracy at ages 7–8), or that the school has not yet covered, such as -al → -aux, already removed ([CSEN 2025](https://www.csen.education.gouv.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8eb4a522-f5fe-4524-b3f3-a52fb82d9907-CSENV2Syntheseorthographe2025web.pdf)). Finally, none of these improvements requires sending the child's data off the computer. All of them fit in the local engine and respect the project's privacy rule.

## Conclusion

The app's main risk is not having too little gamification, but **measuring the wrong thing**. The engine's pedagogically correct decisions (exploring one level above, reinforcing weaknesses, and, soon, interleaving) make in-session success drop. Whoever judges by that number sees decline where there is learning. This applies to the engine, which advances by streaks within the same session, to the report, which marks « En baisse » when the level rises, and to the child herself, who may read the same-for-everything celebration as a sign that everything is worth the same. The app has a rare advantage for fixing this: a difficulty-adjusted skill model and a complete history of spaced reviews. It only needs to use them as the source of truth. The measure of success becomes "she remembers days later, without help", not "she got it right just now".

The second conclusion is that the intrinsic motivation of a 7-year-old feeds on **visible competence and autonomy**, not on the volume of rewards. The evidence allows celebrating, as long as the celebration says something true: "you now know this". The biggest uncertainties remain open. Nobody has established the ideal success rate, the review intervals or the session length for this age, nor how parents read progress charts, and the OECD was not consulted. For that reason, the parameters of the engine and the report should be treated as hypotheses, revised with Melina's real usage data and checked with her teacher, who sees what no algorithm sees.
