# Evidence-based methods for teaching English (L2) to francophone children aged 6–10, and the game mechanics they imply

Scope note for the report writer: the target learner is a 7-year-old francophone girl in Québec (2e année). In most of Québec, English at this age is a *foreign-language-like* setting (a few hours a week, little ambient English). Much of the strongest US evidence (IES/WWC) concerns "English learners" who are *immersed* in English schooling, so it transfers only partly. Evidence strength labels used below:
- **[STRONG]** = meta-analysis or official practice guide rating "strong", consistent across studies.
- **[MODERATE]** = several controlled studies or a meta-analysis with important moderators / caveats.
- **[WEAK]** = single small studies, correlational data, or expert consensus.
- **[UNVERIFIED]** = from the researcher's background knowledge of the literature; the citation is real to the best of my knowledge, but I could not open the paper in this session. Treat as needing confirmation before quoting numbers.

Fetch limits this session: the EEF website (Teaching & Learning Toolkit, digital guidance report) returned HTTP 403, and the Kim & Webb (2022) spacing meta-analysis and Webb (2007) were not accessible in full. The IES/WWC English Learners practice guide was read in full text.

## 1. Vocabulary acquisition in L2 children (exposures, retrieval and spacing, pictures, gestures, songs, stories, incidental vs intentional, word frequency)

### Takeaway
Repetition matters but has only a medium effect on its own, and young learners gain less from mere incidental exposure than older learners; the best-supported recipe for a 7-year-old is **intentional teaching of a small set of words, many spaced retrieval encounters across several days, with pictures plus gestures the child reproduces**. Songs and stories help receptive knowledge but short song exposure alone is not enough for productive use.

### Cited Findings
- **Repetition (meta-analysis) [MODERATE]:** Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019, *Language Learning*) synthesized 45 effect sizes from 26 studies (N = 1,918) on number of encounters and incidental L2 vocabulary learning; the overall correlation was medium, r = .34, and 10 moderators were examined (including nonword use, test format, forewarning of test) — [zendy/Language Learning record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12343); [Cambridge record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C)
- A secondary summary of that meta-analysis reports the repetition effect was **larger for university students than for primary/secondary learners**, and that the number of repetitions needed depends on which aspect of word knowledge is tested and on proficiency/education level — [reference-global (secondary source)](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/eip-2023-0001.pdf)
- A later repetition meta-analysis in the listening modality is cited as finding that **at least about seven repetitions** were needed for significant gains — [Reading/centaur (as summarized in search results)](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/108688/8/13621688221140521.pdf). Not read in full; treat as approximate.
- Webb (2007, *Applied Linguistics* 28(1), 46–65), "The effects of repetition on vocabulary knowledge": the commonly cited finding is that gains in multiple aspects of word knowledge (form, meaning, grammar, association) kept increasing up to 10 encounters, and that even 10 encounters did not give full knowledge — [UNVERIFIED; citation confirmed via Crossref listing](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/receptive-and-productive-vocabulary-learning-the-effects-of-reading-and-writing-on-word-knowledge/DDF362AE7B13D1949B1CD591DA2F3414). Chen & Truscott (2010) replicated Webb's design with real words and found that orthography, part of speech and meaning develop at different rates with more encounters — [Lextutor chapter / search summary](https://www.lextutor.ca/text_encounters/Ch07.doc)
- **Spacing [MODERATE–STRONG for spacing itself; WEAK for "expanding" schedules]:** Nakata (2015, *SSLA*) found a small but significant advantage of expanding over equal spacing, but the **amount** of spacing had a much larger effect (large effect sizes) than the type of schedule — [Cambridge SSLA](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-expanding-and-equal-spacing-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/D1D796306985C52F9BE7A1200AC50DB9)
- Nakata's later critical review concluded that across L2 vocabulary studies the pedagogical value of expanding spacing "may be limited" (none of the other studies found benefits of expanding spacing) — [Kansai University repository](https://kansai-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/10256/files/KU-1100-20181000-02.pdf)
- A meta-analysis of spaced practice in L2 learning (98 effect sizes, 48 experiments; associated with Kim & Webb 2022, *Language Learning* 72(1), 269–319) found spacing had a **medium-to-large** effect; shorter spacing was as good as longer spacing on immediate tests but **worse on delayed tests** — [Western University thesis record (as summarized in search results)](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)
- For collocations, expanding retrieval helped short-term retention while equally spaced retrieval was better long term — [Cambridge SSLA abstract](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/effects-of-retrieval-schedules-on-the-acquisition-of-explicit-automatizedexplicit-and-implicit-knowledge-of-l2-collocations/201FABD72088A7F590598A5973E5B499)
- **Intensive instruction on few words [STRONG, official]:** The IES/WWC guide *Teaching Academic Content and Literacy to English Learners in Elementary and Middle School* (Baker et al., 2014, NCEE 2014-4012) rates "Teach a set of academic vocabulary words intensively across several days using a variety of instructional activities" as **Strong evidence**: six studies met WWC standards with positive effects on vocabulary, English language and reading outcomes; three directly tested intensive instruction on a few selected words. How-to steps: choose a brief engaging text as a platform; choose a **small set** of words for in-depth instruction; teach them in **multiple modalities (writing, speaking, listening)**; teach word-learning strategies (word parts, context, **cognates**) — [IES/WWC guide PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)
- The same guide says visual supports (pictures, diagrams, video clips) help bridge word meanings, and recommends short videos (under ~5 minutes), visuals and graphic organizers to anchor instruction (Recommendation 2, **Strong**) — [IES/WWC guide PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)
- **Gestures / embodiment [WEAK–MODERATE; small samples but consistent]:** Tellier (2008, *Gesture* 8(2), 219–235): 20 French children (mean age 5;5) learned 8 English words, either with pictures (n = 10) or with gestures they had to reproduce while repeating the word (n = 10). Gestures, and especially reproducing them, significantly improved **active (productive)** recall; the author attributes this to added motor memory leaving a "richer trace" — [HAL open archive](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00375251/file/3420.pdf); [JBE platform](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/gest.8.2.06tel). Note the learners were francophone, which makes this directly relevant.
- Porter (2012, Southampton thesis) studied gesture in learning French vocabulary in English primary schools — [Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/381522/1/2012_Porter_A_M_Use_of_Gesture_in_Learning_French_Vocabulary_in_Primary_Schools.pdf) (not read in full).
- Total Physical Response (Asher, 1960s–70s): learners respond physically to spoken commands before speaking; the original studies were small and often by the method's author — [UNVERIFIED, no source opened this session; see Gaps].
- **Songs [WEAK]:** Coyle & Gómez Gracia (2014, *ELT Journal* 68(3), 276–285): 25 Spanish 5-year-olds, three 30-minute lessons built around one well-known song; the authors concluded the song input was **insufficient to develop productive knowledge** in most children (receptive gains reported by secondary sources) — [CSUCA catalogue abstract](https://catalogosiidca.csuca.org/Record/CR.UNA01000275527/Description); [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/659136488/Using-songs-to-enhance-L2)
- Albaladejo, Coyle & Roca de Larios (2018, *System* 76, 116–128) compared stories, songs, and both, for incidental EFL vocabulary in Spanish preschoolers — [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100068165314_0x000001). I could not retrieve which condition won (see Gaps).
- Pichette & Leśniewska (2016) found **no difference in recall** between words met in songs and in stories for preliterate ESL children — [IES REL West Ask-A-REL (as summarized)](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/edlabs/regions/west/Ask/Details/80); [Benjamins LTYL](https://benjamins.com/catalog/ltyl.00003.but)
- **Word frequency (Nation) [STRONG as a principle, UNVERIFIED here]:** Nation's long-standing position is that the most frequent ~1,000–2,000 word families give the largest coverage of everyday speech and should be taught first, with high-frequency words deserving deliberate attention — [UNVERIFIED; no Nation source opened this session].

### Inferences
- Design each English "unit" around **6–10 target words** (WWC "small set"), revisited over **several days**, aiming for roughly **7–10+ meaningful encounters per word** across different activity types (hear → point → choose → say → use in a sentence). The exact number is not fixed by the evidence; younger learners likely need more encounters, not fewer, given the age moderator in Uchihara et al.
- **Spacing beats schedule shape:** a simple equal or mildly expanding review (e.g., same day, next day, 3 days, a week) is enough; do not over-engineer an SRS curve. Prefer longer gaps for long-term retention.
- **Retrieval, not re-exposure:** reviews should make the child *produce or choose* the word (picture → which word did you hear? / say the word) rather than just re-show it.
- **Mechanic "Do the move":** each new concrete word gets an original gesture shown by the character; the child is invited to copy it (camera not needed — the app can ask "fais le geste avec moi!"). This maps to Tellier's reproduce-the-gesture condition. Action verbs suit TPR-style "Simon says"-like commands ("Jump! Touch your nose!") with the child tapping the matching picture.
- **Songs/rhymes as wrap-around, not core:** use short original chants to recycle the unit's words, but always followed by retrieval games; songs alone gave mostly receptive gains.
- **Story mode:** a short original illustrated story read aloud (shared-reading style) containing the unit's words several times, with tap-to-hear and picture support, then comprehension questions.
- Word lists should start with high-frequency, concrete, picturable words (colours, numbers, family, animals, food, body, classroom, actions) plus **true cognates** (see section 2) which give fast early wins.

### Gaps
- Could not open Webb (2007) or the Kim & Webb (2022) meta-analysis to confirm exact numbers or child-specific moderators.
- No source opened this session for Asher's TPR studies, Paivio's dual coding, Nation's frequency lists, or Mayer's multimedia principles; claims about them are flagged UNVERIFIED.
- No result for the Albaladejo et al. (2018) winning condition (stories vs songs vs both).
- Little direct evidence specific to 6–8-year-olds on how many exposures are needed; most repetition studies use older learners.

## 2. Phonics and early reading in English as L2, and transfer from French (phonological awareness, cognates, hard sounds)

### Takeaway
Systematic teaching of phoneme awareness and letter–sound decoding has **strong** evidence for early readers generally, and IES says English learners need the same explicit, systematic foundational instruction, **combined with** oral language/vocabulary. Phonological awareness transfers across French and English, and French–English cognates are a resource; the specific sounds francophones struggle with (/h/, "th", short vs long vowels, stress) need perception plus production practice.

### Cited Findings
- **Foundational reading (K–3) [STRONG for sound awareness and decoding]:** The WWC guide *Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade* rates: (1) teach academic language incl. vocabulary — **Minimal**; (2) develop awareness of segmental sounds in speech and how they link to letters — **Strong**; (3) teach decoding, word-part analysis, writing and recognizing words — **Strong**; (4) daily reading of connected text for accuracy, fluency, comprehension — **Moderate**. The page itself makes no specific statement about English learners — [IES/WWC Practice Guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/21)
- **English learners [MODERATE]:** The IES English Learners guide (Recommendation 4, **Moderate**; six studies, 5 of 14 domain effects positive, 9 non-discernible) states that in the primary grades core instruction must include "explicit and systematic attention to foundational reading skills (i.e., phonological awareness, phonics, and fluency)", but that interventions for ELs should also include listening comprehension, vocabulary and language development, and that ELs do **not** show foundational-skill difficulties at a disproportionate rate compared to native speakers — [IES/WWC guide PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)
- Full ratings of that guide: Rec. 1 intensive vocabulary — **Strong**; Rec. 2 integrate oral and written English into content teaching (videos, visuals, daily structured talk) — **Strong**; Rec. 3 regular structured writing opportunities — **Minimal**; Rec. 4 small-group intervention — **Moderate** — [IES/WWC guide PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf); [IES webinar slides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_ellwebinarslides_052114.pdf)
- **Cognates [WEAK–MODERATE, recommended within a Strong recommendation]:** The guide recommends teaching students to use cognates as a word-learning strategy, notes Romance languages lend themselves to cognate instruction because of shared Latin origin, and warns to teach **false cognates** explicitly (its example: Spanish *embarazada* vs English *embarrassed*) — [IES/WWC guide PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)
- **Cross-linguistic transfer in Canadian French/English children [MODERATE]:** Comeau, Cormier, Grandmaison & Lacroix (1999, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 91, 29–43) is a longitudinal study of phonological processing in children learning to read in a second language; it is cited as showing that phonological awareness and pseudoword decoding predict reading across both languages — [Springer, citing paper](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11145-015-9585-9); [Cambridge Applied Psycholinguistics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/longitudinal-study-of-phonological-processing-skills-and-reading-in-bilingual-children/CEFFA121407BB4C7C2F4B355D725DB46)
- Jared, Cormier, Levy & Wade-Woolley (2010, *J. Educ. Psych.*) found that English phonological awareness, letter-sound knowledge, rapid naming and grammatical ability predicted later French reading in French-immersion children (i.e., cross-language prediction in the English→French direction) — [Université de Moncton PDF](https://www.umoncton.ca/prof/sites/prof.prod.umoncton.ca/files/Jaredetal.JEdPsy2010.pdf)
- **Hard sounds for Québec francophones [MODERATE for /h/ and "th" (Québec studies, adults); WEAK for others]:** A Québec Chief Scientist research summary of a study with 50 francophone Quebecers found their errors on "th" and "h" were **inconsistent rather than systematic**, and suggests training both **perception and production** — [Scientifique en chef du Québec](https://www.scientifique-en-chef.gouv.qc.ca/en/research-impact/pronouncing-the-th-and-h-sounds-is-no-easy-task)
- A 2023 *Frontiers in Language Sciences* study on Québec learners found /h/ is sometimes produced like French uvular /ʁ/, and learners both **delete** /h/ and **insert** it before vowel-initial words — [Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/flang.2023.1286084)
- Teaching sources (not peer-reviewed) list typical francophone errors: "th" → /s z/ or /t d/; /ɪ/–/iː/ merger (ship/sheep); /æ/–/ɑː/ (hat/heart); even French-like stress instead of English stress-timed rhythm — [Pronunciation Studio](https://pronunciationstudio.com/french-speakers-english-pronunciation-errors/); [Université de Limoges, Espaces linguistiques](https://unilim.fr/espaces-linguistiques/631); [pronounce.tv](https://pronounce.tv/blog/common-french-speaker-pronunciation-errors)
- Lightbown & Spada (*How Languages are Learned*) discuss cross-linguistic influence and cognates as resources and Québec ESL classroom research — [UNVERIFIED; book not opened this session].

### Inferences
- Melina is learning to read in French now (2e année). English phonics should come **after** a solid base of oral English vocabulary and should explicitly **contrast** the two code systems: same letters, different sounds (e.g., English "i" in *sit*, "a" in *cat*, silent-e, "th", "sh", "ch", "h" pronounced).
- Suggested order (inferred, not prescribed by the cited guides): (a) oral phoneme games in English (rhyme, first sound) using known words; (b) letter–sound pairs that differ least from French first (m, s, t, p, n...), then the "traps" (short a/e/i/o/u, h, th, sh, ch, w, j); (c) blending CVC decodable words (cat, sit, hop); (d) a few high-frequency irregular words (the, is, you, I, my) taught as whole "tricky words".
- **Mechanic "Ship or sheep?"**: minimal-pair listening games (hear one word, tap the right picture) for /ɪ/–/iː/, /h/ vs no /h/ (hat/at, hair/air), "th" vs s/t (think/sink, three/tree). Because Québec errors are inconsistent, perception practice should be repeated and mixed, not a one-off lesson.
- **Mechanic "Cognate garden"**: quick wins with true cognates (animal, orange, table, music, piano, lion, train...), plus a small "faux amis" mini-game later (e.g., *library* ≠ librairie, *coin* ≠ coin) — the false-cognate warning is in the WWC guide.
- **Stress mechanic:** clap or "big bubble/small bubble" for syllable stress (BAnana, eLEphant), since stress is a known francophone weak point.

### Gaps
- No experimental study found on **synthetic phonics in English as a foreign language for young francophones**; the phonics evidence is from L1 readers or US English learners immersed in English.
- Could not open Comeau et al. (1999) for effect sizes; Québec /h/ and "th" studies concern adults, not children.
- The EEF phonics toolkit page was blocked (403), so EEF months-of-progress figures are not reported here.

## 3. Listening first and comprehensible input vs explicit instruction; age and amount of input (Muñoz)

### Takeaway
In classroom (limited-input) settings, younger starters do **not** outperform older ones in rate; **amount and quality of input and time** matter more than starting age. For a 7-year-old with limited exposure, an app must add a lot of meaningful listening and combine it with explicit, intentional teaching, rather than rely on "natural" absorption.

### Cited Findings
- **Barcelona Age Factor (BAF) project [MODERATE]:** compared classroom learners starting English at 8, 11 and 14; older starters progressed faster in early stages — [Muñoz, GALA invited paper](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/papers/invited%20speakers/Munoz.pdf); [IRAL 2008](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/IRAL.2008.009/html)
- Muñoz (2011, *IRAL*): after similar amounts of input, starting age was not a predictor of outcomes; input had modest but significant effects on proficiency — [De Gruyter IRAL](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral.2011.006/html)
- An ACTFL brief summarizing this work says the amount of time and the kind of classroom activities may predict success more than age — [ACTFL Early Language Learning brief](https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/Documents/Early_Language_Learning_Brief.pdf)
- Muñoz has argued that young learners in foreign-language classrooms may not get enough exposure to benefit from the presumed implicit-learning advantage of youth — [secondary citation via search summary of Muñoz 2006 chapter](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/7430835-for-example-in-spain-the-barcelona-age-factor-baf-project) (low-quality aggregator; the primary is Muñoz (ed.) 2006, *Age and the Rate of Foreign Language Learning*, Multilingual Matters)
- Repetition effects are weaker for primary-age learners than for university learners in incidental learning (see section 1) — [reference-global (secondary)](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/eip-2023-0001.pdf)
- IES rates "integrate oral and written language instruction" with daily structured talk and visual anchors as **Strong** for ELs — [IES/WWC guide PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)
- Krashen's Input Hypothesis (comprehensible input is necessary and sufficient) — [UNVERIFIED; no source opened]. The commonly reported counter-evidence from Canadian immersion (Swain, Lyster) is that input alone left productive accuracy gaps — [UNVERIFIED; see section 4 for Lyster & Ranta, which is the verified part].

### Inferences
- **Receptive before productive:** each word should first be met through listening + picture (tap the picture you hear) before the child is asked to say it. Productive demands come later in the cycle and with support (model, then echo).
- **Comprehensible input is necessary but not sufficient** for this profile: the app should offer lots of short, picture-supported listening (stories, instructions, dialogues) *and* deliberate retrieval practice.
- Since total time is the strongest lever, prioritise **short daily sessions** (frequency over length) and make listening content the "free play" area.
- All English audio must be clear native-like models; the child's L1 (French) can be used for instructions and encouragement so cognitive load stays on the English target.

### Gaps
- No primary source opened this session for Krashen, Swain's output hypothesis, or meta-analyses of explicit vs implicit instruction (Norris & Ortega 2000; Spada & Tomita 2010); these are relevant but unverified here.
- BAF results concern ages 8+; there is little classroom data for 6–7-year-old starters in Québec specifically.

## 4. Corrective feedback for L2 errors in children (recasts vs prompts; Lyster & Ranta, Québec immersion) and how to do it in an app

### Takeaway
Research from Québec/Canadian French immersion shows teachers mostly use **recasts** (reformulating correctly), which produce the least learner repair, whereas **prompts** (elicitation, clarification request, metalinguistic clue, repetition) push the child to self-correct and show larger effects; **younger learners benefit more from corrective feedback** than older ones.

### Cited Findings
- **Lyster & Ranta (1997, SSLA) [MODERATE, observational, Québec-area]:** 18.3 hours of interaction in four Grade 4/5 French immersion classes; identified six feedback types and defined "uptake" as the student's immediate response. Teachers preferred implicit reformulations (**recasts and translation**), leaving little room for learner-generated repair, so uptake and immediate repair rates were low — [McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/0p096b851); [CARLA ACIE Bridge](https://archive.carla.umn.edu/immersion/acie/vol1/Bridge1.3.pdf)
- Reported rates: about 34% of student utterances contained an error and teachers gave feedback on about 62% of errors — [search summary of McGill/other copies](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3057/305752034001.pdf). The widely quoted per-type figures (recasts ≈ 55% of all feedback but only ≈ 31% uptake; elicitation ≈ 100% uptake; metalinguistic and clarification requests ≈ 86–88% uptake) are [UNVERIFIED — the per-type table was not retrievable this session].
- **Lyster & Saito (2010, SSLA 32, 265–302) meta-analysis [MODERATE–STRONG]:** 15 classroom studies (N = 827). Effects were **larger for prompts than recasts**, especially on free constructed-response measures; a regression found **younger learners benefited more from CF than older learners** (age coded as a moderator, so it is a between-study inference) — [Cambridge SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1); [McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- Caveat: Li's (2010) meta-analysis used different inclusion criteria and analyses, so magnitudes are not directly comparable — [Cambridge SSLA page and commentary](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)

### Inferences (app mechanics)
- **Feedback ladder (prompt first, model second):** on a wrong answer, (1) a prompt: "Hmm, écoute encore..." + replay the target audio or highlight the key sound/letter (elicitation / metalinguistic clue in French); (2) second try; (3) if still wrong, give the correct form (a recast/explicit correction) with the picture, then re-present the item later in the session (retrieval).
- For tap/choose items, the "prompt" can be a contrastive replay of the two options (ship vs sheep) rather than simply showing the answer.
- For spoken production (if speech capture is ever used), automatic speech recognition for a child's L2 English is unreliable; a safer design is "listen and repeat" with self-comparison, without scoring pronunciation. Note also the project rule that child audio must not go to external APIs.
- Keep feedback short, kind and specific; never more than one correction per item, and do not penalise inconsistent /h/ or "th" heavily, since Québec research shows these errors are inconsistent even in adults.

### Gaps
- Most CF research is on oral classroom interaction with a teacher; I found no controlled study of **app-delivered** prompts vs recasts for young children.
- Per-type uptake percentages from Lyster & Ranta (1997) need checking against the original table.

## 5. What EEF / IES evidence says about digital L2 learning for children

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses of technology-assisted vocabulary learning for EFL learners show **moderate-to-large** effects, but they mix ages from preschool to university, often compare to weak controls, and no child-only meta-analysis was found; IES guides recommend short videos/visuals as tools within instruction rather than as replacement. EEF guidance could not be retrieved (blocked).

### Cited Findings
- Hao et al. (WSU, 2020): 45 studies, 2,374 preschool-to-college EFL learners; overall effect d = 0.845 for technology-assisted vocabulary learning, with a benefit for long-term retention; moderators include device type and game condition — [WSU showcase](https://showcase.wsu.edu/2020/04/22/technology-assisted-vocabulary-learning-for-efl-learners-a-meta-analysis/); [WSU dissertation record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/doctoral/TECHNOLOGY-ASSISTED-VOCABULARY-LEARNING-FOR-EFL-LEARNERS/99900591957001842)
- Another meta-analysis: 34 studies, d = 0.64, mobile-assisted more effective than computer-assisted — [CORE record](https://core.ac.uk/works/137208221); age comparison unclear in the available text.
- Li (2025, *International Journal of Applied Linguistics*): digital personalized vocabulary learning, 25 effect sizes, 1,342 EFL learners, g = 0.761, with age group, system type and duration as moderators — [CiteDrive record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/unraveling-effects-of-digital-personalized-vocabulary-learning-an-evidencebased-analysis-from-fourdimensional-theory/)
- Quizlet meta-analysis (Frontiers in Psychology, 2024): significant effects on vocabulary achievement, retention and attitude — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1349835/pdf)
- IES EL guide: short videos and visuals as "anchors" plus structured talk and vocabulary instruction (Strong) — technology as a tool inside a designed lesson — [IES/WWC guide PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf)
- EEF *Using Digital Technology to Improve Learning* guidance and the Teaching & Learning Toolkit (phonics, digital technology) — pages returned HTTP 403; [EEF digital guidance (blocked)](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/digital). From background knowledge [UNVERIFIED]: EEF's guidance stresses that technology should supplement good teaching and is most promising for practice/retrieval and feedback; the toolkit rates phonics as high impact for early readers.

### Inferences
- The positive effect sizes support building the app, but likely gains come from the **learning principles embedded** (retrieval, spacing, feedback, multimedia), not from "being digital".
- Personalisation (adapting review to the child's weak words) is a plausible plus given Li (2025), and fits the app's existing weak-point tracking.
- Games should keep extraneous animation low during the learning moment; rewards after correct retrieval, not for passive watching.

### Gaps
- No child-only (6–10) meta-analysis of digital L2 learning found.
- EEF content not retrieved; EEF claims above are unverified.
- No evidence found specific to francophone children using English apps.

## 6. Ranking of methods by evidence, and translation into concrete game mechanics

### Takeaway
Highest confidence: intensive teaching of small word sets over several days with multiple modalities; spaced retrieval practice; systematic phoneme-awareness + letter-sound decoding (once oral English exists); prompts-first corrective feedback. Medium: pictures/video anchors, gesture reproduction, cognates, minimal-pair perception training. Lower: songs alone, pure incidental exposure, expanding (vs equal) spacing schedules.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences (proposed mechanics, by skill)
| Skill | Evidence-backed principle | Game mechanic idea (original content) |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary | Small set, several days, multimodal, spaced retrieval [STRONG] | "Seed packet" of 6–8 words per theme; day 1 listen-and-tap, day 2 choose-the-picture, day 3 say-it/echo, later spaced reviews mixed into other games; a word "blooms" only after successful retrieval on different days |
| Vocabulary | Gesture reproduction [WEAK–MOD] | Character shows a gesture per word; "Do it with me!" moment; action-verb command game ("Hop! Clap! Sit!") with picture taps |
| Vocabulary | Cognates [WEAK–MOD] | "Cousin words" mini-game: hear English, find the French cousin; later a "tricky cousins" (faux amis) round |
| Listening | Receptive first; input amount [MOD] | Short illustrated stories with audio and tap-to-hear words; comprehension by pointing; "follow the instructions" scenes (Put the apple in the basket) |
| Phonics (later) | Phoneme awareness + letter-sound + decoding [STRONG, L1/EL] | Rhyme and first-sound sorting with known words, then English letter-sound "traps" vs French, CVC blending with decodable words, a few tricky words |
| Pronunciation | Perception + production of /h/, th, /ɪ/–/iː/, stress [MOD] | Minimal-pair "Which one did you hear?" (hat/at, three/tree, ship/sheep); clap-the-stress game |
| Sentences | Integrate oral language, structured talk, sentence frames [STRONG, EL] | Picture-sentence matching ("The cat is on the bed"), build a sentence from word tiles heard in audio, yes/no questions about a scene |
| Writing/spelling (later) | Structured writing practice [MINIMAL, EL] | Spell known decodable words by tapping letters after hearing them; copy-then-recall; keep low priority until decoding exists |
| Feedback | Prompts first, then model; younger learners benefit [MOD–STRONG] | Wrong answer → replay/contrast hint → retry → show answer with audio → item comes back later |
| Digital | Tech effects d ≈ 0.6–0.85, mixed ages [MOD] | Personalised review of weak words; rewards tied to retrieval, not to watching |

### Gaps
- None of the strongest guides were designed for EFL with francophone 7-year-olds; all mechanics above are inferences that should be validated by observing Melina's actual performance (the app's own data).
- The order of English letter-sounds for francophone children has no direct experimental source found here; the proposed order is a reasoned inference.
