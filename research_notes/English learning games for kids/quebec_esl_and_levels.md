# Québec elementary ESL curriculum (francophone schools) and the CEFR / Cambridge YLE level ladder

Scope: what francophone Québec children (ages 6–12) are expected to learn in English as a Second Language (ESL, "anglais, langue seconde", ALS) per cycle, how it is taught and assessed, and how this maps to CEFR Pre-A1/A1/A2 and Cambridge Pre A1 Starters / A1 Movers / A2 Flyers. Research date: 18 Nov 2026. Primary documents were downloaded and read in full (text extracted from the official PDFs). Word lists were NOT copied, only their size and category names are described.

## 1. What the Québec ESL program (PFEQ) requires per cycle: competencies, approach, time, progression

### Takeaway
Québec ESL has two different programs. Cycle One (1re–2e année) is a 2006 oral-only, listening-first program built on authentic English songs, rhymes and stories. It has two competencies and explicitly sets **no reading or writing expectations**. Cycles Two and Three (3e–6e) use a communicative approach with three competencies (interact orally, reinvest understanding of texts read and heard, write texts) and explicit focus on form. The official documents (programs 2001/2006, Progression of Learning 2009, Evaluation Framework 2011) are still the ones the Ministry publishes in 2025–2026. I found no sign of a revision.

### Cited Findings
**Status and documents (current as of late 2025)**
- The Ministry's elementary ALS page lists four official documents: the Cycle One program, the Cycles Two–Three program, the Progression des apprentissages and the Cadre d'évaluation. It also lists support material, including Cycle One strategy posters and minicards, a "Profile of Elementary Cycle One ESL Students", functional-language posters and minicards for Cycles 2–3, and intensive-English guides. The page was last updated on 28 November 2025, and it says nothing about a program revision or about instructional time. — [Québec.ca, Anglais, langue seconde (primaire)](https://www.quebec.ca/education/prescolaire-primaire-et-secondaire/programmes-formations-evaluation/programme-formation-ecole-quebecoise/primaire/anglais-langue-seconde)
- The Cycle One program was issued by the MELS in 2006. The minister's cover letter (8 June 2006) made it mandatory from September 2006. It was piloted from 2004 to 2006 in 5 regions, then 7, and the pilot found no interference with French literacy learning. The pilot report says that 1.5 to 2 hours of English per week produced important gains in comprehension and let pupils begin to express themselves simply. — [PFEQ, Anglais, langue seconde, Primaire, Premier cycle (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- The Cycle One program was written so that it would not require changes to the Cycles Two–Three program, which had just been implemented. It also takes into account that grade 1–2 children are just starting to read and write in French. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)

**Cycle One (grades 1–2): the program Melina is in now**
- Two competencies:
  - (C1) *To act on understanding of texts* (Mobiliser sa compréhension de textes entendus). The program calls it the "cornerstone" of Cycle One.
  - (C2) *To communicate orally in English* (Communiquer oralement en anglais).
  
  The program is "axé sur la dimension orale". The teacher speaks only English from the first lesson. Children learn by listening, imitating, singing, acting out, communicating and retelling. There is "aucune attente sur le plan de la lecture et de l'écriture". — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- "Authentic texts" are songs, rhymes and stories written for English-mother-tongue children. The program says to use only texts written for English-speaking children. Songs and rhymes that involve movement (actions, gestures) come first. Stories with predictable sequences and recurring refrains come first among stories. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- The program names the "rhythmicality" of English (prosody: phonemes, stress, intonation, rhythm, pace) as the main thing Cycle One children attend to. Repeated singing makes the brain absorb this prosody. Visual supports are often the only way to decode meaning without falling back on French. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf); [Progression of Learning ESL, Elementary (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Grade 1 and grade 2 learners are described differently:
  - A grade-1 child, especially in the first months, behaves like a preschooler: needs to move, has a very short attention span, lives in the moment.
  - A grade-2 child can read and write in French, cooperates better, already knows some English, and behaves more like a Cycle Two pupil.
  - Interaction is mostly teacher-to-whole-group, especially in grade 1. Pair work becomes more frequent as the cycle goes on.
  
  — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- The group, and classroom routines that make the setting predictable, lower the "affective filter" (the program cites Lightbown & Spada, *How Languages Are Learned*). In a group, individual errors are less visible. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- The program asks for "quiet activities": drawing, labelling, matching pictures to words, done individually while the latest song or story plays in the background. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf); [Profile of Elementary Cycle 1 ESL Students (MEQ working document)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/ressources-pedagogiques/anglais-langue-seconde/Student-Summary-Infographics-English-Second-Language-Elementary-Cycle-1.pdf)
- "Personalized versions of texts" are a **grade-2-only** element. With templates ("canevas") that hold the core of the original text, children swap words of a song, rhyme or story for known words of their own choice. They do not write full sentences and do not use a writing process. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- How a child progresses in C1:
  - At the start, the child reacts mostly non-verbally, echoes words just heard, imitates prosody clumsily, and has a small repertoire of songs and stories.
  - Later, the child names key elements aloud, joins in refrains spontaneously, retells with the group, and creates personalized versions with peers.
  
  — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- End-of-cycle expectations for C1. The child:
  - has developed an ear for English prosody;
  - with support and visuals, names key elements and important events orally;
  - sings known songs and rhymes with the group, saying most of the words with gestures;
  - retells stories with the group (names main characters, recites refrains, knows the order of events, renders passages in own words);
  - creates personalized versions with peers using templates.
  
  — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- How a child progresses in C2:
  - At the start, the child mostly functions within the group, shows understanding non-verbally, answers with isolated words, and rarely starts speaking in English.
  - Later, the child decodes messages more easily, takes risks, and expresses needs and ideas simply from a personal repertoire, increasingly on their own initiative.
  - The program says children "rarely" use ready-made formulas or complete sentences. Instead they combine words creatively.
  
  End-of-cycle expectations for C2: global comprehension of everyday classroom language; reacts appropriately in known situations; with support, takes part in class life only in English, using words, groups of words and short expressions. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- The program states that a child does not need to understand every word to grasp the global meaning. Children decode through known words, **cognates** (e.g. address/adresse), visual cues, stress, pauses and intonation, and make "intelligent guesses". — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- The program grounds its rationale in critical-period and brain claims: ages 3–7 are optimal, capacity declines between 8 and 10, and a "15%" figure is given for age 17, citing Sousa. Note: this is the program's own rationale. The empirical research in section 5 does not support strong early-start advantages in low-hour classroom programs. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf); contrasted by [Lightbown & Spada 2019, *Language Teaching*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0261444819000454/type/journal_article)

**Cycles Two and Three (grades 3–6)**
- Three competencies:
  1. *To interact orally in English* (Interagir oralement en anglais), the central one;
  2. *To reinvest understanding of oral and written texts* (Réinvestir sa compréhension de textes lus et entendus);
  3. *To write texts* (Écrire des textes).
  
  From the start of grade 3, the pupil speaks English in all class situations. The C1 criteria are: use of functional language, use of strategies, participation in exchanges, and pronunciation. — [PFEQ ALS 2e–3e cycles (Ch. 5.2)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-2e-3e-cycles-primaire.pdf)
- The Cycles Two–Three program is based on the communicative approach, plus "explicit focus on form linked to these contexts". — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Learning contexts by cycle (Progression of Learning):
  - Cycle One: language emerges "mostly natural[ly]" through authentic songs, rhymes, stories and routines.
  - Cycle Two: a personal repertoire of functional language through routines and tasks on familiar environments (school, home, neighbourhood, family).
  - Cycle Three: tasks on topics "of a broader scope" (e.g. ecology, heroes, children from other countries).
  
  — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- The MEES 2016 "Evolution of the ESL Programs" chart summarizes the elementary cycles like this:
  - Cycle One: rely on lots of visual support, imitate rhythmicality, use words and strings of words; no expectations for functional language, reading or writing.
  - Cycle Two: start using targeted functional language in brief exchanges (classroom routines; familiar topics such as school, family, food). Reinvestment tasks are step by step, heavily guided, and based on models. Writing is introduced as a process with a checklist and explicit models.
  - Cycle Three: a wider range of functional language and broader topics; short, well-structured texts written from open-ended models.
  
  — [Evolution of the ESL Programs, Elementary Cycle One to Secondary Cycle Two (MEES working doc, 2016)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/ressources-pedagogiques/anglais-langue-seconde/Evolution-ESL-Programs-English-Second-Language-Elementary.pdf)

**Instructional time and intensive English**
- In one school service centre's 2025–2026 timetable grid, Anglais gets 108 minutes in Cycle 1 and 162 minutes in Cycles 2–3. The grid is built on a total of 2,700 minutes, which matches a 9-day cycle of 25 hours/week, so this works out to about 1 h/week in Cycle One and about 1.5 h/week in Cycles 2–3 (my calculation). A separate grid exists for "Anglais intensif 6e année". — [CSS de Portneuf, Répartition des matières 2025-2026](https://cssportneuf.gouv.qc.ca/uploads/documents/bourdons/Repartition-matieres-grille-horaire-du-primaire-2025-2026.pdf)
- The 1–1.5 h/week figure in that grid is below the 1.5–2 h/week the Cycle One pilot used. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- Intensive ESL (EIALS) is offered in grade 6 in many schools:
  - A recognized model concentrates 300 to 369 hours within one school year (for example 5 months of English and 5 months of other subjects, or alternating half-days).
  - It is not immersion: in francophone schools, no other subject may be taught in English.
  - Intensive English has existed in Québec since 1976.
  
  — [MEQ, Modèles d'organisation scolaire EIALS](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/ressources-pedagogiques/anglais-langue-seconde/Anglais-intensif-primaire-modeles-organisation-scolaire.pdf) (via search snippet); [MEQ guide, ALS en contexte intensif](https://www.education.gouv.qc.ca/fileadmin/site_web/documents/education/jeunes/pfeq/prim-en-ls-guide-enseig-ALS-contexte-intensif.pdf) (via search snippet)

### Inferences
- For a 2e année child, the school-aligned target is the **end of Cycle One**: understand classroom English and songs/stories globally, join in refrains and action songs, name key story elements, express needs and ideas with words and short strings, and (grade 2 only) swap words into templated texts. Reading and writing English are *beyond* school expectations at her age. Gentle word-picture matching and labelling fits the program's "quiet activities", so that is the natural bridge.
- The program's pedagogy maps directly onto game mechanics:
  - TPR / "act it out" responses, which the program calls the Physical response strategy;
  - repeated exposure to the same song or story;
  - prediction of refrains;
  - picture sequencing to retell a story (the program suggests ordering illustrations to rebuild a story);
  - substitution templates ("personalized versions");
  - "intelligent guessing" from pictures and cognates.
- "Beyond school" for Melina could legitimately mean Cycle Two content (functional-language chunks like "Can I…?", "I like…", "What colour is it?", plurals, adjective-before-noun) delivered orally first.
- The app must use original content, not the copyrighted books the program cites. Traditional public-domain rhymes are a separate question that the parent should decide (CLAUDE.md rule 5 says "original content").

### Gaps
- I could not find an official MEQ minimum time for ESL per cycle. The time figure above comes from one school service centre's grid. Other centres may differ.
- I did not read the English-language edition of the Cycle One program. I read the official French PDF; competency names in English are taken from the 2011 Evaluation Framework and the 2009 Progression of Learning, which are in English.
- I found no Ministry document announcing a new ESL elementary program for 2026. The quebec.ca page (Nov 2025) still lists the 2001/2006 programs.

## 2. Vocabulary, themes and functional language: Cycle One and beyond

### Takeaway
Cycle One deliberately does **not** prescribe a word list. Each child builds a *personal repertoire* from classroom life, songs and stories. The categories are: classroom routines, action words, objects, people, needs, ideas/likes, courtesy formulas, teamwork phrases, and simple descriptions (adjective + noun). Theme vocabulary (family, food, animals, clothing, sports, special events, colours, shapes, numbers, alphabet, prepositions, time) is formally targeted from **Cycle Two**, alongside a large set of functional-language chunks.

### Cited Findings
- The Cycle One essential knowledge has three categories: Contextual Language, Strategies and Cultural Elements. Cycles Two–Three have five: Functional Language, Strategies, Language Conventions, Text Components and Cultural Products. — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Cycle One contextual language. The program says these words are "not targeted in advance by the teacher"; the child keeps what they need. Subcategories with examples:
  - **Classroom routine**: common questions and statements (e.g. "Finished?", "Ready?", "Red pencil?"), instructions ("Bring your scrapbook"), reacting to instructions ("I'm ready!"), choral responses ("Hurry up!", "No problem"), encouragements ("Good job!", "Excellent!").
  - **Song, rhyme and story language**: key elements (characters, actions, objects, places), events, recurring passages, lyrics.
  - **Personal repertoire**: action words ("Stand up", "Come here"); objects (pencils, desk, flashcards, mittens); people (friends, mom, dad, girl, boy, principal); needs ("Washroom, please?", "Drink, please?", "Repeat?"); ideas ("Happy, today", "I like blue"); courtesy ("Hi", "Good morning", "Please", "Thank you", "Excuse me", "Sorry", "Bye"); teamwork ("My turn/your turn", "Show me", "Good idea!", "Wait a minute!"); descriptions ("Big. Big monster. Big blue pants.").
  
  — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- In the Progression of Learning, Cycle One items are linked by an arrow (→) to their Cycle Two–Three counterparts. The arrow means the Cycle One child is "becoming aware" of that knowledge, which is then targeted in Cycle Two. — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Cycles Two–Three functional language (useful expressions), by function. Each has examples in the document:
  - classroom routines (date, weather);
  - giving instructions (game instructions such as "Roll the dice", "Pick a card");
  - stalling for time;
  - asking for help or clarification ("How do you say…?", "Can you repeat?");
  - identification ("It's a polar bear", "What's this?");
  - describing basic characteristics and asking about them ("What colour is it?", "Is it round?");
  - capabilities ("I can…", "Can you…?");
  - W-questions and yes/no questions;
  - agreement and disagreement;
  - opinions;
  - permission ("May I…?");
  - offering, accepting and declining help;
  - needs and wants ("I need…", "I want…");
  - warnings ("Watch out!");
  - feelings, interests, tastes and preferences ("I'm happy", "My favourite flavour is…");
  - courtesy, greetings and leave-taking, thanking, apologizing;
  - suggestions and invitations;
  - rejoinders to keep an exchange going ("What about you?");
  - teamwork expressions.
  
  — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Cycles Two–Three vocabulary areas:
  - action words frequently used in class;
  - immediate environment (school, then other familiar places such as living room, grocery store, park);
  - **theme-related vocabulary: family, sports, pastimes, food, animals, clothing, special events**;
  - personal pronouns and possessives;
  - alphabet (spelling words in context);
  - cardinal and ordinal numbers frequently met in class;
  - colours and shapes;
  - prepositions and location words;
  - time expressions;
  - question words.
  
  — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Language conventions (Cycles Two–Three only; Cycle One has none and works only on phonology through listening):
  - word order in simple sentences, with adjective before noun;
  - regular "-s" plurals and common irregular plurals (people, children, feet);
  - articles;
  - verb tenses targeted for tasks (imperative, past, future);
  - intelligible intonation and pronunciation;
  - punctuation (capital letter, period or question mark, commas in lists);
  - spelling copied from explicit models (Cycle Two), then from open-ended models (Cycle Three).
  
  — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Strategies:
  - Cycle One compensatory strategies: asking for help, asking for repetition, gesture.
  - Cycle One learning strategies: physical response (acting out a response), self-monitoring, self-evaluation, directed attention, use of prior knowledge, predicting, inferencing (context, cognates, known words, visuals, intonation, recurrent passages), practice (joining in songs and refrains), using resources (posters, flashcards, peers), cooperation, risk-taking (speak only English, experiment).
  - Cycles 2–3 add: delaying speech, circumlocution, planning, note-taking (T-chart, Venn diagram, story web), skimming, scanning, and tolerating not understanding everything.
  
  — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf); [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- Cycle One cultural elements are text *genres*:
  - nursery rhymes, choosing (counting-out) rhymes, jump-rope rhymes, action songs, circle rhymes, ball-bouncing rhymes, clapping rhymes, sing-alongs;
  - predictable picture-book stories;
  - authentic audio models (CDs and DVDs, resource people).
  
  Cycles Two–Three cultural products are texts and media, games, famous people, origins of names, landmarks, celebrations (birthdays, Christmas, Earth Day) and idioms. — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Text components: Cycle One discovers key elements and events orally with the group, and "there are no expectations in Cycle One for students to read texts on their own". Cycles Two–Three use connecting words ("once upon a time", "next", "finally"), contextual cues (titles, illustrations), overall meaning, and sequencing events. — [Progression of Learning ESL (2009)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)

### Inferences
- The app's "absolute beginner" content should be built from Cycle One categories, not a fixed word list:
  - classroom and routine commands as TPR;
  - courtesy and greetings;
  - needs ("…, please?");
  - likes ("I like + colour/food/animal");
  - adjective + noun descriptions (size + colour + noun);
  - people and family;
  - a small set of action-song and story vocabularies.
- The usual themes for a 7-year-old (colours, numbers, animals, body, family, food, clothing, toys, weather, feelings) appear officially as Cycle Two targets or as Cycle One examples. Teaching them orally and receptively is consistent with both cycles.
- Recommended order for the app: Cycle One routines, needs and courtesy → theme nouns plus colour and size adjectives (receptive) → Cycle Two chunks (identification, "Can I…?", "What colour…?", likes and preferences, "I can…") → plurals and word order → reading and spelling from models (Cycle Two writing).

### Gaps
- No official Québec word count or target vocabulary size exists for any elementary cycle. The program is explicitly non-list-based in Cycle One.
- I did not find a Québec-published list of "required" songs. The program's song and story titles are examples only.

## 3. International ladder: CEFR young-learner descriptors and Cambridge YLE (Pre A1 Starters / A1 Movers / A2 Flyers)

### Takeaway
The CEFR Companion Volume defines **Pre-A1** as a band halfway to A1 where the learner has no "generative capacity" yet and relies on words and formulaic expressions. This is essentially what Cycle One describes. The Council of Europe's 2018 *Collated Representative Samples… Young Learners aged 7–10* gives age-adapted can-do statements from Pre-A1 upward and flags which adult descriptors suit 7–10 year-olds. Cambridge's Starters → Movers → Flyers form a three-step "bridge from beginner to A2", with cumulative published vocabulary lists organized in about 18 themes. I found no official word counts.

### Cited Findings
**CEFR / Council of Europe**
- Pre-A1 "represents a 'milestone' halfway towards Level A1". At this band the learner "has not yet acquired a generative capacity" and relies on a repertoire of words and formulaic expressions. Pre-A1 is included in most CEFR scales. — [CEFR Companion Volume (Council of Europe, 2020)](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)
- Sample Pre-A1 descriptors (paraphrased):
  - **Overall listening**: recognizes everyday familiar words delivered clearly and slowly in a familiar context.
  - **Understanding instructions**: understands short simple instructions ("Stop", "Close the door") delivered slowly, face to face, with pictures or gestures, and repeated if needed.
  - **Reading**: recognizes familiar words accompanied by pictures (e.g. a picture book with familiar vocabulary); deduces a word's meaning from an accompanying picture or icon.
  - **Interaction**: understands and uses basic formulas (yes, no, please, thank you, sorry, excuse me), recognizes greetings, greets, gives own name and takes leave.
  - **Conversation/production**: can ask and answer about self and routines with short formulaic expressions, relying on gestures.
  - **Grammar**: uses very simple word-order principles in short statements.
  - **Strategies**: manages very short rehearsed utterances, using gesture and requests for help.
  
  — [CEFR Companion Volume 2020](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)
- The CV's young-learner approach: the Council of Europe deliberately did **not** calibrate new descriptors for children. It collated ELP (European Language Portfolio) descriptors and Cambridge young-learner descriptors, mapped them to the CEFR, and published two documents (ages 7–10 and 11–15) organized by level from Pre-A1. Descriptors judged beyond children's cognitive, social or experiential capacity were filtered out. — [CEFR Companion Volume 2020](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4)
- *Collated Representative Samples of Descriptors… Young Learners, Volume 1: Ages 7–10* (2018, collated by T. Szabo, Eurocentres):
  - Drawn from ELPs from 13 countries (10 sources for the younger group) plus Cambridge English.
  - The 7–10 band was chosen to correspond roughly to Piaget's concrete-operational stage.
  - It is explicitly "not intended as a ready-made curriculum" but as a resource for local curricula and portfolios.
  - Literacy is treated with a "bias for best" assumption (optimum literacy support).
  - C2 is omitted for 7–10 and C1 barely appears. The authors say there is no "glass ceiling", but most C-level descriptors imply maturity irrelevant to this age group.
  
  — [CoE Collated Representative Samples, Young Learners 7–10 (2018)](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688)
- Example young-learner can-dos from that collation (paraphrased):
  - A1 listening: understand simple short songs and rhymes learned at school; understand a short illustrated story (e.g. an animal story); perform the actions to a song or story as it is heard; draw characters and events from a story while listening; understand simple sentences about animals or weather.
  - Pre-A1 (and A1) listening: understand the important words in a song or rhyme; words for colours and shapes, clothes and food, weather, holidays and festivals.
  - Pre-A1 instructions: classroom commands such as stand up, sit down, listen, open your book.
  - A1 spoken production: "I can say or sing a song".
  - A1 writing: write short sentences about likes and dislikes, clothing and free time using given pictures and words.
  
  — [CoE Collated Representative Samples, Young Learners 7–10 (2018)](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688)
- Individual profiles are uneven: a learner "globally B1" may be A2 in some activities and B1+ in others. Levels describe profiles, not uniform abilities. — [CoE Collated Representative Samples, Young Learners 7–10 (2018)](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688)

**Cambridge English Young Learners (Pre A1 Starters, A1 Movers, A2 Flyers)**
- Alignment: Starters targets Pre-A1. Movers targets A1, with a strong performance indicating A1. Flyers targets A1 and A2, with a strong performance indicating A2. Together they "form a bridge to take children learning English as a second language from beginner to basic user level (A2)". Results go on the Cambridge English Scale (Pre-A1 around 80–99, A1 around 100–119, A2 around 120–139, as read from the handbook's chart). — [Cambridge Pre A1 Starters, A1 Movers and A2 Flyers Handbook for Teachers (2024)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)
- There is **no pass or fail**. Every candidate gets a certificate with 1–5 "shields" per skill (Listening, Reading & Writing, Speaking). The design aims for a positive first experience of testing. — [Cambridge YLE Handbook (2024)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)
- Format by level:

  | Level | Listening | Reading & Writing | Speaking | Total time |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Starters | ~20 min (4 parts / 20 items) | 20 min (5 parts / 25 items) | 3–5 min | ~45 min |
  | Movers | ~25 min | 30 min (6 parts) | 5–7 min | ~62 min |
  | Flyers | ~25 min | 40 min (7 parts / 44 items) | 7–9 min | ~74 min |

  Correct spelling is required in Reading & Writing. In Starters Listening, candidates hear only the numbers 1–20 and the spelling of names. — [Cambridge YLE Handbook (2024)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)
- Can-do summaries (paraphrased):
  - **Starters**: understand spoken alphabet letters, short simple instructions, simple questions about self (name, age, favourites, routine), very simple descriptions of people and objects (how many, colour, size, location); name familiar people and things; give very basic descriptions; answer with single words or yes/no; write the alphabet and own name; copy words and short sentences; spell some very simple words; follow very short simple stories.
  - **Movers**: understand simple dialogues and descriptions with pictures; agree and disagree; answer with phrases and sentences; describe pictures and actions; tell a very simple picture story; ask how someone is and about habits and preferences; read simple signs, short factual texts and short stories with pictures; write short sentences about pictures, personal details, likes and dislikes.
  - **Flyers**: understand multi-sentence instructions and everyday conversations; ask basic questions; tell short stories from pictures or own ideas; talk briefly about the past; read short stories with narrative tenses and texts with unknown words; link sentences with "and", "because", "then"; write a short story.
  
  — [Cambridge YLE Handbook (2024)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)
- The vocabulary lists are published in full (alphabetic, thematic and grammatical/structures lists) and are **cumulative**: Movers candidates must know everything on the Starters list, and Flyers candidates everything on the Starters and Movers lists.
  - Thematic categories: Animals; The body and the face; Clothes; Colours; Family & friends; Food & drink; Health; The home; Materials; Names; Numbers; Places & directions; Sports & leisure; Time; Toys; Transport; Weather; Work; The world around us.
  - Input is mainly standard British English, avoiding terms that confuse American English learners. Some US terms are listed, both accents appear in Listening, and either spelling standard is accepted in output.
  
  — [Cambridge YLE Handbook (2024)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)
- Vocabulary size:
  - No official Cambridge word count was found.
  - A third-party site claims about 400 words for the Movers list (unverified). — [flyer.us](https://flyer.us/?p=804)
  - My rough token count of the 2024 handbook's thematic lists (nouns and theme words only, excluding function words and grammar, and imprecise because of PDF extraction) gave roughly 280 Starters, 350 new Movers and 510 new Flyers tokens. This puts the cumulative thematic vocabulary on the order of 1,100+ items by Flyers. Treat it as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a fact. — derived from [Cambridge YLE Handbook (2024)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)
- Study-hour estimates: a publisher's teacher notes give approximately 100 hours of English for Starters, 175 for Movers and 250 for Flyers. This is not an official Cambridge figure. — [Macmillan YLE Starters teacher's notes](https://www.macmillan.pl/files_catalog/07_egzaminy/yle_tests/YLE_Starters_eTeachers_Notes_sml.pdf)

### Inferences (proposed mapping; there is no official Québec–CEFR alignment)
- **End of Québec Cycle One (grade 2) ≈ Pre-A1, receptive-leaning.** The match is close: the CEFR Pre-A1 definition (formulaic repertoire, no generative capacity, gesture support) mirrors the Cycle One end expectations (words, strings of words, short expressions; global comprehension of classroom English; joining in songs). Cycle One has no reading or writing, whereas Starters tests copying and simple spelling, so a grade-2 Québec child would typically be weaker than a Starters candidate on Reading & Writing.
- **End of Cycle Two (grade 4) ≈ A1** (Movers-like functional language: identification, description, likes, abilities, yes/no and W-questions, model-based writing).
- **End of Cycle Three (grade 6) ≈ A1+/A2 in regular programs**, and plausibly A2 or above after intensive grade-6 English. This is speculative, since no study measuring Québec pupils on the CEFR was found.
- Hours sanity check: at about 1 h/week × about 36 weeks, a Québec child gets roughly 70 h of ESL over Cycle One. That is below the publisher's estimate of about 100 h for Starters, so Pre-A1 by the end of grade 2 is a realistic school ceiling.
- A practical app ladder:
  - L0 "first sounds" (Pre-A1 receptive: TPR, greetings, colour and number recognition);
  - L1 "Cycle One complete" (Pre-A1: songs and stories, needs, likes, adjective + noun);
  - L2 "Starters-like" (Pre-A1 to A1: theme nouns across the Cambridge categories, alphabet and spelling of names, numbers 1–20, simple questions about self);
  - L3 "Movers-like / Cycle Two" (A1: sentences, preferences, simple picture stories, reading short illustrated texts);
  - L4 "Flyers-like / Cycle Three" (A2: past tense narratives, connectors, short writing). "Advanced for her age" ≈ L3, and "beyond school" ≈ L4.
- Spelling standard: Québec documents use Canadian/British "colour" and "favourite". Cambridge accepts British and American spellings. The app can use Canadian spelling consistently.

### Gaps
- No official Cambridge vocabulary size per level was found. The figures above are third-party or rough self-counts.
- No official or peer-reviewed alignment of the Québec ESL program to the CEFR was found.
- The CoE collated document has paraphrased descriptors only. I did not extract every scale (for example vocabulary range or phonology for 7–10).

## 4. How young L2 learners should be assessed (receptive before productive, listening first)

### Takeaway
Québec's own framework weights Cycle One toward comprehension of heard texts (60%) over oral communication (40%). It asks teachers to observe ongoing participation with observation grids and anecdotal notes, to give feedback on strategies without marking them, and to accept non-verbal reactions as evidence of understanding. Cambridge YLE uses picture-based, no-fail tasks with shields. Both support a receptive-first, low-anxiety, embedded assessment design.

### Cited Findings
- Evaluation Framework (MELS, 21 April 2011), elementary ESL weightings:
  - **Cycle One**: To act on understanding of texts **60%**; To communicate orally **40%**.
  - **Cycle Two**: Interact orally 50%; Reinvest understanding 35%; Write 15%.
  - **Cycle Three**: Interact orally 45%; Reinvest 35%; Write 20%.
  
  Strategies (and in Cycle One, contextual language and cultural elements) "must be provided with feedback… but must not be considered" in the report-card mark. — [Framework for the Evaluation of Learning, ESL Elementary (2011)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/cadres-evaluation/primaire/anglais/PFEQ-cadre-evaluation-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- Cycle One criteria as clarified in the Framework appendices:
  - Understanding of texts is shown by using words and expressions from texts with appropriate actions when performing songs and rhymes, and by using recurring passages to join in during storytelling and retelling. In Elementary 2 only, it also includes using words from texts and resources to create a personalized version.
  - Understanding of oral messages is shown by reacting with **verbal or nonverbal** responses.
  - Transmitting messages means starting and responding to exchanges with single words, strings of words or short expressions.
  
  — [Framework for the Evaluation of Learning, ESL Elementary (2011)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/cadres-evaluation/primaire/anglais/PFEQ-cadre-evaluation-anglais-langue-seconde-primaire-AN.pdf)
- The Cycle One program recommends that teachers rely on observation of participation with observation grids and anecdotal records, and evaluate only the most frequently used, observable strategies. Children keep a portfolio (lyrics, drawings, labels, personalized texts, reflection sheets). Teacher questions guide self-regulation and self-assessment, for example whether the child watched the gestures, answered in English, or learned new words today. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- End-of-cycle expectations distinguish "guided by the teacher" (a lot of help) from "supported by the teacher" (occasional help on request). This is a built-in scaffolding scale. — [PFEQ Cycle One (2006)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-anglais-langue-seconde-1er-cycle-primaire.pdf)
- Cambridge YLE design principles: tasks use meaningful language in clear, accessible contexts, with heavy picture support. The aim is to "close the distance" between children's learning and testing experiences. Full vocabulary lists are published so children know what to expect. There is no pass or fail, and shields reward each skill. — [Cambridge YLE Handbook (2024)](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/young-learners-handbook-2024.pdf)
- The CEFR young-learner collation includes receptive "can-dos" that are demonstrated by action rather than speech: performing the actions to a song or story as it is heard, and drawing characters and events while listening. — [CoE Collated Representative Samples, Young Learners 7–10 (2018)](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688)
- Secondary sources (teacher-prep guides, lower reliability) attribute these points to McKay (2006, *Assessing Young Language Learners*) and Cameron (2001, *Teaching Languages to Young Learners*): child assessment should be ongoing, formative, low-anxiety and embedded in familiar games and routines, for example TPR "Simon says" tasks that let pre-speaking children show mastery without speaking pressure. — [open-exam-prep study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/tesol-certificate/young-planning-assessment/age-appropriate-formative-assessment) (not a primary source)
- A 2019 paper in *Teanga* criticizes normalizing a long "silent period", arguing it can deny children active participation. Receptive-first should not mean a production ban. — [Teanga, Journal of the Irish Association for Applied Linguistics (2019)](https://doaj.org/article/5894f271c6e8414fa944ffd4aabb551d)

### Inferences
- Make in-app assessment mostly receptive at first: tap or drag the picture you hear; act out or pick the action; order story pictures. Make production optional and encouraged rather than gated. This matches the 60/40 Cycle One weighting and the Pre-A1 descriptors.
- Track strategies (asking for repetition, using picture clues) as feedback only, never as score. This mirrors the Framework rule.
- Use "guided" vs. "supported" as the hint-level model: more hints means "guided", fewer means "supported", and no hints means independent.
- Use no-fail, shield/star-style progress reporting per skill, like Cambridge YLE. Avoid percentages shown to the child.

### Gaps
- I did not access McKay (2006) or Cameron (2001) directly. The principles attributed to them come from secondary summaries.
- No Québec study on how Cycle One teachers actually assess was found.

## 5. Research on early ESL in Québec (Lightbown & Spada and others)

### Takeaway
Québec researchers (Lightbown, Spada, Collins and colleagues) found that **concentrated** (intensive) time beats thinly spread time. They also found no long-term advantage from an early start when instruction is only a couple of hours a week. Grade 1–2 ESL is therefore best treated as building an ear, a positive attitude and a formulaic repertoire, not as a guarantee of later proficiency.

### Cited Findings
- Lightbown & Spada (2019/2020, *Language Teaching*, "Teaching and learning L2 in the classroom: It's about time") state that studies in school settings worldwide "have failed to find long-term advantages for an early start" or for exclusive L2 use. They propose intensive instruction later in the curriculum and integrating language with content. — [Lightbown & Spada, *Language Teaching*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0261444819000454/type/journal_article) (abstract via search)
- Earlier Québec work notes that no substantial research supports the superiority of an early start when instruction is limited to a couple of hours a week. — [TESL Canada Journal, Lightbown/Spada articles](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/526/357/553) (via search snippet)
- Spada & Lightbown (1989) compared intensive grade-5/6 ESL with regular ESL. Intensive learners reported more contact with English afterwards and more positive attitudes. Collins, Halter, Lightbown & Spada (1999, about 700 students) found better outcomes for massed than for distributed time. — [TESL Canada Journal, "Intensive ESL Programmes in Quebec Primary Schools"](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/view/557)
- A later study reports that comprehensibility gains from intensive instruction may last even when later exposure is limited. — [Benjamins, *Journal of Second Language Pronunciation*](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/jslp.20026.fre)
- Lightbown (2014, *Language Awareness*, "Making the minutes count in L2 teaching") reviews "full-flow" approaches: intensive ESL, immersion, sheltered content, CLIL. — [Making the minutes count (abstract)](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/making-the-minutes-count-in-l2-teaching/813163853100613634-5209)

### Inferences
- For an app, the research suggests aiming for **frequent, short, concentrated exposure**, for example several short sessions a week with lots of listening input, rather than relying on age alone. The app can add meaningful hours beyond the roughly 1 h/week at school.
- The research also supports keeping motivation and attitude high. Positive attitude is an explicit Cycle One outcome in the MEQ profile.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve full texts of the Lightbown & Spada papers. Findings come from abstracts and snippets.
- No Québec study measuring actual Cycle One outcomes (vocabulary size, listening scores) at the end of grade 2 was found. The MELS pilot reports (2004–2006) are cited in the program, but the URLs listed for them are dead.
