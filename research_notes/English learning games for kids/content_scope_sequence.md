## 1. Vocabulary themes: order and original example words (picture-friendly flag)

### Takeaway
Start with concrete, picturable, high-frequency themes that work through listening and pointing: classroom routines, colours, numbers, animals, family, body, food. Abstract themes (feelings, time, places) come later and need scenes or animation. Use Canadian English choices and spelling (Mom, fall, toque, mittens, eraser, colour, grey, favourite) because she lives in Québec.

### Cited Findings
- Learners with very small lexicons mostly know concrete words, especially nouns. Verbs and longer utterances come with a larger vocabulary. **MODERATE.** — [Milton 2022, Language Learning Journal 50(2)](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2022.2045680) (citing Alexiou & Konstantakis 2009; Cameron 2001). Full text read from the [Swansea repository PDF](https://cronfa.swan.ac.uk/Record/cronfa60156/Download/60156__24252__9e5f8857101340e597a41cc353e81182.pdf).
- The same paper argues that frequency alone is over-applied in word selection. It recommends mixing frequent words with topical, less frequent "content" words (roughly a 50/50 balance) so learners have something to talk about. **EXPERT.** — [Milton 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2022.2045680)
- Cambridge's young-learner exams (Pre A1 Starters, A1 Movers, A2 Flyers, ages about 6–12) publish level word lists in alphabetic and thematic forms. They are a reasonable external benchmark, but the lists are copyrighted and must not be copied. **EXPERT.** — [Cambridge YLE handbook 2018](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/pl/Images/357180-starters-movers-and-flyers-handbook-for-teachers-2018.pdf); [A1 Movers word list](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/351850-a1-movers-word-list-2018.pdf) (not readable this session)
- The Council of Europe's young-learner descriptors (ages 7–10) start at Pre-A1 and are "can-do" statements, not word lists. They suit level goals, not vocabulary choice. **EXPERT.** — [CoE collated descriptors, ages 7–10 (2018)](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688); [CoE bank of supplementary descriptors](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/bank-of-supplementary-descriptors)

## 2. Phonics sequence for English as L2

### Takeaway
Use a systematic synthetic phonics order. Teach a small first set of high-utility letter–sound pairs that already make many CVC words. Blend left to right from week one. Then go simple to complex: remaining single letters, consonant digraphs, adjacent consonants, final-e long vowels, a few vowel teams. Introduce tricky words gradually and read only decodable text. For Melina, start reading **after** oral vocabulary exists (L3), because she is still consolidating French decoding in 2e année.

### Cited Findings
- **WWC foundational reading guide (K–3, 2016)** has four recommendations:
  - **STRONG** evidence for teaching awareness of sound segments and linking them to letters.
  - **STRONG** evidence for teaching decoding, word parts, and word writing and recognition.
  - **MODERATE** evidence for daily reading of connected text.
  - **MINIMAL** evidence for academic language and vocabulary.
  - Source: [IES/WWC Practice Guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/21)
- **DfE 2021 validation core criteria for SSP programmes (EXPERT, England):**
  - Start with a small, defined set of grapheme–phoneme correspondences that let children read and spell many words early, then progress simple to complex until the main correspondences are covered.
  - Children blend phonemes left to right to read, and segment to spell.
  - Common-exception (tricky) words are introduced gradually.
  - Children should not be taught to guess words from pictures or context or to memorize whole word shapes.
  - Practice texts are almost fully decodable from the letter–sound pairs already taught.
  - Progress is checked often, with extra practice for those falling behind.
  - Source: [GOV.UK SSP validation supporting documentation](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/phonics-teaching-materials-core-criteria-and-self-assessment/validation-of-systematic-synthetic-phonics-programmes-supporting-documentation)
- **Letters and Sounds (DfES 2007):**
  - Its Phase Two introduced letters in small weekly sets. The first set was s, a, t, p, chosen so children can build words like simple CVCs immediately.
  - Later phases added the remaining letters, then digraphs and trigraphs, then adjacent consonants, then alternative spellings.
  - DfE later removed it from the list of validated programmes because it is not a complete SSP programme.
  - Sources: [Letters and Sounds 2007 PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7aa7b6e5274a34770e630c/Letters_and_Sounds_-_DFES-00281-2007.pdf); removal reported by [Tes](https://www.tes.com/news/phonics-should-schools-ditch-letters-and-sounds) and [OUP blog](https://educationblog.oup.com/primary/what-makes-an-effective-phonics-programme-and-how-is-it-validated)
- The WWC English-learners guide recommends explicit, systematic foundational skills for English learners together with oral language. It also recommends using cognates while explicitly teaching false cognates. — [IES/WWC English learners guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf) (via the sibling note `l2_methods_children.md`, not reopened)

### Inferences (original sequence for the app; words are my own)
The principle behind common SSP orders:
- **Utility first**: letters that combine into the most real words, e.g. vowels a and i plus frequent consonants.
- **Easy blending**: continuous sounds such as s, m, n, f can be stretched, which makes blending easier.
- **Separate confusable pairs**: don't introduce b/d or m/n in the same week.

My order adds one francophone principle: put English-specific sounds (short vowels, h, th, w, final-e) in early, clearly flagged as "English letters, different from French".

| Step | Level | New code | Original practice words |
|---|---|---|---|
| P0 | L1–L2 | Oral only: rhyme, first sound, clap syllables, blend spoken sounds (/c/-/a/-/t/ → picture) | cat/hat/bat; sun/fun; ba-na-na |
| P1 | L3 | s a t p i n m d | sat, tap, pin, sip, map, dip, mad, nap, tin, pit |
| P2 | L3 | g o c k e u r | dog, cot, kit, pen, red, cup, rug, sun, mug, gum |
| P3 | L3 | h b f l, double ff ll ss | hat, hop, bed, bus, fun, leg, puff, bell, miss, hill |
| P4 | L4 | j v w x y z qu | jam, van, web, box, yes, zip, quiz, wet, fox, yum |
| P5 | L4 | Digraphs sh ch th ck ng (+ wh later) | ship, fish, chip, much, this, bath, duck, sock, ring, long |
| P6 | L4–L5 | Adjacent consonants (CCVC, CVCC) | frog, stop, flag, swim, hand, milk, jump, nest, crab, plum |
| P7 | L5 | Final-e long vowels a_e i_e o_e u_e, contrasted with short | cap→cape, kit→kite, hop→hope, cub→cube; cake, bike, bone, lake |
| P8 | L5–L6 | Vowel teams ee, ea, oa, ai, ay, igh, oo (long/short), ar, or | tree, seed, boat, rain, play, night, moon, book, star, corn |
| P9 | L6 stretch | Silent letters and -ed endings | knee, write, lamb; jumped, played, painted |

Tricky words, introduced 2–3 at a time from P1 onward and always in sentences: I, the, a, to, is, my, you, he, she, we, said, was, are, they, have, like, come, go, no, so.

App rules drawn from the cited criteria:
- Never ask her to read a word that contains code not yet taught.
- Every read item can be tapped to hear it sounded out and then blended.
- Picture choices come **after** decoding, not as a guessing cue.

## 3. Sentence patterns per level (original examples)

### Takeaway
Start with fixed chunks children can hear and point to ("It's a…", "I like…"). Then add one new structure at a time with lots of recycling: positions, have/has, plurals, there is/are, present continuous, can/can't, do-questions, and the simple past of very common verbs only at the top level.

### Cited Findings
- The CoE young-learner descriptors run from Pre-A1 through A1 to A2. At Pre-A1/A1, children understand and use very short, familiar phrases about themselves and their immediate world, with support. Level descriptions are paraphrased; I did not open the full text. **EXPERT.** — [CoE collated descriptors 7–10](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688)
- Québec Cycle One is built around oral comprehension and oral interaction, using songs, rhymes, stories and classroom routines. **EXPERT.** — [MEQ aide-mémoire Cycle One](https://www.education.gouv.qc.ca/fileadmin/site_web/documents/education/jeunes/pfeq/napperon_ALS_aide-memoire_primaire_1er_cycle.pdf); [PDA 2009](https://laurier.cssdm.gouv.qc.ca/files/PDA_PFEQ_anglais-langue-seconde-primaire_2009.pdf) (snippets)

### Inferences (original pattern bank)
- **L1 (Pre-A1, listen & point)**: "Hello! / Bye-bye!"; "What's this? It's a duck."; "Red!"; "Touch your nose." "Stand up." "Clap your hands."; "Show me five."
- **L2 (Pre-A1+)**: "It's a red car."; "I like apples." / "I don't like soup."; "I have a cat."; "Two cats, three dogs" (regular plural -s); "Is it a frog? Yes! / No!"; "My name is Lulu." (use a character's name, not Melina's)
- **L3 (A1 entry)**: "Where is the cat? It's under the bed."; "The ball is in the box."; "This is my grandma. She is happy."; "I'm seven." / "How old are you?"; "I can jump. Can you swim?"; "It's cold. Put on your mittens."
- **L4 (A1)**:
  - "There is a bird in the tree. There are two bees on the flower."
  - "The dog is running. What are you doing? I'm drawing."
  - "His hat is blue. Her boots are red." (his/her trap)
  - "Do you like pizza? Yes, I do."
  - Irregular plurals: "one mouse, two mice; one foot, two feet"
- **L5 (A1+)**:
  - "She can skate, but she can't ski."
  - "Does he have a sister? No, he doesn't."
  - "I get up at seven o'clock. On Saturday I go to the rink."
  - "How many apples are there? There are six."
  - "I want to play outside." / "Let's go to the park!"
- **L6 (A2 stretch)**:
  - Simple past, common verbs only: "Yesterday I played in the snow." "We went to the farm." "I saw a moose!" "It was sunny." "We ate pancakes."
  - Going to: "Tomorrow I'm going to visit Grandpa."
  - Comparatives: "The elephant is bigger than the mouse."
  - Because: "I'm happy because it's my birthday."

Grammar is never taught as rules on screen for L1–L3. From L4, use short, kind contrasts with French where a trap exists (see section 4).

### Gaps
- No source gives a validated order of grammatical structures for 7-year-old francophone ESL learners. The order above follows common young-learner course progressions and CEFR level logic (**INFERENCE**).

## 4. Francophone-specific traps and helpful cognates

### Takeaway
Target a small set of predictable French→English traps with short perception games first, then production:
- Sounds: /h/, "th", short vs long vowels, final consonants, word stress.
- Spelling and letter names.
- Grammar: adjective position, "I have 7 years", his/her, "the" with general plurals, do-questions, -ed.
- False friends.

Use true cognates early for quick wins, but drill their English pronunciation and stress.

### Cited Findings
- **/h/ (MODERATE, adult Québec studies):**
  - Dropping /h/ (e.g. "hair" sounding like "air") is the core Québec-francophone error. Learners also sometimes add an /h/ before vowel-initial words as hypercorrection.
  - Production is variable rather than all-or-nothing. The authors place the difficulty in perception and mental representation, not in articulation.
  - They doubt that spelling helps, because <h> is silent in French and in some English words (hour, honest).
  - Source: [John & Rigoulot 2023, Frontiers in Language Sciences](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/language-sciences/articles/10.3389/flang.2023.1286084/full)
- **"th" (MODERATE):**
  - Québec francophones typically replace English "th" sounds with stops (think sounding like "tink"), because the sound is absorbed into a nearby French category. — [John & Rigoulot 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/language-sciences/articles/10.3389/flang.2023.1286084/full)
  - Trofimovich found that Québec French speakers may not keep pairs like *three/tree* apart in their mental lexicon, even when they can pronounce some "th" words correctly. — [Trofimovich 2011 chapter, "When three equals tree"](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/lllt.30.09tro) (abstract-level)
  - A Québec study of 50 francophones found "th"/"h" errors inconsistent, and recommends training perception and production. — [Scientifique en chef du Québec](https://www.scientifique-en-chef.gouv.qc.ca/en/research-impact/pronouncing-the-th-and-h-sounds-is-no-easy-task) (via sibling note)
- **his/her (MODERATE, Québec children):**
  - Francophone children in Québec ESL classes have persistent trouble with *his/her*. French *son/sa* agrees with the thing owned, while English agrees with the owner.
  - Explicit instruction contrasting French and English, and metalinguistic teaching, helped Québec Grade 6 learners more than exposure alone.
  - Sources: [White 2008, "Speeding up acquisition of his and her: Explicit L1/L2 contrasts help"](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/lllt.23.13whi); White & Ranta 2002 and White, Muñoz & Collins 2007 ("The his/her challenge", *Language Awareness* 16(4)), as summarized in search results; [McGill thesis on input enhancement](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/n296x076j)
- **Intensive ESL research (EXPERT):** Lightbown and Spada's classroom research was carried out with francophone Grade 5–6 children in intensive ESL programs near Montréal. Their participants were older than Melina. — search summary; [Lightbown & Spada 1990 copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/724428162/LightbownSpada1990) (not opened)
- **Cognates and false cognates:** the WWC English-learners guide recommends teaching cognates as a strategy and teaching false cognates explicitly. — [IES/WWC EL guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/english_learners_pg_040114.pdf) (via sibling note)

### Inferences: traps → original exercise ideas
Sound traps:

| Trap | Why (FR→EN) | Original game idea | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| /h/ dropped or added | French has no /h/; <h> is silent in French | "Hat or at?": hear one word, tap the hat or the "@" sign. Pairs: hair/air, hand/and, heat/eat, hill/ill. A feather on screen moves only for /h/ words (animation only, no microphone) | L2–L4 |
| "th" → t/d (or s/z) | No interdental in French | "Three or tree?": three/tree, thin/tin, bath/bat, they/day. Mascot shows a "tongue peek" mouth picture | L3–L5 |
| Short vs long vowels | French has no tense/lax pairs | "Ship or sheep?" sorting: ship/sheep, bit/beat, full/fool, cap/cape (ties to P7) | L3–L6 |
| Final consonants | Final consonants are often silent in French | "Last sound catcher": cat/cap/can, bed/bell; hear the word, pick the final letter | L3–L4 |
| Word stress | French stresses the last syllable; English often the first | "Big drum, small drum" clapping for cognates: AN-i-mal, BA-by, TI-ger, ba-NA-na | L2–L5 |
| -ed endings | Learners say "-ed" as a separate syllable everywhere, or drop it | Three baskets for past verbs: /t/ jumped, /d/ played, /ɪd/ painted | L6 |
| English letter names | e/i, a/r, g/j sound swapped compared with French letter names; Canada says "zed" | Original alphabet chant plus "spell it" dictation where the letter name is heard and then shown | L3–L5 |
| Letter-sound habits | French "u", "ch", "j", "ou" read the French way | "English eyes" warm-up: chip (not "ship"), bus, jam, out; a quick "English glasses on" toggle | L3–L5 |
| Silent letters | k/w/b/gh silent in English (knee, write, lamb, light) | "Ghost letters" that fade out when tapped | L6 |

Grammar traps:

| Trap | Why (FR→EN) | Original game idea | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adjective after noun ("a car red") | French usually puts colour after the noun | "Paint train": drag the colour wagon in front of the noun wagon; the train only leaves when the order is "a red car" | L2–L4 |
| "I have seven years" | French uses *avoir* for age | Birthday cake: choose "I'm seven" vs "I have seven years"; friendly hint "In English we *are* our age" | L3 |
| his/her | Possessive agrees with the owner, not the object | "Whose boot?": a boy and a girl character; tap the owner, then pick his or her. Short FR/EN contrast hint (supported by White's work) | L4–L5 |
| "the" with general likes | "J'aime **les** pommes" → "I like **the** apples" | "Like it all?": picture of all apples in general → "I like apples"; one specific bowl → "I like the apples in the bowl" | L4–L5 |
| do-questions and negation | French has no "do" support | Question builder with word tiles: "Do you like…?" "I don't like…" | L4–L5 |
| Third-person -s | French verb endings are silent | "Snake -s": she likes, he runs; the -s sticker hisses | L5 |

False friends (kid-level, original picks):
- *raisin* (EN = dried grape; FR = grape)
- *prune* (EN = dried plum; FR = plum)
- *chair* (FR *chaise*; FR *chair* = flesh)
- *pain* (EN = hurt; FR = bread)
- *coin* (EN = money; FR = corner)
- *car* (EN = car; Québec FR *autobus/car* = coach)
- *four* (EN = 4; FR = oven)
- *main* (EN = most important; FR = hand)
- *library* (EN = bibliothèque; FR *librairie* = bookstore)
- *sale* (EN = sale; FR = dirty)
- *rest* (EN = se reposer; FR *rester* = stay)

Game: "Tricky cousins", introduced at L4 after the true cognates are solid.

Helpful true cognates for L1–L2 quick wins (same meaning, often near-identical spelling):
- animal, lion, tiger, elephant, giraffe, zebra, crocodile, dinosaur
- banana, orange, tomato, carrot, chocolate, pizza, salad, soup
- table, lamp, photo, piano, guitar, music, train, bus, taxi, ballet, violet

Teach that the meaning transfers but the sound changes. Pair each cognate with the stress game.

## 5. What each of the 6 levels contains, with target vocabulary size

### Takeaway
Move receptive → productive. L1–L2 are listening and speaking only, matching Québec Cycle One. L3 adds decoding of words. L4–L5 add reading sentences and spelling known words. L6 adds short scaffolded writing and simple past. Cumulative targets go from about 60 to about 850 receptive words. Productive vocabulary is roughly half of that. These sizes fit 1 h/week of school plus about 15 min/day in the app.

### Cited Findings
- In effective classrooms learners acquire about **3–4 words per contact hour**, and only 1–2 in poor conditions. On average about **half** of the words presented are later recalled. — [Milton 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2022.2045680) (citing Milton & Meara; Vassiliu 2001). **MODERATE** (secondary-school data).
- Knowing only **500–800 words** keeps learners at A1. An A2 target for teenage school learners implies roughly 3,000 words known. — [Milton 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2022.2045680). **MODERATE**, mostly teen data, so it is a ceiling and not a target for a 7-year-old.
- Greek EFL children reach at age 8, after 2–3 years of learning, vocabulary levels that UK learners reach only at 17–18. Young children can build substantial lexicons when input is rich. — [Milton 2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/09571736.2022.2045680), citing [Rodusaki & Alexiou 2021, Journal of Applied Linguistics 34](https://ejournals.lib.auth.gr/jal/article/view/8522) (165 Greek children aged 6–9; tests X-Lex and the picture-based Pic-lex; figures not visible on the abstract page). **MODERATE.**

### Inferences (level plan)
Arithmetic behind the targets (**INFERENCE**):
- School gives about 1 h/week and the app about 1.5 h/week (15 min/day), so about 2.5 contact hours/week, or about 100 h/year.
- At 3–4 words/hour that is 300–400 words acquired per year. Nation's figure of about 500 per year is the optimistic bound.
- So 6 levels over roughly 2 school years ≈ 800–900 receptive words. That is within A1 and touching A2 for a child.

Gating and ordering rules:
- **Listening first, always**: every new word is heard (and shown as a picture) before it is read, and read before it is spelled. This mirrors Québec's oral-first Cycle One and the WWC advice to pair decoding with oral language.
- **Reading English starts at L3** and only once the parent area confirms French reading is going well. This guards against French/English letter-sound confusion in 2e année (**INFERENCE**, no direct study found).
- **Unit size**: about 6–10 new words per unit, recycled across days (see `l2_methods_children.md`). At about 100 units of 8 words, the L6 target is reachable.
- **Mastery to advance**: about 80–90% correct on mixed retrieval of the level's words on two different days (**INFERENCE**, aligned with the app's existing "weak point won on another day" mechanic).
- **Data-file fields** per word: `id, en, fr_gloss, theme, level, picturable(bool), phonics_step, cognate(bool), false_friend(bool), trap_tags[] (h, th, vowel, final_c, stress), canadian_variant`.
