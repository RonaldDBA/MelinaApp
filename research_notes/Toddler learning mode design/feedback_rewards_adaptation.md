# Feedback, praise, rewards, voice instructions and difficulty adaptation for ages 2–4 (design of a French-Québec learning mode for a 3-year-old)

Evidence-strength labels used below:
- **[Strong]**: randomized experiment(s) or meta-analysis, replicated, in or near the target age.
- **[Moderate]**: a single experiment or a well-designed longitudinal study in or near the age band.
- **[Weak]**: correlational/small sample, adult/older-child evidence extrapolated, theory, or practitioner guidance.
- **[Secondary]**: I only saw an abstract, press release or a secondary summary, not the full paper.

Note on method: about 18 searches/fetches. Several primary PDFs were blocked (PMC reCAPTCHA, Nottingham repository 403), so many findings come from abstracts, press releases or summaries. These are flagged.

## 1. Praise and feedback at 2–4 (process vs person praise, tangible/expected rewards, immediate vs delayed feedback, errorless learning vs productive struggle)

### Takeaway
The best evidence at this age favours **specific, process/action-focused verbal praise** ("Tu as trouvé le rond rouge !") over trait labels ("Tu es intelligente"). It also favours **no announced, contingent tangible rewards** for an activity the child already enjoys. Expected tangible rewards lowered later free-choice engagement in 3–5-year-olds (Lepper et al. 1973) and lowered helping in 20-month-olds (Warneken & Tomasello 2008). Social praise did not have that effect. I found no direct evidence at 2–4 on immediate vs delayed feedback or on errorless vs productive-struggle learning in apps.

### Cited Findings
- **Gunderson et al. 2013 (Child Development) [Moderate, observational; Secondary]:** 53 families were videotaped at home when children were 1, 2 and 3 years old. Praise was coded as process (effort, strategies, actions), person (a fixed positive quality) or other. A higher *proportion* of process praise predicted more positive approaches to challenge, more strategy generation and more belief that traits can improve at ages 7–8. Total praise volume and person praise did **not** predict these outcomes. Parents of boys gave more process praise than parents of girls. — [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/parents-who-praise-effort-can-bolster-childrens-persistence-self-belief); [Temple News](https://now.temple.edu/news/2013-02-11/praising-effort-toddlers-predicts-positive-attitudes-toward-challenges)
- **Gunderson et al. 2018 (Developmental Psychology, with Dweck and Levine) [Moderate, observational; Secondary]:** process praise to toddlers predicted math and reading-comprehension achievement in elementary school, about 7 years later, through children's incremental motivational frameworks. — [ECR Hub summary](https://ecrhub.org/publications/parent-praise-toddlers-predicts-fourth-grade-academic-achievement-childrens)
- **Caveat:** small sample, correlational design, association only, not proof of cause. — [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/parents-who-praise-effort-can-bolster-childrens-persistence-self-belief)
- **Cimpian, Arce, Markman & Dweck 2007 (Psychological Science 18, 314–316) [Moderate, experiment, n=24 four-year-olds; Secondary]:** in a puppet drawing game, children heard either generic/person praise ("You are a good drawer") or specific/non-generic praise ("You did a good job drawing"). After later mistakes, the generic-praise group reacted worse. Children praised non-generically responded better to criticism and came up with ways to fix their drawings. Proposed mechanism: generic wording implies a stable trait. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children); [ScienceBlogs summary](https://scienceblogs.com/cognitivedaily/2007/09/13/in-1999-melissa-kamins-and)
- **ZERO TO THREE (practitioner guidance) [Weak]:** negative labels ("lazy") are harmful, and even positive labels such as "so smart" can narrow a child's idea of who they are. — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/celebrate-your-childs-strengths/)
- **Practitioner guidance [Weak]:**
  - Replace a generic "good job" with exactly what the child did well, so they know what to repeat. — [Pathways.org](https://pathways.org/more-than-good-job-try-these-ways-to-encourage-your-child)
  - Praise effort or improvement rather than waiting for something done very well, and praise much more often than you correct. — [Raising Children Network (AU)](https://raisingchildren.net.au/toddlers/behaviour/encouraging-good-behaviour/praise)
  - Over-the-top praise can feel patronizing. — [Happiest Baby](https://secure.happiestbaby.com/blogs/toddler/praise-toddlers-without-good-job)
- **Lepper, Greene & Nisbett 1973 (JPSP 28(1)) [Moderate–Strong historically; Secondary, figures unverified]:**
  - Design: preschoolers aged about 3–5 who already liked drawing with markers were assigned to expected award (a "Good Player Award" certificate announced in advance, n=18), unexpected award (n=18) or no award (n=15).
  - Result: the expected-award group later spent significantly less free-play time drawing than both other groups. Unexpected-award and no-award children did not differ.
  - One secondary source gives 8.59% of free-choice time for the expected-award group, which I could not verify.
  - The authors defined the "overjustification hypothesis" as interest being undermined when the activity becomes an explicit means to an extrinsic goal.
  - — [Teaching CS Substack summary](https://teachingcs.substack.com/p/drawing-for-rewards); [UMass ScholarWorks document citing the study](https://scholarworks.umass.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bbbf0f9d-d9fe-4325-994d-6648714fff80/content)
- **Warneken & Tomasello 2008 (Developmental Psychology 44(6), 1785–1788) [Moderate, experiment]:** 20-month-olds who got a **material reward** (a toy) for helping later helped less than infants who got **social praise** or nothing. The authors frame this as an overjustification effect in very young children and say no earlier study had tested it at this age. — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458228)
- **Serve and return (Harvard Center on the Developing Child) [Weak as app evidence; official guidance]:** the five steps are:
  1. Notice the child's serve and share their focus.
  2. Return it with support and encouragement.
  3. Give it a name (label what the child sees, does or feels).
  4. Take turns and **wait** for the child.
  5. Practise endings and beginnings: let the child signal when they are done and lead the start of a new activity.
  - — [Better Care Network reproduction of Harvard "5 Steps"](https://bettercarenetwork.org/library/principles-of-good-care-practices/child-development/5-steps-for-brain-building-serve-and-return); [Children's Literacy Foundation listing](https://childrensliteracy.ca/resource/harvard-center-on-the-developing-child-5-steps-for-brain-building-serve-and-return/)

### Inferences
Concrete rules for the toddler mode.
- **After a correct answer:** respond at once, with one short, warm, *specific action/process* phrase that names what the child did. Examples: "Oui ! Tu as trouvé le chat." / "Tu as compté jusqu'à trois !" / "Tu as bien cherché." Never use trait labels ("Tu es intelligente / bonne / un génie"). Vary the wording a little, but keep the structure consistent.
- **Never announce rewards in advance.** No "Si tu réussis, tu gagnes une étoile." Visible tokens, stars or certificates that become the *goal* recreate the Lepper/Warneken expected-reward condition.
  - If any collectible is used, make it unexpected, non-contingent or purely decorative ("surprise"), not a currency and not a count.
  - This matches the 7-year-old mode's existing "stars without currency" principle (commit e27fa3b, per the repo log).
- **After an error:** use a neutral, non-punitive tone with no buzzer, no red X and no "Non !". Name the target again and give help (see section 3). Do not praise effort falsely when the child was simply guessing; prefer "On essaie encore ensemble."
- **Feedback timing:** feedback should be immediate. This is an inference from the contingency literature in section 3 (Kirkorian et al.), not from a timing study. I found no evidence that delayed feedback helps 2–4-year-olds.
- **Errorless vs struggle:** the evidence at this age is lacking. The scaffolding literature (section 3) points to *low-error* learning with quick, graded help rather than letting a 3-year-old flounder. Brief "productive struggle" is plausible only for tasks the child can already almost do.
- **End of session:** use social, descriptive recognition, not a prize. For example, the character recaps one or two things the child did ("Aujourd'hui, tu as trouvé tous les ronds !").

### Gaps
- **Mueller & Dweck 1998:** not retrieved in this session. It is widely reported to involve late-elementary children, not toddlers, so it should be cited only as background, not as 2–4 evidence. Verify before citing.
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 meta-analysis:** not retrieved. I cannot report its age-specific moderators here.
- **Immediate vs delayed feedback, and errorless vs productive struggle:** no study found specifically at ages 2–4 in digital activities.
- **Naître et grandir:** French-language pages on praise were found, but their article text did not load, so their recommendations are not verified. The relevant pages are [1–3 years self-esteem](https://naitreetgrandir.com/en/step/1-3-years/behaviour/building-self-esteem-in-children) and [building self-esteem](https://naitreetgrandir.com/en/feature/social-skills/building-self-esteem-in-children). The report writer should not attribute specific advice to Naître et grandir from these notes.

## 2. Child-directed speech and spoken instructions (rate, repetition, short sentences, prosody, TTS intelligibility, using the child's name)

### Takeaway
For toddlers, **slower speech and clearer, hyperarticulated vowels help word recognition.** Expanded pitch range alone did not help in an experiment with 19-month-olds. Synthetic speech is generally less intelligible to young children than natural speech, 3-year-olds do worse than 4–5-year-olds in noise, and intelligibility improves with repeated exposure. Recorded human voice, or a high-quality, slowed TTS voice that is tested with the child, is the safer choice.

### Cited Findings
- **Song, Demuth & Morgan 2010 (JASA 128, 389–400) [Moderate, experiment; Secondary]:** in 19-month-olds, natural and manipulated infant-directed speech showed that **slower speaking rate** and **vowel hyperarticulation** improved word recognition (gaze to the target), while **wider pitch range did not**. — [PMC2921436](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2921436); [Macquarie PDF](https://www.mq.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/910080/2010JASA.pdf)
- **Raneri et al. [Weak, correlational]:** slower infant-directed speech rate at 7 months correlated with larger vocabulary at 2 years. — [Journal of Child Language](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/change-in-maternal-speech-rate-to-preverbal-infants-over-the-first-two-years-of-life/F729D7983EB712788E1063505215EAB5)
- **Late talkers [Moderate; Secondary]:** in a 2023 eye-tracking study of 2-year-olds, late talkers processed words more slowly, especially verbs, but learned and retained novel words as well as peers. Processing speed varies a lot at this age, so the activity should allow time. — [JSLHR 2023](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2023_JSLHR-22-00580)
- **Synthetic speech and young children [Weak–Moderate; Secondary]:** the search snippets did not let me reliably tie each finding below to its exact paper; treat the attribution to these ASHA/UMN sources as approximate.
  - A scoping review of about ten studies found little research on synthetic speech for children. Children follow adult trends but with lower intelligibility.
  - A 2012 study with preschoolers and repeated exposure: children identified significantly more words in human-recorded speech and in a higher-quality synthetic voice (AT&T) than in DECtalk, and their accuracy rose across sessions.
  - A 2006 study of 3–5-year-olds in background noise: 4- and 5-year-olds outperformed 3-year-olds.
  - — [UMN Experts: Synthesized speech intelligibility and early preschool-age children](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/synthesized-speech-intelligibility-and-early-preschool-age-childr/); [ASHA AJSLP 2012](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2012/11-0020)); [ASHA AJSLP 2006](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2006/015)); [ASHA AJSLP 2010](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1058-0360(2010/09-0024))
- **Serve and return, "take turns and wait" [Weak, official guidance]:** leaving time for the child to respond keeps the exchange going. — [Better Care Network / Harvard](https://bettercarenetwork.org/library/principles-of-good-care-practices/child-development/5-steps-for-brain-building-serve-and-return)

### Inferences
Concrete rules for spoken instructions.
- **Sentences:** one short sentence per instruction, with one action and the target word placed at the end and stressed. Examples: "Touche… le **chien**." / "Où est le **rond** ?" Avoid negations and two-step instructions for a 3-year-old.
- **Pace:** speak noticeably slower than adult conversation, with clear vowels and a pause before and after the target word. Exaggerated pitch melody alone is not enough (Song et al. 2010), so prioritise rate and articulation.
- **Repetition:** say the instruction once. If there is no touch after a few seconds, repeat it in the same words, then add a visual cue (the target glows or bounces). Keep a large "replay" button (ear or mouth icon) that repeats the exact same audio.
- **Voice:** prefer recorded human Québec-French voice clips for the fixed instruction set, which is small and finite at this age.
  - If TTS is used, choose the most natural neural voice available, slow it, and test it with the child.
  - Expect 3-year-olds to understand synthetic speech worse than older children, especially with background music, so turn music down or off while instructions play.
- **Turn-taking:** after an instruction, wait. Do not stack speech, sound effects and music. Do not interrupt a child who is still choosing.
- **Child's name:** use it sparingly, at the start of the session and in the end recap, to get attention. Do not use it in error feedback. No evidence was found either way (see Gaps).
  - Privacy: per the repo's CLAUDE.md rule 4, the name must never go to an external TTS API. Any name audio must be pre-recorded or generated locally.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found that sets an optimal words-per-minute rate for 2–4-year-olds' instruction-following. I cannot give a numeric rate from evidence.
- No study was found on using the child's own name in app instructions or feedback at this age.
- No study was found on modern neural TTS (as opposed to DECtalk-era voices) with preschoolers, or on Québec-French TTS with children.
- Prosody: a search snippet said local pitch/loudness increases on target words help 2-year-olds learn, but I could not identify the paper, so it is excluded from the cited findings.

## 3. Scaffolding and hints for toddlers (number of tries, gentle answer reveal, modeling, ZPD, frustration signs)

### Takeaway
Classic tutoring work with 3–5-year-olds shows that the **youngest children need much more direct, hands-on help** and that help should be adjusted moment to moment ("contingent"). On touchscreens, 2–3-year-olds learned from video **only when the required touch drew attention to the relevant information**, so taps must be meaningful, not "touch anywhere". No study gives an evidence-based number of attempts.

### Cited Findings
- **Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976 (J. Child Psychology & Psychiatry 17, 89–100) [Moderate, classic observational; Secondary]:**
  - Context: a tutor worked one-to-one with 30 children aged 3–5 on a block-building pyramid task. The paper coined "scaffolding": help that lets a child do what would be beyond their unassisted efforts. — [Carl Hendrick, "We Need to Talk About Scaffolding"](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/we-need-to-talk-about-scaffolding)
  - Age gradient: according to a secondary briefing, the tutor gave direct hands-on help about twice as often to 3-year-olds as to 4-year-olds, and about four times as often as to 5-year-olds. — [Structural Learning research briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/support-that-fades)
  - Contingency: adjusting support to performance ("contingent control") is the core idea. A 2021–22 preregistered replication of the related Wood, Wood & Middleton 1978 study reportedly found contingent instruction did **no better** than demonstration or verbal instruction [Secondary, blog account]. — [Carl Hendrick](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/we-need-to-talk-about-scaffolding)
- **Kirkorian, Choi & Pempek 2016 (Child Development 87, 405–413) [Moderate–Strong, experiment, n=116, 24–36 months]:** toddlers watched an on-screen person label an object in one of three conditions: no interaction, touch anywhere, or touch a specific spot. The youngest children learned from contingent video only when the touch **emphasized the important information**. Yoked (non-genuine) interactivity did not help. — [Ko Eun Choi publication page](https://koeunchoi.github.io/publication/2016-03-28-word-learning-touchscreens)
- **Choi, Kirkorian & Pempek follow-up [Moderate; Secondary]:** in 134 toddlers, working memory moderated whether contingent touch helped or hurt transfer. — [VTechWorks](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/5c728f8a-e4dc-442a-ad24-4807847077f1/full)
- **Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015 (Psychological Science in the Public Interest 16(1)) [Weak–Moderate, expert review]:** an app is educational when it supports learning that is active (minds-on), engaged (not distracted), meaningful and socially interactive, within a clear learning goal, ideally through *scaffolded exploration*. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html); [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps/)

### Inferences
A graded help ladder for a 3-year-old, all immediate.
1. **Attempt 1, wrong:** neutral sound or no sound, and the item gently "wobbles back". Repeat the instruction in the same words.
2. **Attempt 2, wrong:** add a visual hint by highlighting or pulsing the target, or reduce the choices (for example, grey out one distractor).
3. **Attempt 3, wrong:** model the answer ("Regarde !"). The character or a hand points to or moves the correct item. Then invite the child to touch it ("À toi !").
4. **Accept the modeled touch** and praise the specific action ("Tu as touché le chien !"). Count this internally as *assisted* for adaptation purposes, but never show it as failure.

Other rules:
- The exact number of tries (3) is a design choice, not evidence-based. A smaller ladder (2 attempts, then modeling) may be better for 2-year-olds.
- **Make every required touch meaningful:** tap the named object, not "anywhere". Avoid hotspots unrelated to the goal (see section 5).
- **Frustration signs to watch for** (practitioner inference, not sourced):
  - rapid random tapping, or repeated taps on the same wrong item;
  - long inactivity or looking away;
  - two consecutive modeled answers.
  - Response: lower the difficulty at once, offer an easy "sure win", or end the session gracefully.

### Gaps
- No empirical source was found for an optimal number of attempts, or for when to reveal the answer, at ages 2–4 in apps.
- No validated, sourced list of toddler frustration signs was found for digital contexts.
- The original Wood/Bruner/Ross paper and the replication report were not read directly.

## 4. Adaptive difficulty for preschoolers (usefulness, signals, target success rate, evidence from preschool math apps)

### Takeaway
Software built around developmental **learning trajectories** (Building Blocks, ages 3–4) and **self-paced, progressive** maths apps (onebillion, ages about 4–6) have positive RCT evidence. However, the sources I could retrieve do not isolate adaptivity as the active ingredient. The often-quoted "85% success" figure comes from machine-learning theory and simple two-choice tasks, not from preschool data. For a 3-year-old, the practical target should be **high success** with slow, accuracy-plus-help-based adaptation.

### Cited Findings
- **Outhwaite, Faulder, Gulliford & Pitchford 2019 (J. Educational Psychology 111(2), 284–298) [Strong, pupil-level RCT; Secondary]:**
  - Over 12 weeks, children using onebillion maths apps, either as a supplement or instead of a small-group teacher-led maths activity, made significantly greater maths gains than children receiving standard practice. The two implementations did not differ from each other.
  - Population: children in the first year of formal schooling in the UK (about 4–5), **not 3-year-olds**.
  - — [Nottingham ePrints](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/52319); [Imagine Worldwide summary](https://imagineworldwide.org/resource/raising-early-achievement-in-math-with-interactive-apps-a-randomized-control-trial)
- **EEF onebillion trial [Secondary]:** a separate EEF-funded trial of about 1,200 children aged 5–6 in 120 English schools was announced. I did not find its final results. — [University of Nottingham press release](https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/news/pressreleases/2017/june/addition-of-new-maths-app-equals-better-learning-for-children.aspx); [onebillion evidence page](https://onebillion.org/impact/evidence)
- **Building Blocks (Clements & Sarama) [Strong for the overall programme; adaptivity not isolated]:**
  - Programme: delivered by teachers in short daily sessions to 3–4-year-olds, mostly from disadvantaged backgrounds, through software plus print materials, organized around learning trajectories (levels that build on children's natural ways of thinking).
  - Evidence: a large cluster RCT and later analyses show gains that persist into elementary school.
  - — [Foundations guidebook info sheet](https://foundations.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/guidebook-intervention-info-sheet-building-blocks-and-triad.pdf); [Successful STEM Education](https://successfulstemeducation.org/node/1131); [IES WWC study entry](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/77947)
  - A publisher white paper describes adaptive systems in general as giving content based on the student's current level. It is not a description of the Building Blocks engine itself [Weak, commercial]. — [McGraw Hill white paper](https://www.mheducation.com/unitas/school/explore/sites/number-worlds/number-worlds-white-paper-adaptive-learning-building-blocks.pdf)
- **Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen 2019 (Nature Communications 10, 4646) [Weak for children; theoretical]:**
  - For gradient-descent learners, training is fastest at about 85% accuracy (about 15.87% error). This was shown in artificial neural networks and a perceptual-learning model on two-choice tasks.
  - Wilson declined to extend the rule to school grades.
  - — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/)

### Inferences
Concrete adaptation rules for a 3-year-old.
- **Adapt slowly, using a few simple signals:**
  - first-try accuracy;
  - which help level was needed (hint or model);
  - non-response or time-outs;
  - random-tapping bursts.
  - Response time on its own is unreliable at this age because processing speed varies widely (see the section 2 late-talker study).
- **Target success:** aim for roughly 80–90% *independent* first-or-second-try success, and close to 100% *eventual* success with help. The 85% figure is a heuristic here, not preschool evidence.
- **Step up** only after a short streak of independent successes, for example 4 of the last 5. **Step down** at once after 2 consecutive modeled answers or signs of frustration. Step sizes should be tiny:
  - one more distractor;
  - one larger quantity;
  - a slightly less salient contrast.
- **Order levels by a developmental trajectory**, in the Building Blocks style, rather than by arbitrary "difficulty", for example matching → naming → counting small sets → comparing.
- **Start each session one level below** the last level reached, so the first items are an easy warm-up.
- The parent area should show adaptation decisions in plain language, consistent with the existing app.

### Gaps
- I found no preschool-specific (age-3) RCT of the onebillion apps. The user's reference to a "Maths 3–5" app could not be confirmed. The Outhwaite trials found involve children aged about 4–6.
- I found no study that compares adaptive and fixed-sequence versions of the same app with 2–4-year-olds.
- I found no empirical optimal success rate for preschool learners.
- The Building Blocks software's actual feedback and adaptation mechanics (number of tries, hint sequence) were not retrieved from primary sources.

## 5. Sounds, animation, celebration and ending a session (overstimulation, attention, consistent celebrations, natural endings)

### Takeaway
For young children, extra interactive and visual features that are not tied to the learning goal measurably **hurt learning and attention**. Hotspots and games in e-books distracted children, and heavily decorated rooms raised off-task time and lowered test scores in kindergartners. Celebrations should be **brief, consistent, goal-linked and calm**. Sessions should end on a success, with a predictable closing ritual.

### Cited Findings
- **Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015 (Review of Educational Research) meta-analysis [Strong]:**
  - Interactive elements in technology-enhanced storybooks (hotspots, games, dictionaries) were distracting, while multimedia features aligned with the story (animation and sound that illustrate the text) helped.
  - Interactive features were especially detrimental, and multimedia features especially helpful, for children from less stimulating home environments.
  - In a sample of popular e-books, about 75% had hotspots and 65% game-like activities, but only about 20% of hotspots and a quarter of games related to the story.
  - — [SAGE PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989); [OpenDevEd record](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/NDJ8IRSS)
- **Bus, Takacs & Kegel 2015 (Developmental Review) [Moderate, narrative review]:** this review proposed that interactive features distract young children and reduce learning. The meta-analysis above tested and supported the idea. — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2872126/view)
- **Fisher, Godwin & Seltman 2014 (Psychological Science) [Moderate, experiment, n=24 kindergartners]:**
  - In a lab classroom over six lessons, a highly decorated room meant more off-task time (38.6% vs 28.4%) and lower test accuracy (42% vs 55%) than a sparse room.
  - The authors caution against over-generalizing to real classrooms. A 2022 follow-up found that habituation reduced but did not remove the effect.
  - — [Carnegie Mellon News](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2014/may/may27_decoratedclassrooms.html); [APS press release](https://psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/heavily-decorated-classrooms-disrupt-attention-and-learning-in-young-children.html/comment-page-1)
- **Kirkorian et al. 2016 [Moderate–Strong]:** interactivity helps toddlers only when it directs attention to the relevant content, which is consistent with "goal-linked effects only". — [Ko Eun Choi](https://koeunchoi.github.io/publication/2016-03-28-word-learning-touchscreens)
- **Harvard serve and return, step 5 "Practise endings and beginnings" [Weak, guidance]:** let the child signal when they are done and lead the move to something new. — [Better Care Network / Harvard](https://bettercarenetwork.org/library/principles-of-good-care-practices/child-development/5-steps-for-brain-building-serve-and-return)
- **Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015 [Weak–Moderate, review]:** "engaged" learning means engaged with the learning content, not distracted by peripheral features. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)

### Inferences
Concrete rules for celebration and endings.
- **Per-item celebration:**
  - one short, consistent, positive sound (well under 1–2 s, soft, not a fanfare);
  - a small animation *on the target object itself*, such as the found animal doing a little hop;
  - the spoken specific praise.
  - No full-screen confetti after every item, no flashing, no animation unrelated to the goal.
- **Larger celebration:** only at the natural end of a short set, for example after 5–8 items. Keep it the same each time so it becomes a predictable ritual. It stays calm and still short.
- **Screen and sound:**
  - Keep the scene visually sparse. Within the app's 1920×1080 fixed stage, few decorative elements and no idle animated distractors near the answer area.
  - No background music during instructions or choices; at most a very soft loop between activities, and preferably none.
  - No hotspots that do something unrelated to the current task.
- **Session length and ending:**
  - Plan short sessions of a few minutes and a small number of items.
  - End on a success: if the last item needed modeling, add one very easy item first.
  - Close with a fixed goodbye ritual: the character recaps one specific thing the child did, says goodbye, and the scene goes quiet. No "one more?" prompt, autoplay of the next activity, cliff-hangers or countdown pressure.
  - Allow the child (or parent) to stop at any time with no penalty or loss message.
- **Effect of rule 9:** the project's no-sound testing rule (CLAUDE.md rule 9) does not affect the design, but tests should verify that the celebration audio has a short, fixed duration and that no music plays over instructions.

### Gaps
- I found no study that quantifies an optimal celebration duration or sound level for 3-year-olds in apps.
