# Development science (ages 2–4) and activity types for a 3-year-old French (Québec) learning mode

Evidence-strength legend used below: **[Strong]** = official norms, meta-analysis or several converging peer-reviewed studies; **[Moderate]** = single peer-reviewed studies or official guidance without direct outcome data; **[Weak]** = secondary summaries, parenting sites, or a search-result snippet not checked against the full text. Items marked "(snippet)" come from search-result summaries, not a full read of the source.

## 1. Developmental milestones around 30–48 months

### Takeaway
At 30 months a typical child says ~50 words, combines two words, points to a named picture/colour and follows two-step instructions; by 36 months speech is mostly intelligible to strangers and the child asks who/what/where/why questions; by 48 months the child uses 4+ word sentences, names a few colours, recites bits of rhymes and tells what comes next in a known story. Number sense at 3 is mostly small-set recognition (subitizing 1–3, then up to 4 between ages 3 and 4) with counting words and one-to-one correspondence still being assembled; phonological awareness (rhyme) only begins to emerge between 3 and 4.

### Cited Findings
**CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early." (2022 revision) — method** [Strong]
- The 2022 revision targets skills that ~75% of children show at a given age (previously the 50th percentile/average age), cut the number of milestones and removed duplicates; so each listed item is something most children already do, and absence is a reason to talk to a clinician, not a teaching target. — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/series/early-developmental-milestones); [AAFP editorial](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2022/1000/editorial-cdc-developmental-milestone-checklist.html)

**CDC 30 months** — [CDC 30 mo](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-30mo.html) [Strong]
- Language: says about 50 words; says two or more words together with one action word ("Doggie run"); names things in a book when an adult points and asks "What is this?"; uses "I", "me", "we".
- Cognitive: uses objects to pretend (feeds a block to a doll); simple problem solving (stands on a stool to reach); **follows two-step instructions** ("Put the toy down and close the door"); **shows he knows at least one colour, e.g. points to a red crayon when asked "Which one is red?"** (note: the CDC milestone itself is a *hear-and-point* format).
- Movement: twists doorknobs/unscrews lids; jumps with both feet; **turns book pages one at a time**.
- Social: plays next to other children; "Look at me!"; follows simple routines (clean-up time).

**CDC 3 years** — [CDC 3 yr](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-3yr.html) [Strong]
- Language: conversation with at least two back-and-forth exchanges; asks who/what/where/why; **says what action is happening in a picture or book ("running", "eating")**; says first name; talks well enough for others to understand most of the time.
- Cognitive: draws a circle when shown; avoids hot objects when warned.
- Fine motor/self-care: **strings large beads or macaroni**; puts on some clothes; uses a fork.
- Social: calms within 10 minutes after caregiver leaves; joins other children to play.

**CDC 4 years** — [CDC 4 yr](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-4yr.html) [Strong]
- Language: sentences of 4+ words; says some words from a song, story or nursery rhyme; talks about one thing from the day; answers "What is a coat for?".
- Cognitive: **names a few colours of items**; **tells what comes next in a well-known story**; draws a person with 3+ body parts.
- Motor: catches a large ball; pours water with supervision; unbuttons some buttons; **holds a crayon between fingers and thumb (not a fist)**.
- Social: pretends to be something else; comforts others; "likes to be a helper"; changes behaviour by setting.
- Note: in the CDC sequence, *pointing to* one colour is a 30-month item while *naming* a few colours is a 48-month item — receptive precedes expressive by ~1.5 years for colour. — [CDC 30 mo](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-30mo.html); [CDC 4 yr](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-4yr.html)

**Québec source: Naître et grandir (3–5 years, reviewed by speech-language pathologists, updated Aug 2024)** [Moderate]
- 3–4 years: short (often imperfect) sentences, sentences of 4+ words, starts using "because/if/when"; follows more detailed instructions; grasps abstract ideas like "next door", "later"; knows rainbow colours but still struggles with time; strangers understand most of the time; "th" and clusters (bl, sp) still hard. — [Naître et grandir](https://naitreetgrandir.com/en/step/3-5-years/reading-language/language-development-in-preschoolers)
- 4–5 years: follows instructions with complex spatial terms ("Go behind the chair"); ~90% intelligible to strangers. — [Naître et grandir](https://naitreetgrandir.com/en/step/3-5-years/reading-language/language-development-in-preschoolers)
- Tips: introduce new words with simple definitions, actions, pictures or comparisons; avoid baby talk; narrate; **sort objects into categories and name the groups**; ask why/when/how questions; **"From age 4, explore rhymes and word sounds through books and songs."** — [Naître et grandir](https://naitreetgrandir.com/en/step/3-5-years/reading-language/language-development-in-preschoolers)
- A Québec CISSS handout: at 2½–3 years the child uses small words "le/la/les", "pour/à/avec/dans"; at 3½–4½ longer sentences with "pis", "parce que", "après", "qui". — [CISSS Chaudière-Appalaches, 3–4 ans](https://www.cisssca.com/clients/CISSSCA/Services_offerts/Famille_enfance_jeunesse/Enfants_4_ans/3-4_ans.pdf) (snippet) [Weak–Moderate]
- Vocabulary-size figures at 3 years vary widely across French sources (200–300 words vs. ~1000 words). — [pass-education.fr and others, search summary](https://www.pass-education.fr/developpement-langage-3-6-ans/) [Weak; conflicting]

**Phonological awareness** [Moderate]
- Rhyme awareness (judging/producing rhymes) typically first appears around 3–4 years; Lonigan, Burgess & Anthony (2000) found PA develops significantly between 3 and 4; some children as young as 3;3 recite nursery rhymes (MacLean et al., 1987). — [ASHA CICSD paper](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/cicsd_30_S_41) (snippet)
- Usual sequence: syllable → onset-rime → phoneme awareness; children detect similar vs. dissimilar-sounding words before they can manipulate sounds; the strict order is debated and PA behaves like a single ability in preschool. French-speaking children reach syllable awareness more slowly than Turkish/Greek/Italian speakers (Cossu et al.). — [ASHA CICSD paper](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/cicsd_30_S_41); [Anthony & Lonigan summary](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/860438413) (snippets)
- NELP (2008): six precursors with strong, consistent relations to later literacy — alphabet knowledge, phonological awareness, rapid naming of letters/digits, rapid naming of objects/colours, name writing, phonological memory. — [Florida DOE summary of NELP](https://info.fldoe.org/docushare/dsweb/Get/Rendition-13116/index.htm); [Teale et al. 2010](https://www.readinghalloffame.org/sites/default/files/Teale_et_al_EdRsrchr_NELP_2010.pdf) [Strong for correlations]
- Caveat: critics warn "There is less certainty that teaching these variables early on will result in later achievement improvement." — [Teale, Hoffman & Paciga 2010](https://www.readinghalloffame.org/sites/default/files/Teale_et_al_EdRsrchr_NELP_2010.pdf)

**Early numeracy** [Moderate–Strong]
- IES/REL subitizing progression (based on Clements & Sarama learning trajectories) spans ages 2–6; the band "Instantly labels sets up to 4 (perceptual)" runs from about age 3 to 4; conceptual subitizing (seeing 2+2 as 4) comes later. — [IES TMYC subitizing progression](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/rel/math-young-children/pdf/TMYC_Mod1_Subitizing_Developmental_Progression.pdf)
- Children use subitizing to build cardinality when learning to count; an early level of the Baroody–Clements–Sarama hypothetical progression is subitizing-based number recognition and set creation *before* understanding that a count gives the total; the cardinality principle comes later. — [Baroody, Clements & Sarama, NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10535904) (snippet)
- Counting milestones in order: reciting number words → connecting words to objects (one-to-one correspondence) → cardinal principle (last word = how many), "the final major milestone" of early counting; Give-N tasks with manipulatives reveal it. — [OISE Robertson Program blog](https://www.oise.utoronto.ca/robertson/blog/how-do-children-learn-numbers-2025-10-08) [Moderate]
- At 2–2.5 years a child usually learns that "one" means a set of one. — [TaleAfrica review](https://dev.taleafrica.com/?p=4402) [Weak]
- Correct enumeration alone does not show the child understands cardinality. — [Stanford pre-K math doc](https://prek-math-te.stanford.edu/system/files/media/document/2019/Expanded%20Version%20What%20Young%20Children%20Operations.pdf)
- Age of acquiring cardinality strongly predicts later number knowledge; 3-year-olds in childcare are an understudied group. — [IJMTL article](https://cimt.org.uk/ijmtl/index.php/IJMTL/article/download/386/109/1653) (snippet)

**Executive function (Diamond)** [Strong]
- Core EFs: inhibitory control, working memory, cognitive flexibility; higher-order EFs (planning, reasoning) build on them; flexibility builds on WM + inhibition; WM and inhibition each independently predict math and reading; inhibitory control is the greatest challenge for young children. — [Diamond 2012/2013 Annual Review](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/executive-functions/); [Diamond 2020 chapter](https://mail.devcogneuro.com/Publications/Diamond_2020_executive_functions.pdf); [Diamond MAS 2017 slides](https://cehd.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/Diamond-MAS-2017.pdf)

### Inferences
- A 3-year-old mode should be **receptive-first** (tap/point to what was heard) for colours, numbers and vocabulary; expressive naming of colours is a 4-year-old milestone.
- Number targets for age 3: subitizing 1–3 (stretch to 4), matching quantities, "one / two / many", counting 1–3 with one object lit at a time; do not grade the child on "how many?" answers that depend on the cardinal principle.
- Phonological awareness at 3 should be exposure (rhymes, songs, syllable clapping) not tested rhyme judgement; rhyme judgement belongs near 4.
- Two-step instructions are a 30-month milestone, so short two-step audio instructions are reasonable at 3.

### Gaps
- Could not read CDC 2- and 5-year pages in this session (only 30 mo, 3 yr, 4 yr).
- No reliable Québec-French vocabulary-size norm at 36 months; ÉVIP norms have been questioned for Québec children (see section 6).
- Shapes, patterns and sorting milestones at 3 were not found in an official age-norm source in this session (Clements & Sarama shape/pattern trajectories not fetched).

## 2. Activity types with evidence for this age

### Takeaway
The best-supported formats for 2–4-year-olds are adult-child interactive (dialogic) reading for vocabulary, playful code-focused activities (rhymes, songs, syllables) for phonological awareness, game-like number activities built on subitizing and small sets, and movement/song/pretend games for executive function. Screen-based learning works best when it is goal-directed, active, meaningful and socially shared; toddlers learn words less well from screens than from people (video deficit), and tapping interactivity can help or hurt depending on age and design.

### Cited Findings
**Reading and vocabulary**
- WWC review of practices for 3–5-year-olds: positive results for phonological awareness training and for interactive and dialogic reading. — [Reading Rockets summary of WWC](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/preschool-and-child-care/articles/preschool-language-and-literacy-practices) [Strong]
- Pillinger & Vardy (2022) systematic review (ages 2–5.5, mostly low-income): dialogic reading had significant effects on receptive and expressive vocabulary, novel vocabulary and verbal fluency, but no discernible effect on phonological processing. — [Reach Out and Read listing](https://reachoutandread.org/pillinger_2022_a-story-so-far-a-systematic-review-of-the-dialogic-reading-literature) [Strong]
- NELP: shared reading had a moderate impact on oral language and print awareness but not on phonological awareness or alphabet knowledge; code-focused interventions had large effects on PA. — [NELP 2007 draft summary](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080916004545/http://nifl.gov/nifl/NELP_2007.pdf) [Strong; wording from draft]
- Mol et al. (2008) meta-analysis compared dialogic vs. typical shared reading (expressive vocabulary subset: 9 studies, 322 children); effect size not retrieved. — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2899524/view) (snippet)
- Quality caveat: a WWC review of 59 dialogic-reading studies with children with disabilities found 57 did not meet evidence standards. — [AJSLP 2026](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/pdf/10.1044/2026_AJSLP-25-00477?download=true) (snippet) [Weak until verified]
- Repeated reading of the same stories promotes word learning ("Get the story straight: contextual repetition promotes word learning from storybooks", Horst, Parsons & Bryan 2011). — [Frontiers in Psychology 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00017/pdf) [Moderate; title-level only]

**Executive function (Harvard Center on the Developing Child, activity guide 2014)** [Moderate — expert guidance, not outcome trials]
- 3–5 years: **imaginary play** (children set rules for roles, hold ideas in mind, inhibit off-role impulses; realistic props for younger children); **storytelling** (dictate/write down the child's stories, group stories, act them out, tell stories in different languages); **movement challenges, songs and games** (obstacle courses, balance beam, fast/slow music, freeze dance, songs with repeating words/motions, cumulative songs, backward-counting songs). — [Harvard GSE "The Art of Control"](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/14/10/art-control); [Harvard 3–5 PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-3-to-5-year-olds.pdf)
- **Matching and sorting games** can be made harder by asking children to sort by different rules (cognitive flexibility). — [Harvard activities guide](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/handouts-tools/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills/) (snippet)
- The guide is 16 pages with separate chapters for 6–18 mo, 18–36 mo, 3–5 yrs, etc. — [Harvard activities guide page](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/handouts-tools/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills/)

**Numeracy**
- Learning-trajectory approach: subitizing is foundational for counting and cardinality, unitizing and arithmetic. — [IES TMYC subitizing](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/rel/math-young-children/pdf/TMYC_Mod1_Subitizing_Developmental_Progression.pdf); [Clements & Sarama book page](https://www.routledge.com/Learning-and-Teaching-EarlyMath-The-Learning-Trajectories-Approach/Clements-Sarama/p/book/9780367521974) [Strong]
- Many children include math ideas in free play without prompting; play-based learning supports math. — [Encyclopédie sur le développement des jeunes enfants, "Jouer pour apprendre les mathématiques"](https://www.enfant-encyclopedie.com/sites/default/files/textes-experts/fr/4997/jouer-pour-apprendre-les-mathematiques.pdf) (snippet) [Moderate]

**Screens and apps**
- Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*): four pillars — **active** (minds-on), **engaged** (not distracted), **meaningful** (connected to the child's life), **socially interactive** — within a **supported learning goal**; without a learning goal an app is entertaining, not educational. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html); [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps/) [Strong (review)]
- Kirkorian, Choi & Pempek (2016, 116 children 24–36 mo): touch-contingent video that required tapping a specific spot helped younger 2-year-olds learn words but **disrupted learning in slightly older children** who could already learn from passive video. — search summary of [Frontiers mini-review 2016](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01078/full) / [PMC4947576](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4947576) [Moderate; exact host URL not verified]
- PLOS ONE 2020 (2–4-year-olds, tablet): children who chose which objects to learn did *worse* than yoked peers shown the same items passively ("Word learning from a tablet app: Toddlers perform better in a passive context"). — [PLOS ONE 2020](https://dx.doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0240519) [Moderate]
- 24–30-month-olds learned novel verbs from live video chat as well as from in-person interaction, but showed no learning from non-interactive video; transfer from screen to the real world is the central difficulty for young children. — search summary, [Frontiers mini-review 2016](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01078/full) [Moderate]
- Picture realism: 15–18-month-olds transfer labels from picture books to real objects more reliably with realistic photographs than cartoons (Ganea, Pickard & DeLoache 2008); Simcock & DeLoache (2006) reported imitation from photos at 18 mo but from colour drawings only by 24 mo and line drawings by 30 mo; book gimmicks such as flaps may hinder learning (Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018 review). — [Ganea et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2865246); [Strouse et al. 2018, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00050/pdf) [Strong for under-2s; Simcock age figures via a secondary source, Weak]

### Inferences
- Digital activities that fit the evidence at 3: short goal-directed audio→tap tasks (vocabulary, colours, small numbers), rhyme/song listening with a shared adult, "same/different" and matching, 2–3 item sorting with a rule switch, simple sequencing of a known story (what comes next), freeze-style "tap only when you hear X" inhibition games.
- Avoid: free-exploration "tap anything and it animates" screens, flaps/hotspots unrelated to the learning goal, and reward animations that compete with the target (Hirsh-Pasek "engaged, not distracted").
- Design the app to be co-used: a parent prompt ("Ask her: which one did you like?") converts a solo screen into the socially interactive pillar.
- Because tapping a specific location may disrupt learning in older 2–3-year-olds, keep the tap *meaningful* (the answer), not a gate that must be tapped to advance the narration.

### Gaps
- Could not extract the text of the Harvard 18–36-month and 3–5-year PDFs (binary PDF, no extractor installed); "quieting" and "cooking/puzzles" items that I recall from the guide are unverified here.
- No effect sizes retrieved for Mol et al. 2008 or for Clements & Sarama's Building Blocks RCTs.
- No source found on patterns (AB repeats) at exactly age 3.

## 3. Québec preschool and childcare frameworks

### Takeaway
Québec has two official frameworks touching ages 3–5: the Ministère de la Famille's childcare program *Accueillir la petite enfance* (2019, for CPE/garderies/milieu familial, ages 0–5) organised in four developmental domains, and the Ministère de l'Éducation's *Programme-cycle de l'éducation préscolaire* (2021, maternelle 4 ans and 5 ans) organised in five domains. Both are holistic and play-based; neither sets individual academic targets for 3-year-olds. Passe-Partout is a school-board transition program for 4-year-olds and their parents.

### Cited Findings
- *Accueillir la petite enfance* (2019) is the ministry's educational program for childcare services and home childcare, a shared working tool for educators; many CPEs adapt it into their own programs. — [MFA programme PDF (now redirects)](https://www.mfa.gouv.qc.ca/fr/publication/Documents/programme_educatif.pdf); [CPE HEC program](https://www.hec.ca/sites/default/files/2026-08/ProgrammeEducatifCPEHEC.pdf) [Strong (official) / content via snippet]
- Its four domains — physique et moteur, social et affectif, cognitif, langagier — are described as influencing one another. — [Info-Qualité vol. 3 no 2, automne 2019](https://www.mfa.gouv.qc.ca/fr/publication/Documents/info-qualite-vol3no2-automne2019.pdf) (snippet)
- *Programme-cycle de l'éducation préscolaire* (2021) covers maternelle 4 ans and 5 ans; five domains: physique et moteur, affectif, social, langagier, cognitif, given equal weight and addressed together; each domain has competencies, axes and components; end-of-cycle expectations are targets for the group, not individual requirements; implemented in all schools from 2021. — [PFEQ programme préscolaire 2021 (CSSDM copy)](https://st-albert-le-grand.cssdm.gouv.qc.ca/files/PFEQ_programme-prescolaire2021.pdf); [CSSDM domaines](https://cybersavoir.cssdm.gouv.qc.ca/edupresco/documents-legaux/domaines-de-developpement); [Québec.ca announcement](https://www.quebec.ca/nouvelles/actualites/details/implantation-du-nouveau-programme-de-leducation-pour-la-maternelle-des-la-rentree-2021/) [Strong]
- Passe-Partout: free, for 4-year-olds (4 by 30 Sept) and their parents; 18 meetings of 2 hours Sept–May, in three strands (parents only, parent-child, children only); child goals: initiate to group life, familiarise with school, foster autonomy and self-confidence; created in the 1970s for disadvantaged areas, now open to all families. — [CSSHL dépliant 2025](https://csshl.gouv.qc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/RE_Depliant-passe-partout_septembre-2025.pdf); [Université Laval news 2025](https://nouvelles.ulaval.ca/2025/12/11/passe-partout-un-programme-prescolaire-qui-change-des-trajectoires-2ff45d0d-3193-475b-81c2-70678bfbe8a9) [Moderate]
- A Québec government study reported that Passe-Partout participants had slightly lower average kindergarten-transition scores than non-participants (likely selection: the program historically targets disadvantaged families). — [Étude programme Passe-Partout](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/recherches/Etude-programme-passe-partout.pdf) (snippet; selection interpretation is mine) [Weak]

### Inferences
- A 3-year-old mode can align its tags to the *Accueillir la petite enfance* domains (langagier, cognitif, physique et moteur, social et affectif) rather than to the PFEQ, since 3-year-olds are in childcare, not school; add the PFEQ five-domain labels only for a later 4-year mode.
- The Passe-Partout model (parent-child sessions plus parent coaching) supports an app design where the parent area gives short "à faire ensemble" activity suggestions.

### Gaps
- Could not read the full *Accueillir la petite enfance* text (the MFA URL now redirects to a Québec.ca migration page). The commonly cited principles (child as first agent of his development, learning through play, global and integrated development, each child is unique, educator-parent partnership) and its sub-components (e.g., "éveil à la lecture et à l'écriture", "éveil aux mathématiques") are **unverified in this session**.
- No official Québec stance on screen use in childcare was found.

## 4. What should stay offline/physical, and how an app can point families to it

### Takeaway
Canadian paediatric guidance limits routine screen time to under 1 hour/day for ages 2–5, and the strongest-evidence activities at this age (shared/dialogic reading, pretend play, movement and song games, manipulative counting, fine-motor tasks) are physical and social. An app should be a short, goal-directed supplement that hands off to offline activities.

### Cited Findings
- Canadian Paediatric Society: limit routine/regular screen time to less than 1 hour/day for ages 2–5; no screen time under 2; avoid screens at least 1 hour before bed; "4 Ms": minimize, mitigate, be mindful, model. — [CPS clinician tool](https://cps.ca/uploads/tools/CPS_ScreenTime-EN.pdf) [Strong (official)]; one news report claimed the 1-hour limit was dropped, contradicted by the CPS document and other coverage. — [CBC 2022](https://amp.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.6662929)
- Dialogic reading effects come from adult-child conversation during reading. — [Reading Rockets/WWC](https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/preschool-and-child-care/articles/preschool-language-and-literacy-practices)
- Harvard EF activities for 3–5 are almost all physical/social (pretend play with props, storytelling and acting out, obstacle courses, balance beam, freeze dance, song games). — [Harvard GSE](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/14/10/art-control)
- CDC fine-motor milestones (stringing beads, drawing a circle, crayon grip, unbuttoning, pouring) need real objects. — [CDC 3 yr](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-3yr.html); [CDC 4 yr](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-4yr.html)
- Give-N counting with manipulatives is how adults observe one-to-one correspondence. — [OISE Robertson blog](https://www.oise.utoronto.ca/robertson/blog/how-do-children-learn-numbers-2025-10-08)
- Naître et grandir recommends narrating daily activities, pretend play, sorting real objects into named categories, jokes. — [Naître et grandir](https://naitreetgrandir.com/en/step/3-5-years/reading-language/language-development-in-preschoolers)
- Young children struggle to transfer screen learning to the real world; live (video-chat) social interaction restores word learning that passive video does not provide. — [Frontiers mini-review 2016](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01078/full) (search summary)

### Inferences
- Keep offline: shared reading, pretend play, tracing/drawing/pencil grip, beads/puzzles/play-dough, counting real objects (snacks, stairs), outdoor play and movement games, songs with gestures.
- App hand-off ideas: after a colour game, a parent card "Cherchez trois choses rouges dans la maison"; after an animal-sound game, "Imitez les animaux en marchant comme eux"; after counting 1–3, "Comptez les marches ensemble"; a weekly "comptine à chanter" card. Cards in the parent area, not shown as instructions to the child.
- Session cap well under the CPS hour; the app could show the parent the day's minutes.

### Gaps
- No direct trial comparing an app-plus-offline-cards design with app alone for 3-year-olds was found.

## 5. Attention span and suitable durations at 3

### Takeaway
There is no peer-reviewed "minutes of attention" norm for 3-year-olds; sustained attention depends heavily on whether the activity is self-chosen and engaging. Popular estimates (6–15 minutes) are not research norms. Lab evidence shows 3-year-olds look away from computer tasks more and often fail to finish long computerised tasks.

### Cited Findings
- In a computerised selective sustained-attention task with 3–5-year-olds, half of children under 4 could not complete the task; 3-year-olds made more errors and looked away from the screen significantly more than 4–5-year-olds. — [ERIC EJ1006825](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_eric_primary_EJ1006825) (snippet) [Moderate]
- Children attended longer when given more choice (DiCarlo et al., 2016, 63 preschoolers); a 2025 study of 93 3-year-olds compared child-chosen, adult-chosen and adult-presentation conditions. — [Early Childhood Education Journal 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10643-025-01985-w); [LSU repository](https://repository.lsu.edu/education_pubs/38) (snippets) [Moderate]
- Popular figures: 6–8 min, 6–9 min on a structured task, 10–15 min on an engaging task. — [Happiest Baby](https://secure.happiestbaby.com/blogs/toddler/attention-span); [earlyyears.tv](https://www.earlyyears.tv/?p=535830) [Weak; conflicting]
- PPVT-5 CDN-F (picture-pointing vocabulary test) administration takes ~10–15 minutes from age 2;6 with an examiner present. — [Pearson Canada flyer](https://www.pearsonclinical.ca/content/dam/school/global/clinical/ca/assets/evt-3/PPVT5_EVT3-Cdn-FR-Flyer-can.pdf) [Moderate]

### Inferences
- Plan activities of ~1–3 minutes (5–8 rounds), sessions of ~5–10 minutes, with a natural ending and an easy stop at any time; let the child pick between 2 activities (choice lengthens attention).
- Detect disengagement (no tap for N seconds) and offer a gentle end rather than prompting repeatedly.

### Gaps
- Full results of the 2025 and DiCarlo studies (actual durations) not retrieved.

## 6. The "hear a word → point to the picture" receptive-vocabulary format

### Takeaway
Picture-pointing to a spoken word is the standard way to measure receptive vocabulary from about 30 months (PPVT/ÉVIP use 4 pictures from age 2;6; touchscreen tasks for toddlers use 2 pictures), and the CDC's own 30-month colour milestone is phrased this way, so the format fits a 3-year-old well. Start with 2 choices, move to 3, and reach 4 only for well-known words; use realistic images, vary the exemplars across rounds for generalisation, and choose distractors deliberately.

### Cited Findings
**Receptive vs. expressive**
- CDC: at 30 months the child *points to* a red crayon when asked "Which one is red?"; *naming* a few colours is listed at 4 years. — [CDC 30 mo](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-30mo.html); [CDC 4 yr](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-4yr.html) [Strong]
- At 30 months the child names things in a book when an adult points and asks "What is this?"; at 3 years says what action is happening in a picture. — [CDC 30 mo](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-30mo.html); [CDC 3 yr](https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/actearly/milestones/milestones-3yr.html)

**How standard tests use picture-pointing**
- PPVT-5 (and Canadian French PPVT-5 CDN-F) measure receptive vocabulary from 2;6 by non-verbal picture designation; the CDN-F version covers 2;6–16;11 with ~10–15 min administration. — [Pearson Canada flyer](https://www.pearsonclinical.ca/content/dam/school/global/clinical/ca/assets/evt-3/PPVT5_EVT3-Cdn-FR-Flyer-can.pdf) [Strong]
- PPVT shows four colour line drawings per page, from 2½ years to adulthood. — search summary, [Styles & Plunkett 2009 / related](https://blogs.ntu.edu.sg/blip/files/2016/01/StylesPlunkett09_JCL_OpenAccess-11pvn8z.pdf) [Moderate]
- In Québec, receptive vocabulary from 30 months is often measured with the ÉVIP (Dunn, Thériault-Whalen & Dunn, 1993), but its norms have been questioned for Québec children. — [ISQ, "fascicule école bon pied" / Québec study](https://www.jesuisjeserai.stat.gouv.qc.ca/publications/fascicule_ecole_bon_pied.pdf) (snippet) [Moderate]

**Number of choices**
- Toddler touchscreen comprehension task (Computerized Comprehension Task, 16–20 months) uses **two images per trial**; reliability was high and the touch format improved attention and compliance. — [CCT reliability & validity, J. Child Language](https://www-cambridge-org.ezproxyberklee.flo.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/reliability-and-validity-of-the-computerized-comprehension-task-cct-data-from-american-english-and-mexican-spanish-infants/DCCCE59D67EB4D48D2EE620D552F4898) (snippet; proxy URL) [Moderate]
- Across 36 two-alternative experiments with 2.5–6.5-year-olds, pointing accuracy and looking strongly correlated (r = .7); pointing is the typical response from about age 3. — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6wh2s76n/qt6wh2s76n.pdf) (snippet) [Moderate]
- Two-choice paradigms are vulnerable to non-lexical strategies (e.g., side bias, picking the unfamiliar item), so they can overstate knowledge. — search summary, [PMC5501698](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5501698) [Weak]
- No head-to-head study of 2 vs. 3 vs. 4 choices for 3-year-olds was found.

**Realistic images vs. drawings**
- Toddlers transfer words from pictures to real objects better from realistic photos than cartoons; iconicity matters most for the youngest; by ~30 months line drawings work for imitation. — [Ganea et al., PMC2865246](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2865246); [Ganea, Harvard LDS PDF](https://www.harvardlds.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/Ganea-Toddlers’-referential-understanding-of-pictures.pdf); [Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00050/pdf) [Strong for <2 years; weaker evidence at 3]

**Same picture vs. varied exemplars**
- Preschoolers (with language impairment) trained with 3 *different* objects per new word showed no immediate advantage but significantly better retention 3 weeks later than those trained with 3 identical objects. — [LSHSS 2017](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2017_LSHSS-17-0031) [Moderate]
- Perry, Samuelson, Malloy & Schiffer (2010, *Psychological Science*): object variability led to better abstraction of category organisation (18-month-olds); "Learn locally, think globally: exemplar variability supports higher-order generalization and word learning". — [UEA research portal](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/learn-locally-think-globally-exemplar-variability-supports-higher/) [Moderate]
- Contextual repetition (same storybooks) helps 3-year-olds learn new words. — [Horst et al. 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00017/pdf) [Moderate]

### Inferences — design rules for the format
- **Choices:** 2 pictures for new words and the first rounds; 3 once the child is mostly correct; 4 only for consolidated words (matching the PPVT layout used from 2;6). Randomise target position to defeat side bias; never let a 2-choice task alone mark a word as "known" — require success at 3–4 choices or across exemplars.
- **Images:** realistic, single object on a plain background, consistent scale; avoid stylised cartoons for first learning; original art only (CLAUDE.md rule 5).
- **Variability:** keep the *frame* constant (same voice, same carrier phrase, same layout) and vary the *exemplar* (three different dogs, red objects of different kinds) — matches "repetition of context + variability of exemplars".
- **Distractors:** for a new word, distractors should be familiar and from a different category (chien vs. pomme) so mutual exclusivity helps; later, same-category distractors (chien vs. chat) test real meaning; for colours, keep the object constant and vary only the colour, then vary objects so colour is the only cue; avoid distractors whose names sound alike at first (e.g., *poule/boule*), use them deliberately later for listening.
- **Prompt:** a short carrier phrase, target word last and stressed: "Où est le chien?", "Touche le rouge.", replay available by tapping a speaker icon; feedback names the item again ("Oui, le chien!") and on error shows and names the right one without penalty.
- **Order of word categories for 2–4-year-olds** (rough, combining CDC items and typical early vocabulary; categories are my synthesis, see Gaps): 
  1. ~24–30 mo: familiar animals and their sounds (chien *ouaf*, chat *miaou*, vache *meuh*, canard *coin-coin*, mouton *bê*, cochon *groin-groin*, coq *cocorico*); body parts (yeux, nez, bouche, mains, pieds, oreilles, ventre); family words (maman, papa, bébé, grand-maman/grand-papa — "mamie/papi" also used); everyday objects (balle, verre, cuillère, lit, livre, chaussure/soulier); foods (pomme, banane, lait, pain, biscuit, fromage).
  2. ~30–36 mo: one colour then several (rouge, bleu, jaune, vert); clothing (manteau, tuque, mitaines, bottes, bas [Québec "socks"], chandail, pantalon); vehicles (auto, camion, autobus, avion, bateau, vélo, train); actions/verbs from pictures (CDC 3 yr: "manger", "courir", "dormir", "boire", "sauter", "jouer").
  3. ~36–42 mo: sizes (gros/petit, grand/petit), "un / deux / beaucoup", numbers 1–3 by subitizing, positions (dans, sur, sous), more colours (orange, rose, mauve, brun, noir, blanc), simple shapes (rond/cercle, carré, triangle), function questions ("Lequel sert à boire?" — CDC 4 yr "What is a coat for?").
  4. ~42–48 mo: positions derrière/devant/à côté (Naître et grandir: "behind the chair" at 4–5), quantities to 4–5, emotions (content, triste, fâché), categories (animaux, fruits), two-step instructions.
- **Audio-first variations:** "Trouve celui qui fait *meuh*" (sound → picture); real animal/vehicle sound clip → picture; "Lequel est gros?" (size); "Touche le chat qui est *dans* la boîte" (position); odd-one-out with spoken prompt "Lequel n'est pas un animal?" (closer to 4); "Touche le chien, puis le chat" (two-step); "Montre-moi deux pommes" (quantity 1–3); "Qu'est-ce qu'on mange?" (function/category); listen-and-freeze inhibition: tap only when the voice says *chien*.

### Gaps
- No study found comparing 2, 3 and 4 picture choices specifically at 36 months; the progression above is an inference from test practice (2 choices in toddler tasks, 4 in the PPVT from 2;6).
- No Québec-French norms found for the age order of specific word categories; the word lists and Québec terms (tuque, mitaines, bas, chandail, auto, souliers) and onomatopoeia (ouaf, meuh, coin-coin, bê, groin-groin, cocorico) are from general knowledge and should be checked by a Québec speech-language pathologist or against the Québec French CDI (Inventaire MacArthur-Bates du développement de la communication, adaptation québécoise), which was not retrieved in this session.
- Evidence on real photos vs. drawings is strongest for children under 2; whether realistic images still matter at 3 is less clear.
