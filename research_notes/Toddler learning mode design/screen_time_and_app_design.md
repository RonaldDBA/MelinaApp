# Screen-time guidance for children under 5 and evidence-based design for preschool learning apps (ages 2–4)

Context: notes for a French (Québec) learning mode for a 3-year-old inside a family learning app on a Windows PC (mouse and/or touch). Research date: 18 November 2026. Evidence-strength labels used below:
- **[Strong]** = consistent experimental/meta-analytic evidence or formal guideline with graded evidence.
- **[Moderate]** = several controlled studies, or official guidance built on observational evidence.
- **[Weak]** = single studies, small samples, expert opinion, industry/UX guidance, or secondary reports.

---

## 1. What official bodies recommend about screen use at 2–4 years (amount, co-use, content, timing, sleep)

### Takeaway
All four main bodies (CPS 2022, AAP 2016, WHO 2019, Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines) converge on the same ceiling: **at most about 1 hour per day of sedentary screen time for ages 2–4, "less is better"**, ideally co-viewed with an adult, high-quality and non-commercial, never in the hour before bed, and never displacing active play, sleep (10–13 h at 3–4 years) or face-to-face interaction. The numeric ceilings rest on low/very-low certainty evidence and are precautionary, but they are the current official consensus.

### Cited Findings

**Canadian Paediatric Society (CPS) — the most relevant source for a Québec family**
- Current statement: "Screen time and preschool children: Promoting health and development in a digital world", posted 24 Nov 2022, *Paediatr Child Health* 2023;28(3):184–192, lead author Michelle Ponti, CPS Digital Health Task Force. No reaffirmation date shown on the page as of Nov 2026. [Moderate] — [CPS position statement](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Under 2: screen time not recommended except video-chatting with caring adults; "There is no evidence to support introducing technology at an early age." — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Ages 2–5: limit routine or sedentary screen time to about 1 hour or less per day; routine sedentary screen time should not be part of child care. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Co-use: co-view whenever possible and stay present and engaged; shared viewing preferred over solitary use. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Content: choose educational, age-appropriate and interactive programming over passive viewing; prefer quality non-commercial sources; minimize advertising; use screens for creative activities (e.g., drawing) rather than passive viewing. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Background TV: avoid it; high exposure is linked to weaker language, cognition, executive function and less parent–child interaction; turn screens off when not in use. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Sleep/timing: avoid screens for at least 1 hour before bed (stimulating and melatonin-suppressing effects); screens in bedrooms strongly associated with less sleep. Keep daily screen-free times, especially family meals. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Apps/touch: interactive apps with timely adult responses may help toddlers learn new words; some learn-to-read apps may support early literacy; active touchscreen use has been linked to earlier fine-motor milestones; but apps have not been shown to replace live interaction, and children learn language best from live interaction with caring adults. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- For children 2 and older, quality, age-appropriate content with specific educational goals can support language, literacy and social skills, particularly for disadvantaged children; co-viewed content may aid learning but evidence is mixed. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- CPS frames counselling around the "4 Ms": **Minimize** screen time, **Mitigate** risks (be present, co-view), be **Mindful** of use, **Model** healthy habits. — [CPS clinician tool (PDF)](https://cps.ca/uploads/tools/CPS_ScreenTime-EN.pdf); [CPS Digital media and screen time hub](https://cps.ca/tools-outils/digital-media-and-screen-time)
- The 2022 advice is reported as largely unchanged from the earlier 2017 CPS statement, while acknowledging families' greater reliance on screens. (Secondary report.) — [CTV News](https://www.ctvnews.ca/mobile/sci-tech/doctors-urged-to-encourage-minimal-screen-use-for-toddlers-and-preschoolers-1.3437205)
- Separate CPS statement for 5–19 years (June 2019, "Digital media: Promoting healthy screen use in school-aged children and adolescents") sets no fixed time limit and focuses on screen use that interferes with sleep, activity and relationships — relevant to the older sibling's mode, not the 3-year-old. — [CPS digital media statement](https://www.cps.ca/en/documents/position/digital-media)

**American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP)**
- "Media and Young Minds" policy statement (Council on Communications and Media, lead author Jenny Radesky), released 21 Oct 2016, *Pediatrics* Nov 2016. Under 18 months: avoid screen media except video-chatting. 18–24 months: if introduced, choose high-quality programming and watch together. 2–5 years: limit to 1 hour/day of high-quality programs and co-view to help children understand and apply what they see. (Retrieved via secondary coverage; primary text not opened.) [Moderate] — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20161020/aap-recommends-1-hour-limit-on-digital-media-use-for-preschoolers); [EurekAlert AAP release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/695617)
- AAP Family Media Plan (launched 2016) lets families tick household rules; AAP guidance stresses that parents' own device use can pull attention from verbal and emotional engagement with children. — [Reach Out and Read summary (Moreno 2024)](https://reachoutandread.org/moreno_2024)
- AAP's family-facing guidance is described as revised toward "quality, context and conversation" rather than simple time limits; AAP runs a Center of Excellence on Social Media and Youth Mental Health (focused mostly on older children/teens). — [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/how-to-build-healthy-digital-habits-tips-for-families.aspx); [AAP Media and Children](https://aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children)

**World Health Organization (WHO), 24 April 2019** — "Guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behaviour and sleep for children under 5 years of age" (WHO/NMH/PND/2019.4)
- 1–2 years: at least 180 min of physical activity at any intensity; not restrained >1 h at a time; for 1-year-olds sedentary screen time not recommended; for 2-year-olds no more than 1 h, "less is better"; 11–14 h good-quality sleep including naps, regular sleep/wake times. — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)
- 3–4 years: at least 180 min physical activity including at least 60 min moderate-to-vigorous; not restrained >1 h at a time or sitting for extended periods; no more than 1 h sedentary screen time, "less is better"; 10–13 h sleep, may include a nap. — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)
- WHO stresses "quality sedentary time spent in interactive non-screen-based activities with a caregiver" (reading, storytelling, singing, puzzles). — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)
- Evidence strength: WHO labelled the recommendations "strong" while acknowledging they rested on "very low quality evidence" (GRADE); the UK Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health said the data were too weak to set thresholds. News reports quoted WHO's lead saying the limits target passive screen time, not an interactive game or a programme where children are encouraged to move (secondary report, wording not verified against WHO text). — [Business Standard (AFP)](https://business-standard.com/amp/article/health/screen-time-for-children-who-s-new-approach-may-do-little-to-curb-obesity-119050600198_1.html); [KTVU (AP)](https://www.ktvu.com/news/un-no-screen-time-for-babies-only-1-hour-for-kids-under-5)

**Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for the Early Years (0–4 years)** — CSEP, HALO/CHEO, University of Alberta, Public Health Agency of Canada, ParticipACTION (first released 2017)
- Toddlers (1–2): ≥180 min varied activity incl. energetic play; 11–14 h sleep incl. naps with consistent times; not restrained >1 h; screen time not recommended under 2; at 2, 1 h or less. — [CSEP Early Years guidelines](https://csepguidelines.ca/guidelines/early-years/)
- Preschoolers (3–4): ≥180 min incl. ≥60 min energetic play; 10–13 h sleep; not restrained >1 h; sedentary screen time 1 h or less. Encourages reading/storytelling with a caregiver when sedentary; replacing screen time with energetic play and indoor with outdoor time gives greater benefits. — [CSEP Early Years guidelines](https://csepguidelines.ca/guidelines/early-years/)
- Developed from best available evidence plus expert consensus and input from >600 stakeholders. — [CSEP](https://csepguidelines.ca/guidelines/early-years/)

### Inferences
- For a 3-year-old, the app is competing for a share of a **≤1 h/day total** that also covers TV, phones and any other screens. A single app mode should therefore budget well below 1 h — realistically one short session (10–20 min) per day, possibly two, so the family keeps room for other media and for non-screen play.
- The app should make co-use the default (parent present), which is the one mitigation all bodies endorse.
- An evening cutoff (no sessions within ~1 h of the child's bedtime, configurable by parents) directly implements CPS/WHO sleep guidance.
- Because the WHO lead reportedly distinguished passive viewing from interactive/movement-inviting activities, building "get up and move" or "find it in the room" prompts into the mode is consistent with the spirit of the guidelines — but the 1-hour ceiling should still be treated as applying to the app.

### Gaps
- Could not open the AAP 2016 policy text directly or confirm whether AAP has formally replaced the 2–5-year 1-hour figure; the 2024–2026 AAP guidance appears to shift emphasis to quality/context, but no new preschool policy statement was found.
- Exact CSEP release date not shown on the page (the guidelines date from 2017; no 2024–2026 revision for early years was found).
- No CPS reaffirmation or revision after 2022 was found as of Nov 2026.
- The WHO "passive screen time" clarification comes from news reports, not the guideline text.

## 2. What "high-quality, educational" means at this age; co-use (joint media engagement); the video/transfer deficit

### Takeaway
"Educational" means an app with a clear learning goal that is **active (minds-on), engaged (not distracting), meaningful (connected to the child's real life) and socially interactive** (Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015). At 2–3 years children show a **transfer deficit** — they learn less from screens than from live demonstrations and struggle to apply screen learning to real objects. Adult scaffolding, genuine contingency (the app responds to what the child does), repetition and real-world follow-up all reduce it.

### Cited Findings
- **Four pillars** (Hirsh-Pasek, Zosh, Golinkoff, Gray, Robb & Kaufman 2015, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 16(1):3–34, doi:10.1177/1529100615569721): apps support learning when they are active, engaged, meaningful and socially interactive and serve a defined learning goal; without a learning goal an app with the pillars is "entertaining rather than educational". [Moderate — narrative synthesis of learning science] — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html); [New America summary](https://newamerica.org/education-policy/edcentral/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps)
  - *Active* = mental effort, not just tapping/swiping. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)
  - *Engaged* = free of distractions; noise, motion or side games unrelated to the goal disrupt learning; a story wrapper can sustain engagement. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)
  - *Meaningful* = connected to prior knowledge/daily life (e.g., finding triangles around the house beats picking one from a lineup). — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)
  - *Socially interactive* = characters respond to the child's choices or speech, or the app prompts discussion with parents. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)
  - Learning is strongest when the app sits in a context of scaffolded exploration, questioning and discovery. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)
- **Transfer deficit**: children under 3 can learn from interactive touchscreens but still have difficulty applying new learning from the screen to the 3D world (Moser et al. 2015). [Moderate–Strong] — [Barr, McClure & Parlakian 2019, ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- CPS likewise states children under 5 have difficulty transferring screen learning to real life and learn most effectively through live face-to-face interaction. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- **Joint media engagement**: high-quality parent co-engagement (simple explanations, labelling, organising the task, praise) helped 15-month-olds transfer from touchscreen to real objects (Zack & Barr 2016). Only ~25% of parents report co-engaging during tablet use (Rideout 2017). [Moderate] — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- **Social demonstration**: 2- and 3-year-olds who watched an adult hand demonstrate a 3-piece puzzle on a tablet could complete it; those who saw a "ghost" demonstration (pieces moving by themselves) could not (Zimmermann et al. 2017). [Moderate] — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- **Contingency**: 24–30-month-olds learned verbs as well via live video chat as face-to-face (Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff 2014); 12–25-month-olds learned better from live video chat than prerecorded video (Myers et al. 2017); toddlers learned more with a responsive co-viewer (Myers et al. 2018). [Moderate] — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- Contingency interacts with age/working memory: in 134 toddlers aged 24–36 months, contingent touchscreen interaction decreased transfer in younger toddlers but increased it in older ones (Choi, Kirkorian & Pempek 2021); interactivity must be authentic (yoked/fake interactivity did not help). Evidence that interactivity always alleviates transfer difficulty is mixed. [Moderate, mixed] — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.621372/full); [PMC7943612](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7943612)
- **Scaffolding**: toddlers learned a word from video only when a parent gave verbal scaffolding (Strouse & Troseth 2014); parent verbal interaction mitigated negative effects of media on toddler language (Mendelsohn et al. 2010). — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- **Repetition** of the same content increased imitation/learning at 1–5 years and reduced the transfer deficit (Barr et al. 2007). — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- **Familiar characters**: 2-year-olds learned a math concept better from a familiar character than an unfamiliar one (Howard Gola et al. 2013). — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- **Too much interactivity hurts**: in 3–6-year-olds, a highly interactive e-book with extra hotspots led to fewer recalled story details than print (Chiong et al. 2012); a meta-analysis of 29 studies found well-constructed e-books equal to print when adults read them (Takacs, Swart & Bus 2015). Principle: "just-right" interactivity that serves the learning goal. [Moderate] — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)
- Practical advice: connect screen activities to the real world (e.g., counting napkins while setting the table); be cautious with autoplay; reduce "technoference" (children learned less when a phone call interrupted teaching, Reed et al. 2017). — [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resources/3005-maximizing-the-potential-for-learning-from-screen-experiences-in-early-childhood-what-the-research-says)

### Inferences
- Design the toddler mode as a **parent-and-child activity**, not a solo babysitter: a short spoken "conversation starter" for the parent per activity (e.g., in the parent area or on a small parent card), and pauses where the narrator invites the child to point, name or say something to the adult.
- Avoid "ghost" manipulation: when demonstrating, show a visible hand/character moving the object, and keep demonstrations slow.
- Use one stable, original, friendly guide character (familiar-character effect) — must be original content per project rule 5.
- Prefer few, meaningful interactions over many decorative hotspots; every tappable thing should relate to the goal.
- Build "off-screen bridges": end-of-session suggestions like "Trouve quelque chose de rouge dans la maison" spoken aloud, which also serve as natural stopping points.
- Allow and encourage replay of favourite activities (repetition helps at this age) rather than constantly generating novelty.

### Gaps
- Direct papers by Barr and Troseth were not opened; findings are via the Barr et al. 2019 review. No single study gives a precise age at which the transfer deficit disappears (it is generally reported to diminish across ages 3–5, but I did not find a citable source for an exact age).
- No evidence found specific to French-language or Québec preschool apps.

## 3. Concrete interaction design rules for 2–4-year-olds (audio-first, targets, gestures, mouse vs touch, text, navigation)

### Takeaway
Pre-readers need **spoken instructions paired with visual cues**, **tap as the primary gesture**, very large targets (≥2 cm), no essential drag/pinch/swipe/double-click/hover, nothing interactive at the screen edges, landscape layout, and almost no text. On a PC, a 3-year-old may not be able to use a mouse at all — touchscreen (or a parent driving the mouse) is preferable, and mouse play must be reduced to single clicks on large targets.

### Cited Findings
- **Sesame Workshop / Joan Ganz Cooney Center, "Best Practices: Designing Touch Tablet Experiences for Preschoolers" (Dec 2012, based on >50 studies)**: tap is the most intuitive interaction; pinch and flick/swipe are often difficult and should not be essential to a task; design for landscape; avoid hotspots/icons along the bottom edge where children rest wrists; pair dialogue with visual reinforcement because many preschoolers can't read; let parents turn off distracting hotspots; design for joint engagement with parent tips and progress trackers. [Weak–Moderate: industry research synthesis] — [Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=19607)
- **Nielsen Norman Group (Liu, 8 Jul 2018)**: touch targets of at least 2 cm × 2 cm for young children (vs ~1 cm for adults); tap is the simplest gesture; drag is possible on touchscreens but precise dragging is hard — offer a tap alternative (tap object, then tap destination); under 5 avoid two-handed actions and quick reactions to visual stimuli; on desktop use single clicks or simple keys and avoid dragging, scrolling and clicking small objects; one 3-year-old could not fit her hand around a mouse and did not connect cursor movement to mouse movement; many young children preferred a trackpad. [Weak: qualitative UX research] — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/)
- **Mouse pointing in preschoolers (Hourcade, Bederson, Druin & Guimbretière, ACM TOCHI 2004; UMD tech report 2003)**: 4- and 5-year-olds vs adults on point-and-click — age significantly affected accuracy, target re-entry and Fitts' index of performance; target size significantly affected accuracy and re-entry; Fitts' law models children well only for the first entry into the target (children overshoot/re-enter). No 3-year-olds were tested. [Moderate for 4–5; extrapolation to 3] — [UMD DRUM](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/f3e5dc45-7acc-40c6-8dfd-a258b9c0bc9b); [ACM DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1145/1035575.1035577)
- Older children's drag-and-drop errors are driven mainly by immature fine motor control and eye–hand coordination; one design-guidance source states children under 5 cannot use drag-and-drop effectively and recommends tap-and-tap instead. [Weak] — [Nacher et al., IJHCS (author version)](https://riunet.upv.es/bitstream/10251/64752/3/VersionAutor.pdf); [UXPA Magazine](https://oldmagazine.uxpa.org/?p=11776)
- **Instructions/prompts**: a Cooney Center-related study on when children understand embedded prompts reportedly found children under 3 could interpret instructions only when they came from an adult model (secondary report; original not verified). [Weak] — [UXPA Magazine](https://oldmagazine.uxpa.org/?p=11776)
- CPS notes active touchscreen use has been associated with earlier fine-motor milestones. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Engagement pillar: unrelated noise, motion and side activities pull children off task. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)

### Inferences (proposed rules for the toddler mode)
- **Audio-first**: every instruction spoken in Québec French by a single warm voice, short (≤ ~8 words), with a visual demonstration (animated hand/character) and a "replay instruction" button (big ear/mouth icon). Zero reliance on reading; text appears only for the parent. Note: this conflicts with project rule 9 only for tests (tests must stay muted) — in real use, audio is essential.
- **Targets**: at least 2 cm physical size. On the fixed 1920×1080 stage the physical size depends on the screen: on a 24" 1080p monitor (~0.28 mm/px) 2 cm ≈ 72 stage px, but on a 14" 1080p laptop (~0.16 mm/px) 2 cm ≈ 125 stage px. A floor of ~160 stage px per target (plus a hit-area larger than the visible shape, which helps with the overshoot/re-entry seen in preschool mouse data) covers small laptops with margin. (Arithmetic inference, not from a source.) Keep 3–4 choices max per screen at age 3.
- **Gestures**: single tap/click only. No drag required; if a "move the object" activity exists, implement tap-object-then-tap-destination, and accept a drag as a bonus. No double-click, no hover-dependent states, no right-click, no scrolling, no timed/fast reactions, no two-handed actions.
- **Mouse vs touch**: if the family PC has a touchscreen, prefer touch for this mode; with a mouse, assume the parent may drive or guide the hand (co-use), make the whole object clickable, treat mouse-down as the activation (not mouse-up after drag), and ignore accidental movement. Consider a large, visible cursor.
- **Edges**: no interactive elements in the outer margin (bottom edge especially), and the exit/parent control in a corner protected from accidental taps (e.g., press-and-hold or a parent gesture).
- **Consistency**: same layout and same guide character every session; one home "garden" screen with a few large doors; the same "back/home" affordance always in the same place.
- **Feedback**: immediate, gentle audio + visual feedback on every tap; for wrong answers, no buzzers or loss — the narrator re-models the correct answer (errorless/scaffolded learning).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing mouse vs touchscreen for 3-year-olds specifically, nor validated target sizes for mouse use at 3; recommendations above extrapolate from 4–5-year-old mouse data and touchscreen UX guidance.
- The Cooney/Sesame full PDF (gesture categorisations, visual design tips) was not read; only the summary page.
- No peer-reviewed source found on optimal spoken-instruction length for 3-year-olds.

## 4. Ads, dark patterns, infinite loops and age-appropriate design standards

### Takeaway
Manipulative design is pervasive in apps used by 3–5-year-olds (Radesky et al. 2022: 98.8% of children used at least one such app; only ~20% of apps had none). The categories to design out are **parasocial pressure, time pressure, navigation constraints and lures**, plus advertising. The UK Children's Code treats 0–5 as "pre-literate and early literacy" and asks for high-privacy defaults, no nudges toward less privacy, and parent involvement.

### Cited Findings
- **Radesky, Hiniker, McLaren et al. 2022**, "Prevalence and Characteristics of Manipulative Design in Mobile Applications Used by Children", *JAMA Network Open* 5(6):e2217641. 160 children aged 3–5 (US convenience sample), 133 unique apps (109 free, 24 paid), coded for 11 features. [Moderate: cross-sectional content analysis] — [JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
  - *Parasocial relationship pressure* (characters urge the child to keep playing or buy, e.g., a character says it is getting sleepy or asks if the child wants to give up): 24.8% of apps for gameplay, 18.8% for purchases. — [JAMA Netw Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
  - *Time pressure* (countdowns, "limited time"): 17.3% gameplay, 10.5% purchases. — [same](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
  - *Navigation constraints* (no clear exit between levels, pop-ups, auto-advancing, faint opt-out buttons): 45.9% gameplay, 36.8% purchases; 90.6% of children encountered them in gameplay. — [same](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
  - *Lures* (stickers, trophies, leaderboards, daily rewards, sparkles/pulsing buttons at decision points): 45.1% gameplay, 45.9% purchases; 93.1% of children encountered gameplay lures. — [same](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
  - Advertising: roadblock ads 21.8%, strategically timed ads 18.1%, ads rewarded with lives/items 16.5% of apps. — [same](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
  - Apps averaged 3.0 manipulative features; 19.6% had none; free apps had more (median 3.0 vs 1.0); PBS KIDS apps had none except autoplay; children from households without a college degree had higher scores (exploratory). — [same](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
  - Authors call for ethical standards and point to the UK Age-Appropriate Design Code and EU Digital Services Act. — [same](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)
- **Autoplay / infinite continuation (Hiniker, Heung, Hong & Kientz, CHI 2018, "Coco's Videos")**: field deployment with 24 families comparing three video-player designs (one video per session lock-out; a prompt to do another activity; autoplay). Autoplay made transitions away hardest and reduced children's opportunities to decide to stop; the authors argue apps can be designed to foster or undermine children's autonomy and should give parents a role. (Numbers beyond n=24 not verified.) [Weak–Moderate: small field experiment] — [UW News](https://washington.edu/news/2018/05/01/apps-for-children-should-emphasize-parent-and-child-choice-researchers-say); [UW iSchool](https://ischool.uw.edu/news/2018/05/apps-children-should-emphasize-parent-and-child-choice-researchers-say)
- Cooney Center research summary (2018): apps with natural stopping points and transition support make it easier for children to self-regulate; apps that auto-play make it harder; apps that can be interrupted and leave the user in control better support dialogue and interpersonal engagement. [Weak–Moderate] — [Cooney Center 2018](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/2018/05/21/research-suggests-well-designed-preschool-apps-can-encourage-family-engagement-and-self-regulation/)
- **UK ICO Age Appropriate Design Code (Children's Code, v2.1)**: uses 0–5 = "pre-literate and early literacy" as a developmental band; for this band recommends high-privacy-by-default architecture, nudging toward keeping high privacy or toward parent/trusted-adult involvement if a default change is attempted, and avoiding explanations (present as simple protective rules). Nudge techniques must not lead children to give more personal data or turn off privacy protections. [Regulatory code; UK only] — [ICO, 3. Age appropriate application](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/3-age-appropriate-application/); [ICO, 13. - CPS: minimize advertising, prefer non-commercial content. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)

### Inferences (proposed "never" list for the toddler mode)
- No ads, purchases, currencies, shops or unlockables (the app is already non-commercial; keep it that way).
- No parasocial pressure: the guide character never expresses sadness, sleepiness, disappointment or "don't go" when the child stops or is idle; leaving is always praised ("Bravo, à demain !").
- No timers, countdowns or speed scoring.
- No autoplay into the next activity: after each activity, return to a calm choice screen or the end screen; the child or parent chooses.
- No streaks, daily-login rewards, leaderboards, pulsing/sparkling "play more" buttons, or loss-framed messages ("you'll lose your flower").
- Always-visible, simple exit; no tunnel without an exit.
- Rewards, if any, are informational and tied to effort/learning (consistent with the app's existing "stars without currency" design noted in recent commits), not variable/random.
- Privacy: no network calls from the toddler mode; nothing about the child leaves the PC (aligns with project rule 4).

## 5. Session length, gentle time limits and a clear end ("the garden is resting")

### Takeaway
No official body specifies a per-session length for preschool apps; the only firm numbers are the ≤1 h/day sedentary-screen ceiling and "not restrained or sitting >1 h at a time". The evidence on transitions (Hiniker 2018; Cooney Center) supports **built-in natural stopping points, a predictable ritualised ending and no autoplay**, with parent-set limits rather than abrupt cut-offs.

### Cited Findings
- Daily ceiling ≤1 h sedentary screen time (2–4 y) and no restraint/sitting >1 h at a time. — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more); [CSEP](https://csepguidelines.ca/guidelines/early-years/); [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- A design that limited each session (one video per session) and one that prompted another activity were compared with autoplay; autoplay made stopping hardest and undermined children's autonomy. — [UW News on Hiniker et al. CHI 2018](https://washington.edu/news/2018/05/01/apps-for-children-should-emphasize-parent-and-child-choice-researchers-say)
- Natural stopping points and transition support help self-regulation; interruptible, user-controlled apps support dialogue. — [Cooney Center 2018](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/2018/05/21/research-suggests-well-designed-preschool-apps-can-encourage-family-engagement-and-self-regulation/)
- Avoid screens ≥1 h before bed. — [CPS](https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/screen-time-and-preschool-children)
- Parents should set household media rules (AAP Family Media Plan). — [Reach Out and Read (Moreno 2024)](https://reachoutandread.org/moreno_2024)

### Inferences (proposed mechanics)
- **Session = a fixed small number of activities** (e.g., 3–5 short rounds of ~2–3 min each, ~10–15 min total), not a timer. Ending is reached by completing the set, so the stop is predictable and never mid-task. A parent-configurable daily cap (default e.g. 15–20 min, hard maximum well under 60 min) acts as a backstop.
- **Visible progress toward the end**: e.g., 3–5 seeds to plant; when the last seed is planted, the garden "goes to sleep" (soft music, the character waves, lights dim) and the screen offers no "play again" button — only a parent-protected action.
- **Warn, then wind down**: if the cap is reached mid-activity, let the current round finish, give a spoken 1-round warning ("Encore une, puis le jardin se repose"), then the resting scene. No sudden black screen (abrupt cut-offs are where transition conflicts arise — inference, consistent with Hiniker's findings).
- **Bedtime lock**: parent sets bedtime; the mode shows the resting garden in the hour before.
- **Off-screen hand-off**: the resting scene ends with a spoken real-world invitation (dance, find an object, help set the table), turning the stop into a transition rather than a loss.
- **Resting state persists** for the rest of the day (the garden "wakes up tomorrow"), framed positively, with no streak or penalty for skipped days.
- Parent area shows today's minutes, so the parent can fit the app inside the ≤1 h total.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence found for an optimal per-session duration for 3-year-olds using learning apps; session numbers above are design inferences from the daily ceiling, not evidence-based thresholds.
- The full Hiniker et al. 2018 paper (quantitative results on tantrums/transition success) could not be read (PDF text extraction unavailable); only press summaries were used.
- No research found specifically testing "resting/sleeping world" end metaphors for preschoolers.
