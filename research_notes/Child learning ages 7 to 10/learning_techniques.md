# Learning techniques with the strongest evidence for children aged 7–10 (grades 2–4), and what they mean for an adaptive math + French reading/spelling app

Research notes, 2026-11-17. No information about any specific child was used in searches.
Limits on access: the EEF website (educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk) returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches, so its figures below come from search snippets of EEF pages or from secondary or older Toolkit editions, each flagged. PMC (NIH) served a bot-check page instead of the article for Wilson et al. 2019. The Sisk et al. 2018 PDF downloaded but could not be parsed.

## Q1. What do the major evidence reviews say about retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, worked examples and feedback, and how strong is the evidence for children 7–10?

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (quizzing) and spaced practice have the strongest and broadest support (Dunlosky 2013: "high utility"; IES 2007: spacing moderate, quizzing for re-exposure strong). Interleaving, self-explanation and elaborative interrogation are "moderate". Most of the evidence comes from older students or lab settings. Direct evidence for ages 7–10 is thin but encouraging: spacing (ages 5–7), interleaved math (grade 4), self-explanation in math (ages 8–11). Elaborated feedback beats right/wrong-only feedback in computer-based settings (0.49 vs 0.05).

### Cited Findings
**Dunlosky et al. 2013, "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology", Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 4–58**
- Ratings: two techniques are "high utility": practice testing and distributed practice. Three are "moderate": elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice. Five are "low": summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning and rereading. — [ScienceDaily summary, 2013](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm); [EurekAlert / APS release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/508403); [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654)
- Practice testing and distributed practice were reported to benefit learners of different ages and abilities across many criterion tasks. The moderate ratings partly reflect how little research exists on those techniques. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)
- The review judged generalizability across four groups of variables: learning conditions, student characteristics (including age), materials and criterion tasks. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=82680)

**IES/WWC practice guide, Pashler et al. 2007, "Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning" (NCER 2007-2004)**
- Recommendations and evidence levels:
  1. Space learning over time: moderate.
  2. Interleave worked-example solutions with problem-solving exercises: moderate.
  3. Combine graphics with verbal descriptions: moderate.
  4. Connect and integrate abstract and concrete representations: moderate.
  5. Quizzing: using quizzes to re-expose students to key content is **strong**; pre-questions to introduce a new topic are minimal/low.
  6. Help students allocate study time: delayed judgments of learning are minimal/low; using quizzes to identify what to study is minimal/low.
  7. Ask deep explanatory questions: **strong**.
  — [WWC practice guide page](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1); PDF (path given on that page, full URL not opened): https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf

**Retrieval practice in classrooms**
- Yang et al. 2021 (Psychological Bulletin), a meta-analytic review of classroom quizzing, found g = 0.50 (49 effect sizes, n = 5,374). 57% of the effects were medium or large. Feedback increases the benefit, but retrieval helps even without it. Question formats worked roughly equally well. — [Learning and the Brain blog (secondary)](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/getting-the-details-just-right-retrieval-practice-2); [PMC article citing Yang 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10950551)
- Agarwal et al. 2021, "Retrieval practice consistently benefits student learning: a systematic review of 50 experiments in schools and classrooms": **only 5 of the 50 studies were in primary schools**, and the authors call for more primary research. — [WGU summary](https://guidance.wgu.edu/patterns/resources/retrieval-practice-consistently-benefits-student-learning-a-systematic-review-of-50-experiments-in-schools-and-classrooms); [TeacherToolkit commentary](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/?p=191348)
- Rowland 2014 (lab meta-analysis, mostly adults) is cited secondhand with a similar g ≈ 0.50. — [Learning and the Brain (secondary)](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/getting-the-details-just-right-retrieval-practice-2)

**Spacing in young children**
- Vlach & Sandhofer 2012 (Child Development; N = 36, ages 5–7): science lessons were given massed, clumped or spaced. Spaced lessons led to better generalization of both simple and complex food-chain concepts. The proposed mechanism is that spacing lets children forget irrelevant details. — [Vlach & Sandhofer 2012 PDF](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Sandhofer-CD-2012.pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3399982)
- Gluckman, Vlach & Sandhofer 2014: in an elementary science curriculum, spacing helped both memory and generalization, but the two measures were not correlated. — [Gluckman et al. 2014 PDF](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Gluckman_Vlach_Sandhofer_2014.pdf)
- Vlach, Ankowski & Sandhofer 2012 (2-year-olds; younger than our target): massed/simultaneous presentation won on an immediate test, spaced presentation won after a 15-minute delay. This shows that in-session performance misleads. — [PDF](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Ankowski_Sandhofer-2012.pdf)

**Interleaving in grade 4 math**
- Taylor & Rohrer 2010, "The Effect of Interleaving Practice" (Applied Cognitive Psychology, DOI 10.1002/acp.1598): fourth-graders practised four kinds of math problems, blocked or interleaved, with spacing held constant. Interleaving **lowered practice-session performance but doubled scores on a test one day later**. — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1760/); [RetrievalPractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2017/interleaving)
- Caution: the often-quoted "72% vs 38%" comes from Rohrer, Dedrick & Burgess 2014 (grade 7, classroom), not from the 2010 grade-4 study. — [RetrievalPractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2017/interleaving); [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2021/10/28-1)
- Proposed mechanism: interleaving trains learners to pick the right procedure for each problem type, which blocked practice never requires. — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1760/)
- IES funded an "Interleaved Mathematics Practice" project (classroom RCT, middle school). — [IES award](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/interleaved-mathematics-practice)

**Self-explanation (math, ages 8–11)**
- Rittle-Johnson 2006, Child Development 77(1), 1–15 (n = 85, ages 8–11, mathematical equivalence): a 2×2 design crossed instruction versus invention of a procedure with self-explanation versus no explanation. — [Rittle-Johnson 2006 PDF](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-sub/wp-content/uploads/sites/280/2023/08/04183033/ATME_Rittle-Johnson_2006.pdf)
- Rittle-Johnson, Loehr & Durkin 2017 (ZDM Mathematics Education), a meta-analysis of prompted self-explanation in math: **small-to-moderate** gains in procedural knowledge, conceptual knowledge and procedural transfer. There are notable cases where prompting did not help or even harmed learning. — [Springer ZDM 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11858-017-0834-z)

**Feedback**
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, "The Power of Feedback Revisited" (435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000): overall d = 0.48, with high heterogeneity. This is well below the d = 0.79 of Hattie & Timperley 2007. Effects grow with the information content of the feedback. Praise, punishment and rewards have low effects. — [Frontiers in Psychology / PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456); [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/pdf)
- Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen 2015, "Effects of Feedback in a Computer-Based Learning Environment on Students' Learning Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis" (Review of Educational Research; 40 studies, 70 ES):
  - Elaborated feedback (with an explanation): **0.49**.
  - Showing the correct answer (KCR): **0.32**.
  - Right/wrong only (KR): **0.05**.
  - Effects were larger in mathematics. They were smaller with delayed feedback and in primary/high-school samples (most samples were older).
  — [ACER publication page](https://people.acer.org/en/publications/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-s/); [Research on Feedback summary (secondary)](https://djplaner.github.io/memex/sense/Learning/learning-research/research-feedback/)
- Mertens, Finn & Lindner 2022 (network meta-analysis of computer-based feedback): elaborated feedback is most likely the most effective type for both recall and transfer outcomes. — [Leibniz IPN](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/effects-of-computer-based-feedback-on-lower-and-higher-order-learning-outcomes)

### Inferences (design rules for the app)
- **Retrieval first.** Every game round should require the child to produce or choose an answer from memory, not re-read or re-watch it. For spelling, use hear → write (dictée), not copy-from-model. For math facts, recall the answer instead of counting dots that stay on screen. (Based on Dunlosky high utility, IES "strong" for quizzing, Yang g ≈ 0.50.)
- **Spaced review scheduler.** Each skill or item should come back across sessions (e.g. next day, then a few days later, then a week later), not be finished in one sitting. Measure mastery with a **delayed** check, not in-session accuracy. The 2-year-old and grade-4 studies both show that immediate performance overstates learning. The exact interval schedule for ages 7–10 is not established by these sources (see Gaps).
- **Interleave once items are known.** Introduce a new type of problem in a short blocked burst, then mix it with earlier types, e.g. addition, subtraction and missing-addend in one round, or the spellings é/er/ez or s/ss in one round, so the child must pick the strategy. Expect and accept lower in-round accuracy. Do not let the adaptive engine read the dip as "too hard".
- **Feedback must explain, not just mark.** After an error, show the correct answer plus a short "why" (an elaborated hint, a visual model such as a number line or ten-frame, or the spelling rule). Avoid a red-X-only design (KR ≈ 0.05). Give feedback immediately for 7-year-olds; delayed feedback did worse in the computer-based meta-analysis.
- **Prompted self-explanation, used sparingly.** Occasionally ask "Pourquoi?" with **choice-based** explanations (pick the reason among 2–3 options), since 7-year-olds' writing is limited. Expect small-to-moderate effects; don't prompt on every item.
- **Worked example → faded → independent.** For new procedures (e.g. regrouping), show a fully worked example, then a partly completed one, then a problem to solve alone (IES rec. 2, moderate). Pair visuals with words (rec. 3) and link concrete and abstract representations (rec. 4), e.g. base-10 blocks ↔ digits.
- **Deep "why/how" questions** (IES rec. 7, strong): include occasional "Pourquoi ça marche?" items in math and "Pourquoi ce mot prend un s?" in spelling.

### Gaps
- No primary-school-specific pooled effect size for retrieval practice was found. Only 5 of 50 classroom experiments in Agarwal et al. 2021 were in primary schools. Yang et al. 2021 age moderators were not checked in the original.
- Exact Taylor & Rohrer 2010 percentages were not verified from the original paper; secondary sources disagree.
- No meta-analysis of worked examples specifically with ages 7–10 was found. The worked-example evidence cited (IES rec. 2) is mostly from older students.
- Elaborative interrogation for young children: no age-specific findings were retrieved. Dunlosky's monograph reportedly notes the evidence is mostly from older learners, but that was not confirmed here.
- Optimal spacing intervals (gap length relative to retention interval) for ages 7–10 were not found.

## Q2. What does the EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit say (feedback, metacognition/self-regulation, mastery learning, individualised instruction, digital technology), in months of progress?

### Takeaway
Per EEF:
- Metacognition and self-regulation: **+8 months** (raised from +7 in the May 2025 update).
- Feedback: about **+6 months** (current edition, per a secondary source; older editions said +8).
- Mastery learning: **+5 months**.
- Individualised instruction: **+4 months**, with limited evidence; digital versions are "as effective as" non-digital ones, not more.

"Digital technology" as a stand-alone strand could not be verified in the current Toolkit.

### Cited Findings
- **Metacognition and self-regulation:** the average impact is about **+8 months'** additional progress over a year. The review was last updated in May 2025 and was previously +7 months. — [EEF strand page (via search snippet)](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation); [EEF news: updated guide to metacognition](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/updated-eef-guide-to-metacognition-and-self-regulation); [Education Commute podcast: "355 Studies, Eight Months' Progress"](https://theeducationcommute.co.uk/episodes/1-355-studies-eight-months-progress-eefs-metacognition-review)
- **Feedback:** a secondary guide gives **+6 months**, with high evidence security from a large body of studies. The October 2014 Sutton Trust–EEF Toolkit listed feedback at **+8 months**. The figure has changed across editions; verify on the live page. — [Structural Learning guide (secondary)](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/eef-teaching-learning-toolkit-guide); [Sutton Trust–EEF Toolkit Oct 2014 PDF](https://thehub-beta.walthamforest.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2019-07/sutton_trust_toolkit.pdf); live page (403 to bot): https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/feedback
- **Mastery learning:** **+5 months**. One edition rates it "moderate impact for low cost, moderate evidence"; a later table says "high impact for very low cost, limited evidence". — [Sutton Trust–EEF Toolkit 2014](https://thehub-beta.walthamforest.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2019-07/sutton_trust_toolkit.pdf); [EEF Mastery learning 2014 PDF](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/eef-eef-mastery-learning-28th-april-2014.pdf); [ASE "Closing the Disadvantage Gap (EEF Toolkit)"](https://www.ase.org.uk/sites/default/files/Closing%20the%20Disadvantage%20Gap%20(EEF%20Toolkit)%20ASE%20IIS.pdf)
- **Individualised instruction:** **+4 months**, "moderate impact for very low cost based on limited evidence". **Approaches using digital technology to individualise instruction are as effective as those without technology.** The 2013–14 editions said +2 months. — [EEF Individualised instruction page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/evidence-summaries/teaching-learning-toolkit/individualised-instruction/); [Sutton Trust–EEF Toolkit Summer 2013](http://www.growthmindsetmaths.com/uploads/2/3/7/7/23776169/teaching_and_learning_toolkit_july_13.pdf)
- **Growth mindset in the EEF portfolio:** see Q3. The EEF contrasts its null growth-mindset RCT with the stronger metacognition evidence. — [Teacherhead: "Mindsets vs metacognition: two EEF reports and a clear conclusion"](https://teacherhead.com/2019/09/15/mindsets-vs-metacognition-two-eef-reports-and-a-clear-conclusion/)

### Inferences (design rules for the app)
- **The biggest EEF levers are metacognition and feedback, not the "digital" part.** The app's value comes from good feedback, retrieval and spacing, not from being on a screen. EEF itself says digital individualisation is only as effective as non-digital.
- **Light metacognition for 7-year-olds:**
  - Before a round: "Je pense que je vais réussir: un peu / beaucoup?"
  - After a round: compare that guess with the actual result.
  - Simple strategy choice, e.g. "Comment as-tu trouvé? compter / je le savais / j'ai utilisé 10".
  - A parent-area view of strategy use.
  - Keep it brief and optional. The EEF evidence comes from teacher-led classroom approaches, not app prompts.
- **Mastery gating (+5 months):** require a mastery criterion on a delayed check before unlocking the next skill, and re-teach (a different explanation or representation) on failure rather than just repeating.
- Months-of-progress figures are averages of mostly teacher-delivered, whole-class programmes across ages 4–16. Don't promise them for an app.

### Gaps
- Current (2021+ Toolkit) figures for feedback, mastery learning, homework (primary), and whether "Digital technology" still exists as a strand could not be verified directly. EEF pages returned 403. The 2018 Toolkit PDF exists at https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/33734/1/EEF-Teaching-Learning-Toolkit-October-2018.pdf but was not opened.
- Primary-versus-secondary breakdowns within each EEF strand were not retrieved.
- The EEF guidance report "Using Digital Technology to Improve Learning" (2019) was not reviewed.

## Q3. What is the evidence on growth mindset interventions for this age?

### Takeaway
The evidence is weak to null for attainment. Sisk et al. 2018 found a weak mindset–achievement link and very small average intervention effects. The large EEF effectiveness RCT in English primary schools (Year 6, 101 schools, 5,018 pupils) found **no** gain in KS2 reading, grammar/punctuation/spelling or maths.

### Cited Findings
- **Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara 2018**, "To What Extent and Under Which Circumstances Are Growth Mind-Sets Important to Academic Achievement? Two Meta-Analyses", Psychological Science, DOI 10.1177/0956797617739704:
  - Meta-analysis 1 (k = 273, N = 365,915): the mindset–achievement relation is weak overall and stronger for children and adolescents than for adults.
  - Meta-analysis 2 (k = 43, N = 57,155): intervention effects are small on average and non-significant for several subgroups (e.g. adolescents, typical students, students in transition).
  — [ScienceDaily 2018](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/05/180522114523.htm); [Purdue lab PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/sisk-et-al-2018-to-what-extent-and-under-which-circumstances-are-growth-mind-sets-important-to-academic-achievement-two.pdf); [Georgia Tech Engle Lab PDF](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf)
- **EEF "Changing Mindsets" effectiveness trial** (NIESR evaluation, July 2019): an RCT with 101 schools and 5,018 Year 6 pupils, run Sept 2016–Feb 2017.
  - No additional progress in literacy or numeracy on KS2 national tests, including for free-school-meal pupils.
  - A small, non-significant positive effect on "intrinsic value".
  - Rated high security, with about 11% attrition.
  - Caveat: many control teachers already knew mindset ideas.
  - EEF advises caution and has no plans for a further trial.
  — [EEF project page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/changing-mindset-2015); [Foliano et al. report, UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10118795/11/Foliano_Changing%20Mindsets.%20Effectiveness%20trial_report_VoR2.pdf); [EEF news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-publishes-findings-from-growth-mindsets-approach)
- **Earlier EEF efficacy trial** (36 schools, KS2): pupil workshops were estimated at +2 months, but teacher training in maths at 0 months. The positive signal did not replicate at scale. — [EEF Changing Mindsets first trial](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/changing-mindsets)

### Inferences (design rules for the app)
- Don't build "growth mindset lessons" into the app as a learning lever; the attainment evidence is null for this age group.
- Keep the low-cost, low-risk parts that are consistent with feedback research:
  - Praise the strategy and effort ("Tu as essayé une autre façon!"), not ability ("Tu es intelligente").
  - Frame errors as normal and informative.
  - Show the child her own progress over time (delayed-retention gains).
  Present these as good feedback design, not as an intervention expected to raise scores.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes from Sisk et al. 2018 (overall r and d, plus age and SES moderators) were not verified. The PDF could not be parsed and search snippets did not give the numbers.
- Later work (Yeager et al. 2019 national study in Nature, adolescents; Burnette et al. 2023 meta-analysis; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023 critique) was not retrieved.

## Q4. How should difficulty be calibrated (desirable difficulties, ~70–85% success, zone of proximal development; the "85% rule")?

### Takeaway
The "85% rule" (Wilson et al. 2019) is a theoretical result for **binary-choice learning by gradient-descent algorithms**. It is not a classroom finding, and the lead author warns against applying it to school grading. The empirically grounded principle for children is that **in-session success is a poor proxy for learning**: interleaving and spacing lower practice accuracy but raise delayed performance. So target a high but not perfect success rate, and judge difficulty by delayed retention.

### Cited Findings
- Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen 2019, "The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning", Nature Communications 10:4646, DOI 10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4:
  - For gradient-descent learners on two-choice tasks with Gaussian noise, the optimal training error rate is ~15.87% (accuracy ~85%).
  - Shown in one- and two-layer neural networks and a perceptual-learning model; training at this difficulty can speed learning exponentially.
  — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/d1aeacdf1f304a25962f05454cebb437); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0v22s4g4/qt0v22s4g4.pdf?t=sdxgqm); [Univ. of Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-eighty-five-percent-rule-for-optimal-learning/)
- Press coverage stresses that the result applies to binary right/wrong tasks, and that Wilson said it should not be read as a general rule for school grading. — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/)
- Desirable-difficulty evidence in children: interleaving lowered grade-4 practice scores but doubled next-day test scores. — [Taylor & Rohrer 2010](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1760/)
- Spacing beats massing on delayed tests even though massing looks better immediately (2-year-olds). — [Vlach, Ankowski & Sandhofer 2012](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Ankowski_Sandhofer-2012.pdf)
- Corrective feedback is especially strong when learning new skills; feedback effects depend on information content. — [Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456)

### Inferences (design rules for the app)
- **Target band:** aim for roughly **75–90% first-try success on review items** and accept lower success (about 60–75%) during interleaved or new-skill rounds. This is an engineering heuristic loosely informed by Wilson et al., not an empirically validated figure for 7-year-olds.
- **Step size:**
  - Raise difficulty by one small step after sustained success, e.g. 3–5 correct in a row across sessions.
  - Lower it, or switch to a worked example or other representation, after 2 consecutive errors on the same skill.
  - Never let the session reach long error streaks: frustration risk at age 7.
- **Measure mastery on delayed checks** (next session or later), not on within-round streaks, so interleaving and spacing are not mistaken for "too hard".
- **Format matters:** for multiple-choice items, chance success (50% with 2 options, 33% with 3) inflates accuracy. Prefer production items (type the answer or spell the word) for mastery decisions, and use multiple choice for early learning.
- **ZPD:** no primary-source quantitative ZPD success rate was found. Treat "70–85%" claims in blogs as heuristic.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that identifies an optimal success rate for children 7–10 in educational software.
- The Wilson et al. full text could not be fetched (PMC bot check), so author caveats are taken from press coverage.
- Bjork's "desirable difficulties" primary sources and Metcalfe's "region of proximal learning" were not retrieved.

## Q5. What pitfalls should be avoided (massed practice, too-easy tasks, extrinsic over-rewarding)?

### Takeaway
The best-supported pitfalls are:
- Massed or blocked practice judged by immediate performance.
- Low-utility techniques: rereading, highlighting, summarization, keyword mnemonic.
- Right/wrong-only feedback.
- Relying on praise and rewards as feedback.

Specific evidence on extrinsic rewards (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999) could not be retrieved in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Rereading, highlighting, summarization, the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text were rated **low utility**. — [ScienceDaily on Dunlosky 2013](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)
- Blocked practice inflates practice performance but halves next-day test performance compared with interleaving (grade 4). — [Taylor & Rohrer 2010](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1760/)
- Massed presentation wins on immediate tests but loses after a delay. — [Vlach, Ankowski & Sandhofer 2012](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Ankowski_Sandhofer-2012.pdf)
- Spaced lessons improve generalization in ages 5–7. — [Vlach & Sandhofer 2012](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Sandhofer-CD-2012.pdf)
- Praise, rewards and punishment show low average effects as feedback; information-rich feedback works better. — [Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456)
- Correctness-only feedback in computer environments: ES ≈ 0.05. Feedback was less effective in primary/high-school samples and with delayed timing. — [Van der Kleij et al. 2015, ACER](https://people.acer.org/en/publications/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-s/)
- Prompted self-explanation sometimes fails or harms learning, so it is not a universal add-on. — [Rittle-Johnson et al. 2017, ZDM](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11858-017-0834-z)
- Growth-mindset programmes as a standalone attainment strategy showed null results in a large primary RCT. — [EEF Changing Mindsets](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/changing-mindset-2015)
- Digital individualisation is no better than non-digital, so tech alone is not the active ingredient. — [EEF Individualised instruction](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/evidence-summaries/teaching-learning-toolkit/individualised-instruction/)

### Inferences (design rules for the app)
- No "finish all 20 of the same item type now" rounds as the main mode: mix and space.
- No "watch / re-read the rule" screens as the practice activity; every practice screen should require retrieval.
- Rewards (stars, garden items) should mark progress and mastery, not every click. Avoid rewards that can be farmed on easy items, and never pay more for speed on items the child doesn't know. This follows the low effect of praise and rewards in Wisniewski 2020; intrinsic-motivation crowding-out evidence was not verified here.
- Avoid too-easy loops: if first-try accuracy on a skill stays near 100% across sessions, retire it to long-interval review and move on.
- Report learning to the parent area as delayed-retention mastery, not raw in-session % correct, which rewards blocked, easy practice.

### Gaps
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (Psychological Bulletin) on extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation, and later work on gamification in children's learning apps, could not be retrieved in this session's search budget. These should be verified before rules on rewards are finalised.
- No direct evidence was found on session length or attention span for 7–10-year-olds using learning apps.
- No sources were found specifically on French-language spelling/reading practice techniques (e.g. dictée, orthographe lexicale) for this age; that likely belongs to an adjacent research task.
