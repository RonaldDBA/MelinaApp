# Cognition, executive functions and mathematical reasoning (ages 7 to 10) — evidence and the Quebec curriculum

Research notes (17/11/2026). Primary sources consulted directly whenever possible; when only an abstract/search snippet was available, this is indicated. Curriculum terms kept in French.

## 1. What does the Harvard Center on the Developing Child recommend for executive functions (ages 7–12)?

### Takeaway
For ages 7–12, Harvard's activities guide recommends card games and board games, strategy games, physical games with rules, music/singing/dancing and puzzles (crosswords, sudoku, spatial puzzles), always with a **progressive increase in difficulty**. It is a practical guide based on the literature, not an efficacy trial per activity.

### Cited Findings
- The guide "Enhancing and Practicing Executive Function Skills with Children from Infancy to Adolescence" has sections by age range, including 7–12 years — [Harvard CDC, guide page](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/handouts-tools/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills/); PDF read (French version hosted on the same site): [EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- General principle of the 7–12 section: games challenge and train EF and self-regulation, and "it is important to progressively increase the difficulty of the games and activities" — [guide PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- **Card and board games**: games in which the child must keep track of the cards played train working memory and flexibility to build a plan and strategy (e.g. Hearts/Dame de Pique, Spades, bridge); fast-reaction games (e.g. "crapette rapide"/Speed) train attention and quick decision-making, since they require monitoring one's own game and the opponent's; for younger children, games of **sorting cards by suit/colour or number** (rummy/gin rummy) test cognitive flexibility — [guide PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- **Strategy games**: "any game that requires strategy" is good training because it forces the player to hold complex actions in mind, anticipate several moves and adjust plans (examples: chess, Go; modern games awarded by Mensa Mind Games); games with an RPG/imaginary-world element (e.g. Minecraft) require keeping information about places, rules and goals — [guide PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- **Physical games**: organized games train holding complex rules and strategy, monitoring oneself and others, deciding quickly; intense, coordinated physical activity (e.g. soccer) can improve EF; jump rope/elastics with chants combine attention control and working memory; vigilance and quick-response games train selective attention, monitoring and inhibition — [guide PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- **Music, singing, dancing**: an instrument trains selective attention, self-regulation and working memory; following a rhythm/improvising (clapping, percussion) and singing in canon/harmony coordinate working memory, attention, flexibility and inhibition — [guide PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- **Puzzles**: those that require remembering information and using it in working memory are "extremely stimulating"; crosswords (letters/words) and **sudoku** (numbers) train working memory and flexibility; spatial puzzles (e.g. Rubik's cube) require flexibility and spatial information; the guide cites Cogmed and Lumosity as computer programs — [guide PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- For adolescents (outside the range, but useful as a "ceiling" for the app): goals, planning and self-monitoring ("is this part of the plan?"), inner speech, post-task reflection on what worked — [guide PDF](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- For an app, the best-fitting types are: card games with memory/tracking (e.g. "which card has already come out?"), sorting games with a changing rule (flexibility, à la switching criterion colour→number), turn-based strategy games with anticipation of moves, children's sudoku (4×4, 6×6) and spatial puzzles — all with adaptive difficulty.
- The mention of Cogmed/Lumosity in the Harvard guide should be read in light of the meta-analysis in section 2 (near gains from training, no far transfer); do not use the guide as an endorsement of "brain training".

### Gaps
- The guide is a collection of practices supported by general references; it gives no effect sizes per activity. The version read is the French one hosted by the site itself (the downloaded PDF was in French); I did not confirm whether there is a more recent English edition.

## 2. "Brain training" and working memory training: what transfers and what does not?

### Takeaway
Working memory training improves tasks similar to the trained ones (near transfer), but does **not** reliably improve IQ, reading or arithmetic (far transfer). Programs that work for EF in children involve repeated practice with increasing difficulty, and those who start weaker gain more. Chess and music also show no robust far transfer in well-controlled studies.

### Cited Findings
- Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(4):512–534: meta-analysis of 87 publications / 145 comparisons; reliable immediate gains in intermediate transfer (verbal and visuospatial working memory), but **no convincing evidence** of improvement on far measures (nonverbal ability, verbal ability, decoding, reading comprehension, **arithmetic**) when compared with an active control; working memory gains did not predict far gains — [PMC4968033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033) (content via summary/abstract; full text not accessible by fetch)
- Meta-analysis in typically developing children (Sala & Gobet, *Developmental Psychology*): significant effect on skills close to working memory training (ḡ = 0.46) and little evidence of far transfer (ḡ = 0.12) — [PMC5724589](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724589) (via search snippet)
- Sala & Gobet (2017), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 26(6):515–520: two meta-analyses on chess and music in children; effects are **inversely proportional to the quality of the design** (e.g. presence of an active control group); conclusion: far transfer rarely occurs — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417712760); [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/102204)
- Diamond & Lee (2011), *Science* 333:959–964, "Interventions Shown to Aid Executive Function Development in Children 4 to 12 Years Old": reviews computerized training, non-computerized games, aerobic exercise, martial arts, yoga, mindfulness and school curricula; all successful programs involve **repeated practice** and a **progressive increase** in EF demands; children with weaker EF at the start are those who benefit most; in computerized training with young children there was improvement on only some of the trained games and no transfer to unpracticed tasks — [PMC3159917](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3159917/) (content via search snippets; the PMC page blocked the fetch)
- Diamond & Lee also argue that activities that work on EF together with emotional, social and physical development (e.g. martial arts, yoga, aerobics) may be more effective than narrow EF training — [PMC3159917](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3159917/) (via snippet)

### Inferences
- Design implication: do not market the app as "brain training" that improves IQ or grades. EF exercises are worth what they directly train; if the goal is math, EF should be exercised **within** math tasks (e.g. holding a problem's data in mind, switching strategy, inhibiting the "obvious" wrong operation in comparison problems), instead of generic n-back.
- Adaptivity (increasing difficulty) and distributed practice are the two common ingredients worth implementing.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of Melby-Lervåg 2016 or of Diamond & Lee 2011 (cookie block on PMC); age moderators were not verified.
- I did not verify Melby-Lervåg & Hulme (2013, *Developmental Psychology*) directly; the 2016 one reanalyzes and extends it.
- I did not search for specific meta-analyses on action video games or on board games (outside number) for ages 7–10.

## 3. What do the IES/WWC guides recommend for elementary math?

### Takeaway
The 2021 WWC guide (K–6 intervention) has six recommendations, **all with strong evidence**: systematic instruction, precise mathematical language, concrete and semi-concrete representations, the **number line**, deliberate instruction on **word problems** and **timed activities** for fluency. The problem-solving guide (grades 4–8) gives strong evidence for monitoring/reflecting on the process and for teaching visual representations.

### Cited Findings
- *Assisting Students Struggling with Mathematics: Intervention in the Elementary Grades* (WWC 2021006), recommendations and level of evidence: (1) systematic instruction — strong; (2) clear and concise mathematical language, supporting the student in using it to communicate understanding — strong; (3) a well-chosen set of concrete and semi-concrete representations for concepts and procedures — strong; (4) use the **number line** for concepts and procedures and to prepare for advanced math — strong; (5) deliberate instruction on **word problems** to deepen understanding and application — strong; (6) regularly include **timed activities** as one way to build fluency — strong — [WWC Practice Guide 26](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/26); [full PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf); [summary](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC-PraxGuide-Elementary-Math-Summary-508c.pdf)
- The guide itself warns that it is a decision-support tool, not a "cookbook" — [PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC2021006-Math-PG.pdf) (via snippet)
- *Improving Mathematical Problem Solving in Grades 4 Through 8*: (1) prepare problems and use them in whole-class instruction — minimal evidence; (2) help students **monitor and reflect** on the problem-solving process — strong; (3) teach students to use **visual representations** — strong; (4) expose students to **multiple strategies** — moderate; (5) help students recognize and articulate concepts and notation — moderate — [WWC Practice Guide 16](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/16)
- **Linear** numbered board game (Siegler & Ramani): four 15-min sessions eliminated numerical estimation differences between low-income children and their peers; versions with colours instead of numbers had no effect; linear boards work, circular ones do not; gains stable for ~9 weeks; replicated in small groups in class; 2009 study rated by the WWC as an RCT that meets standards without reservations — [IES award](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/improving-childrens-pure-numerical-estimation); [WWC study 73675](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/study/73675); [Ramani et al. 2012 PDF](https://education.umd.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/Ramani%20et%20al.%2C%202012%20%281%29.pdf)

### Inferences
- For ages 7–10, the combination best supported by the evidence is: the number line as the central representation (whole numbers up to 1000/100 000, then fractions and decimals); word problems with explicit teaching of structure (ajout/retrait, réunion, comparaison — which matches the PFEQ); short, timed practice of faits numériques **after** they are built with strategies; and self-monitoring prompts ("what am I looking for? does this make sense?").
- The Siegler & Ramani studies are with preschoolers; for ages 7–10 the analogue is estimation on a 0–100 / 0–1000 line and, later, fractions on the line.
- Timed activities: the guide supports them as **one** way to build fluency, not the only one; in an app for a child, the timer should be optional/gentle so as not to create anxiety (inference, not verified in the source).

### Gaps
- I did not read the internal detail of the recommendations (pages on "schema instruction" for word problems and on the format of timed activities); only the titles and levels of evidence were confirmed.
- I did not consult the guide *Teaching Math to Young Children* (preschool/kindergarten), less relevant for ages 7–10.

## 4. What does the EEF guide "Improving Mathematics in Key Stages 2 and 3" recommend?

### Takeaway
November 2017 guide (systematic review led by Jeremy Hodgen) with eight recommendations; those that matter for an app are: assessment to start from what the student knows, **manipulatives and representations** explicitly linked to the mathematical ideas (without becoming a crutch), **teaching problem-solving strategies**, a rich network of knowledge (including fact fluency) and developing **independence and motivation (metacognition)**.

### Cited Findings
- Published in November 2017, eight recommendations, based on a systematic review by Hodgen, Foster and Marks — [EEF news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-publishes-guidance-to-help-teachers-boost-maths/); [UCL IOE](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/ioe/news/2017/nov/practical-and-evidence-based-recommendations-teaching-mathematics) (both blocked the fetch; content via search snippets)
- Recommendations confirmed by the snippets: use assessment to build on existing knowledge; use manipulatives and representations, making students understand the link between the material and the mathematical idea and avoiding dependence; teach problem-solving strategies; enable students to develop a rich network of mathematical knowledge; develop independence and motivation; support the primary→secondary transition — [Third Space Learning, summary](https://thirdspacelearning.com/blog/2017-eef-mathematics-report-close-the-attainment-gap-primary); [Univ. Nottingham](https://nottingham.ac.uk/education/news/news-items/news1718/improving-maths.aspx)
- Stated goal: help schools reduce the attainment gap between disadvantaged students and the others — [EEF news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-publishes-guidance-to-help-teachers-boost-maths/) (via snippet)

### Inferences
- Strong convergence between EEF and WWC: representations (concrete → semi-concrete → symbolic, with an explicit link), problems with taught strategies, and metacognition. In the app: show the same number in base-10 blocks, on the line and as a symbol side by side, and gradually remove the visual support.

### Gaps
- The official EEF page returned 403; the exact wording and numbering of recommendations 6 and 7 (from memory: "use tasks and resources to challenge and support" and "use structured interventions") **were not verified** in this session. One secondary source listed an item about "teacher feedback/comments" that may be an error in the summary.

## 5. Quebec (MEQ): PFEQ and Progression des apprentissages in mathématique — 1er cycle and start of the 2e cycle

### Takeaway
The elementary math PFEQ has three competencies: **(1) Résoudre une situation-problème mathématique; (2) Raisonner à l'aide de concepts et de processus mathématiques; (3) Communiquer à l'aide du langage mathématique**. The content is in five fields (arithmétique, géométrie, mesure, statistique, probabilité). The numerical benchmark is: **nombres naturels < 1000 in the 1er cycle (1re–2e)**, **< 100 000 in the 2e cycle (3e–4e)**, < 1 000 000 in the 3e cycle.

### Cited Findings
**Official documents**
- Official page (updated on 02/02/2026) with: Programme d'études (PDF), Progression des apprentissages (PDF), Cadre d'évaluation des apprentissages (PDF) and the program's Aide-mémoire (training resource) — [Québec.ca – Mathématique primaire](https://www.quebec.ca/education/prescolaire-primaire-et-secondaire/programmes-formations-evaluation/programme-formation-ecole-quebecoise/primaire/mathematique); [Programme](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf); [Progression](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf); [Cadre d'évaluation](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/cadres-evaluation/primaire/francais/PFEQ-cadre-evaluation-mathematique-primaire.pdf); [Aide-mémoire](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/formations/Aide-memoire-programme-math-primaire.pdf)
- The page does not mention a new or revised program — [Québec.ca](https://www.quebec.ca/education/prescolaire-primaire-et-secondaire/programmes-formations-evaluation/programme-formation-ecole-quebecoise/primaire/mathematique)
- The Progression des apprentissages read (a third-party copy identical to the ministerial document) is dated **6 octobre 2009**; it uses three states per year: learns with teacher intervention / does it alone by the end of the school year / reuses the knowledge — [copy read](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf); official version at the link above

**Competencies (PFEQ, program text)**
- C1 Résoudre une situation-problème: components — décoder les éléments, modéliser, appliquer différentes stratégies, valider la solution, partager l'information. A problem situation is not an application exercise; it involves decoding, modelling, checking, making explicit and validating, and must include metacognitive reflection; problem statements may have complete, superfluous, implicit or missing data — [Programme PFEQ](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- C1, end of the **1er cycle**: solves a problem situation with **complete data**, identifies the task and the useful data using objects, drawings, tables, diagrams, symbols or words; solution in **one or two steps**; checks occasionally; communicates with elementary mathematical language. End of the **2e cycle**: there may be **more than one type of data** (implicit data), uses diagrams, **anticipates the result**, solution in several steps, **validates** and communicates with "élaboré" language — [Programme PFEQ](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- C2 Raisonner: components — cerner les éléments de la situation, mobiliser concepts et processus, appliquer des processus, **justifier** des actions ou des énoncés. 1er cycle: observes simple numerical and geometric patterns, connects numbers and operations, tries simple chance activities, interprets and builds everyday diagrams. End of the 1er cycle: creates **personal processes** for addition and subtraction (mental and written), builds plane figures and solids, measures lengths and time. End of the 2e cycle: personal processes for the **four operations**, written **conventional processes** for addition and subtraction (natural numbers and decimals), describes figures and solids, begins to estimate/measure/calculate lengths, surfaces and time, produces **frises and dallages par réflexion**, simulates chance activities, interprets and builds **diagrammes à ligne brisée** — [Programme PFEQ](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- C3 Communiquer: components — s'approprier le vocabulaire, établir des liens entre langage mathématique et langage courant, interpréter ou produire des messages. End of the 1er cycle: elementary language and **at least one mode of representation**; end of the 2e cycle: elaborate language and **more than one mode**, including diagrams — [Programme PFEQ](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf)

**Content (Progression 2009 and the PFEQ's Savoirs essentiels)**
- Natural numbers: < 1000 (1er cycle), < 100 000 (2e), < 1 000 000 (3e). Actions: count in increasing/decreasing order and **by jumps**; count collections by grouping; read/write; represent with material where grouping is visible (tokens, cubes, bags of 10) → structured material (base-10 blocks, number chart) → symbolic place value (abacus, money); compose/decompose (e.g. 123 = 100 + 20 + 3 = 50 + 50 + 20 + 3); equivalent expressions; compare/order; **place on the number line**, grid and number strip; even/odd; classify numbers by properties; **approximation** of collections — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Vocabulary/symbols of the 1er cycle (first block of the table): groupement, chiffre, nombre, unité, dizaine, centaine, nombre pair/impair, est égal à, plus grand/plus petit que, ordre croissant/décroissant, **droite numérique**; symbols 0–9, <, >, =. 2e cycle: base dix, valeur de position, millier, nombre carré/premier/composé, ≠ — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Fractions (always with concrete material or diagrams): recognize everyday fractions; represent from a whole or a collection; part of a whole (equivalent parts); numerator × denominator; compare to 0, ½ and 1; equivalence; order; place on the line. Initial vocabulary: fraction, demi, tiers, quart; then numérateur, dénominateur. The text insists on building **meaning** before calculation — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf); [Programme PFEQ](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Decimals: up to hundredths (2e cycle) and thousandths (3e cycle) — [Programme PFEQ](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Meaning of operations: addition/subtraction as **transformation (ajout, retrait), réunion, comparaison**, then composition of transformations (positive/negative, then mixed); multiplication/division as rectangular arrangement, repeated addition, Cartesian product, sharing and contenance (with material and diagrams); equality relation (e.g. 3 + 2 = 6 − 1); equivalences through commutativity, then associativity and distributivity; **missing term** (a + □ = c, □ − b = c etc.) — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Operations: approximating the result; **répertoire mémorisé** of addition (0+0 to 10+10) and the corresponding subtractions, built with material, drawings, grid or table, then strategies linked to the properties, then mastery; likewise multiplication (0×0 to 10×10) and divisions; official note: the memorized repertoire "requires more than the simple memorization of the times tables"; mental calculation with personal processes; personal written calculation with numbers < 1000 and then conventional with up to 4 digits; **non-numerical** patterns (colours, shapes, sounds, gestures) and numerical ones; **complete a sequence** of at least 3 given terms; calculator (+, −, =) — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Geometry: locating oneself and objects in space (spatial relations), location on a plane and on an axis; Cartesian plane (1st quadrant, then 4); solids (boule, cône, cube, cylindre, prisme, pyramide): compare, build, identify; faces, sommets, arêtes; development (nets) of prisms and pyramids; plane figures: closed curved/broken lines, identify and describe carré, rectangle, triangle, losange, cercle; then convex/non-convex polygons, parallel/perpendicular lines, quadrilaterals; frises and dallages (isometric figures, patterns with figures, reflection, then translation) — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Measurement: compare lengths, build rulers, measure with non-conventional units and then conventional ones (m, dm, cm → mm → km), perimeter; area and volume (non-conventional → conventional); angles (compare; right/acute/obtuse); capacity, mass (non-conv. → conv.); time (day, hour, minute, second; daily/weekly/yearly cycles); temperature (°C) — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Statistics: formulate enquête questions, collect and organize data in tables, interpret and represent with **diagrammes à bandes et à pictogrammes** (1er cycle), then **à ligne brisée** (2e), circle graph only interpreted and the mean in the 3e — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Probability: recognize variability (uncertainty), equiprobability, independence between throws; experiment with spinners, 6/8/12-sided dice, marbles; predict qualitatively with the **droite des probabilités**: résultat **certain / possible / impossible**, then événement **plus / également / moins probable**; distinguish prediction from the result obtained; record in tables; count possible outcomes (simple → table/tree). The document notes the typical misconception: after yellow comes up three times, the child expects red to come up — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Appendix of cognitive and metacognitive strategies (planning: "What task must I carry out? What information is relevant? Do I need to divide it into subproblems?") — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)

### Inferences
- Mapping by cycle, inferred from the order of the items in the Savoirs essentiels + "attentes de fin de cycle" (the arrows/stars per year are graphical and were not extracted):
  - **2e année (end of the 1er cycle)**: natural numbers < 1000 with place value; addition/subtraction with personal processes (mental and written) and mastery of addition facts up to 10+10; meaning of ajout/retrait/réunion/comparaison; missing term; everyday fractions (demi, tiers, quart); even/odd; sequences; number line; basic figures and solids; m/dm/cm and time; table, bar graphs, pictographs; certain/possible/impossible. Multiplication and division already appear as **meaning** (repeated addition, sharing) with material, without memorized times tables.
  - **3e année (start of the 2e cycle)**: natural numbers up to 100 000; times tables 0×0 to 10×10 under construction; conventional addition/subtraction algorithms; fractions of a whole/collection compared to 0, ½ and 1; decimals up to hundredths (probably more in 4e); mm and perimeter; areas with non-conventional units; quadrilaterals, parallel/perpendicular lines; frises by reflection; broken-line graph; more/equally/less probable.
- "Going beyond the curriculum" coherently: the PFEQ's own competencies (implicit/superfluous data, validation, justification, multiple modes of representation) are the natural axis for extension — more than moving on to bigger numbers.

## 6. Logical reasoning tasks suited to ages 7–10 and the evidence

### Takeaway
The best evidence of trainability with some effect beyond the task is for **inductive reasoning** (classification, series, analogies, patterns — Klauer's program) and **spatial reasoning** (rotation, visualization, construction). Both also appear in the PFEQ (patterns, classification of numbers/figures, frises, nets, location). For chess and "brain training", far transfer does not hold up.

### Cited Findings
- Klauer & Phye (2008), *Review of Educational Research*: meta-analysis of 74 inductive reasoning training experiments with children (≈3 600 children according to one summary; another summary gives N = 1 325 — **unresolved conflict**); effects on intelligence tests increased over time and **transfer to academic learning was greater** than to intelligence tests; the results are not explained by placebo or teaching to the test — [Springer/record](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF03172941); [Hogrefe](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123) (via snippets)
- An expanded analysis (109 studies) reported d = 0.60 on fluid intelligence and d = 0.68 on academic learning — [Hogrefe](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1024/1010-0652/a000123) (authorship not confirmed by the snippet)
- Uttal et al. (2013), *Psychological Bulletin* 139(2): 217 studies; mean spatial training effect g = 0.47 vs. control; **durable** gains (not affected by the interval until the post-test) and with transfer to other untrained spatial tasks — [Temple University](https://now.temple.edu/news/2012-08-01/spatial-skills-may-be-improved-through-training-new-review-finds); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/07/120725120634.htm)
- Yang et al. (2020), *Frontiers in Psychology*: 20 spatial training studies in children aged 0–8, mean effect 0.96, moderated by study design, sex and type of outcome; small and younger sample — [Frontiers 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.01938/full)
- PFEQ/Progression already include logical reasoning tasks: classifying numbers by properties, completing sequences (≥ 3 terms), non-numerical patterns (colours, shapes, sounds, gestures) and numerical ones, equivalent expressions, frises/dallages, nets of solids, location on a plane, probabilistic prediction — [Progression](https://nunavik-ice.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Progression-apprentissages-mathematique-primaire.pdf)
- Harvard recommends sudoku and spatial puzzles as working memory and flexibility exercises — [Harvard guide](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/EF-Activities-Guide_FINAL-1.pdf)
- Chess and music: effects on cognitive/academic skills disappear in studies with an active control — [Sala & Gobet 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417712760)

### Inferences
- Recommended exercise types for the app (ages 7–10), in order of strength of evidence + PFEQ alignment:
  1. **Number line / estimation** (0–100, 0–1000, then fractions) — WWC strong, PFEQ explicit.
  2. **Word problems with explicit structure** (ajout/retrait/réunion/comparaison; missing/superfluous data; 1–2 steps → several steps) with self-monitoring prompts — WWC strong + PFEQ C1.
  3. **Linked multiple representations** (base-10 blocks ↔ line ↔ symbol; diagram ↔ equation) — WWC/EEF + PFEQ C3.
  4. **Fluency in faits numériques** built through strategies and then practiced in short rounds (optional timer) — WWC strong + PFEQ "répertoire mémorisé".
  5. **Inductive reasoning**: series/patterns, classification with a rule to discover, figural/numerical analogies, "odd one out" — Klauer.
  6. **Spatial reasoning**: mental rotation, nets, tangram/polyominoes, symmetry/frises, location on a grid — Uttal; PFEQ geometry.
  7. **EF games** with adaptive difficulty (card memory, switching the sorting rule, turn-based strategy games, 4×4/6×6 sudoku) — Harvard/Diamond & Lee, without promising far transfer.
  8. **Playful probability** (virtual spinners/dice, predicting and comparing with results; correcting the "gambler's fallacy") — PFEQ.

### Gaps
- In this session I did not find a specific meta-analysis on **Piagetian seriation/classification** or on **grid-puzzle-type logic problems** for ages 7–10.
- I did not verify evidence on training **estimation** of quantities (approximation) beyond the number line studies.
- I did not look into specific Quebec research (e.g. studies by Quebec universities on math apps) or PISA/PIRLS/TIMSS data by province.
