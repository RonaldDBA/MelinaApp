# Motivation, engagement, feedback, rewards and reports to parents in educational apps (ages 7–10)

Research notes (17/11/2026). Scope: evidence-based design principles for keeping a 7–10-year-old child engaged without undermining intrinsic motivation, and good practices for weekly/monthly reports to parents. Context: home app, non-commercial, French-speaking child in Québec. No personal data was used in the searches.

Method note: several primary PDFs (Deci et al. 1999 and 2001, Sailer & Homner 2020, Springer articles) could not be read in full: the server refused, asked for a login or delivered an unreadable compressed PDF. When a number comes from a secondary summary or a search snippet, this is indicated. Example phrases in French appear only under "Inferences"; they are design proposals, not quotations.

## 1. Self-determination theory and the overjustification effect: which rewards harm intrinsic motivation and which do not

### Takeaway
The meta-analysis by Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), with 128 experiments, shows that **tangible, expected rewards given for engaging, completing or reaching a performance level** reduce free-choice intrinsic motivation, and that the effect is worse in children. **Positive, informational verbal feedback** tends to increase it, as long as it is not controlling. Unexpected rewards, or rewards that do not depend on the task, show no undermining effect.

### Findings cited
- The meta-analysis pooled 128 studies and appeared in *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6), 627–668. According to the authors, tangible rewards reduce intrinsic motivation, and the results "gave strong support to cognitive evaluation theory". The authors also respond to Cameron & Pierce (1994), who had considered the effect "minimal" — [PubMed 10589297](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/); [PDF, Univ. Washington](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- Effects on free-choice motivation: engagement-contingent rewards d = −0.40, completion-contingent d = −0.36, performance-contingent d = −0.28. Tangible rewards as a whole and expected rewards as a whole also reduced motivation. Source: sentence from the abstract located by exact search; the full PDF was not read — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/); [Ewha repository](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-experiments-examining-the-effects-of-ex/)
- A more recent meta-analysis, in a Turku thesis (2023), finds similar numbers: engagement d = −0.42, completion d = −0.48, performance d = −0.24 — [Lehtivuori, Annales Univ. Turku](https://www.utupub.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/173853/Annales%20E%2099%20Lehtivuori%20DISS.pdf?sequence=1)
- Since 2001 both sides of the debate agree that **engagement-contingent rewards reduce intrinsic motivation**. The disagreement continues about performance-contingent rewards (secondary summary) — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001, *Review of Educational Research*](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf); [Ewha: "Extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation in education: reconsidered once again"](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/extrinsic-rewards-and-intrinsic-motivation-in-education-reconside/)
- Later reviews summarize that, in general, positive feedback increases intrinsic motivation because it confirms the sense of competence — [Henderlong & Lepper 2002, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12206194/)
- Henderlong & Lepper (2002, *Psychological Bulletin* 128(5)) conclude that praise can **increase, reduce or leave unchanged** children's intrinsic motivation, depending on the conditions. Studies that cite them point to these determinants: the focus of the praise (person or process/product) and social comparison. Mastery praise increased motivation; social-comparison praise reduced it when the child was uncertain about subsequent performance — [PubMed 12206194](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12206194/); [PDF Henderlong & Lepper](https://jwilson.coe.uga.edu/emat7050/Students/Ramsey/HenderlongLepper2002.pdf); [Corpus & Lepper 2007](https://www.reed.edu/psychology/motivation/assets/downloads/Corpus_Lepper_2007.pdf)
- The classic drawing experiment with preschoolers used an **expected** reward. The overjustification effect is conditional, not universal (secondary popular-science source) — [Structural Learning](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/overjustification-effect)

### Inferences
- For the app: **do not give tangible or exchangeable prizes** (item shops, coins that buy things) **announced in advance in exchange for playing or finishing**. This is the format with the strongest evidence of harm: expected rewards, contingent on engagement or completion.
- Visual progress elements, such as a plant growing in the garden or a growing collection, work better as **information about competence** ("you have mastered more things") than as payment. Cognitive evaluation theory supports this reading; it is not a direct finding about apps.
- Occasional, **unannounced** surprises, with no link to a quantity goal (for example, a new flower appears on some random day), are the least risky format according to the meta-analysis.
- Avoid controlling language ("tu dois", "il faut finir"). Prefer language that offers choice, consistent with the autonomy pillar of self-determination theory.

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of Deci et al. (1999), because the PDF was unreadable. The following remained unconfirmed in the text: the exact d of verbal praise on free-choice behaviour and on interest, the result for unexpected and non-contingent rewards, and the numerical comparison between children and university students. From memory of the abstract, verbal praise is around d ≈ +0.33 and +0.31 and tangible rewards are more harmful for children. These values **were not verified in this session** and must not be cited as fact without checking.
- I also did not read the text of Ryan & Deci (2000/2017) on the three needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. The concept is widely known, but no source was opened to cite it here.

## 2. Gamification for children: what works and what carries risk

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses find **small to medium positive** effects of gamification on learning (g ≈ 0.46–0.50). The cognitive effect is the most stable; the effects on motivation and behaviour are less robust. **Fiction or narrative** and the combination of **competition with collaboration** help. Leaderboards and rankings cause anxiety and jealousy in some students. In children's apps, manipulative patterns are common and paediatric authorities condemn them.

### Findings cited
- Sailer & Homner (2020), *Educational Psychology Review* 32(1), 77–112: gamification has a small positive effect on cognitive, motivational and behavioural outcomes. The cognitive effect held in the most rigorous studies; the motivational and behavioural ones were less stable — [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); [gamification-research.org](https://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)
- Values from Sailer & Homner according to a secondary source: g = 0.49 (cognitive), 0.36 (motivational), 0.25 (behavioural), across 38 publications and 40 experiments. **I did not confirm this in the article** — [gamification-research.org](https://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)
- In the same study, **game fiction** (narrative) and **competition combined with collaboration** were especially effective moderators for behavioural outcomes — [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)
- Bai, Hew & Huang (2020), *Educational Research Review* (DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2020.100322): 30 interventions, 3,202 participants, overall effect g ≈ 0.504 on achievement. In the qualitative synthesis, students liked **feedback on performance** and recognition, and cited **anxiety and jealousy** as reasons for disliking it, which relates to leaderboards and rankings — [Bai et al. 2020, UZH entry](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020)); [Frontiers in Psychology 2023, review citing the study](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full)
- In the studies, leaderboards and rankings usually come **combined with other elements**, such as badges, which prevents isolating their effect — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full)
- Huang et al. (2020), *ETR&D* 68, 1875–1901: g = 0.464 [0.244–0.684] in favour of gamification. The 14 design elements analysed, such as leaderboards, have different effects from one another — [Springer DOI 10.1007/s11423-020-09807-z](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11423-020-09807-z)
- Radesky et al. (2022), *JAMA Network Open*: only **20%** of the apps used by children aged 3–5 had no manipulative feature at all, and about 99% of the children used at least one app with this kind of design. The patterns found were: parasocial relationship pressure (a character that "scolds" the idle child), fabricated time pressure (countdowns), navigation constraints, lures to play more or buy, and ads. Exposure was higher in lower-income and lower-education families — [Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/956153); [deceptive.design](https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children)
- The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), in "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" (page updated on 3/6/2026), says that autoplay, infinite scroll and targeted ads "are not made with children's well-being in mind". It also states that "engagement-based designs" can take the place of sleep, play and family time, and calls for child-centred design — [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Helping-Kids-Thrive-in-a-Digital-World-AAP-Policy-Explained.aspx)

### Inferences
- Elements to keep or prioritize: immediate feedback; visualization of progress per skill; an original, light narrative (the "garden"); choice of activity or order (autonomy); adapted difficulty levels (competence).
- Elements to avoid with a 7-year-old who plays alone at home:
  - leaderboards and rankings, since there is no one to compare with and the emotional effect is negative for some children;
  - daily "streaks" that can be lost;
  - countdowns;
  - a character that complains when she leaves;
  - surprise boxes or prize draws with variable rarity ("loot box" mechanics).
  These items correspond to the manipulation categories of Radesky et al. or to time pressure.
- If there is a day count, it must be **forgiving**: it does not "break", it only accumulates ("tu as jardiné 12 jours ce mois-ci"). This is a design inference; I found no specific study on "streaks" in children.
- Collaboration (for example, a challenge done with a parent) fits the Sailer & Homner finding on collaboration and the "socially interactive" pillar (section 4).

### Gaps
- I could not get the full text of Sailer & Homner or of Huang et al. 2020, because Springer requires a login. Missing: the per-element effects (badges, points, leaderboards) and the results broken down by age group or elementary school.
- I found no robust empirical study on **"streaks"** or loot box mechanics **in educational apps for children aged 7–10**. The recommendations above come from Radesky (ages 3–5), the AAP and self-determination theory.

## 3. Praise and feedback: process praise versus person praise; how to word messages after a correct answer and after a mistake

### Takeaway
Praising the **process** (effort, strategy, persistence) and giving feedback on the **task, the process and self-regulation** is better than praising the **person** ("tu es intelligente"), which leads to avoiding challenges and giving up after mistakes. Effective feedback answers three questions: where am I going, how am I doing, what is the next step. The evidence that "growth mindset" interventions improve grades is weak. So it is worth using process praise, but without promising miracles.

### Findings cited
- Mueller & Dweck (1998), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 75(1), 33–52: six studies with 412 fifth-grade students (ages 10–12). Children praised for **intelligence** came to care more about performance goals than learning goals and chose easy problems more often. Those praised for **effort** preferred hard problems and showed more enjoyment and persistence after failure. The persistence data comes partly from a secondary summary — [PDF Columbia](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf); [Lumen/UCF](https://pressbooks.online.ucf.edu/lumenpsychology/chapter/psych-in-real-life-growth-mindsets/)
- Gunderson et al. (2013), *Child Development*, observational study with 53 children: the proportion of **process** praise parents gave at ages 1–3 predicted, at ages 7–8, preference for challenges, the ability to generate strategies when facing obstacles and the belief that intelligence can be developed. The **total amount** of praise predicted none of this. The study is correlational — [PMC3655123](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123); [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/article/2013/02/12/parents-who-praise-effort-can-bolster-children-s-persistence-self-belief)
- In the 2018 follow-up (*Developmental Psychology*), process praise predicted math and reading achievement in 4th grade, by way of beliefs about traits (fixed or malleable intelligence) — [ECR Hub](https://ecrhub.org/publications/parent-praise-toddlers-predicts-fourth-grade-academic-achievement-childrens)
- Hattie & Timperley (2007), "The Power of Feedback" (*Review of Educational Research*), define four levels of feedback:
  - **task** is useful, but generalizes little;
  - **process** is powerful;
  - **self-regulation** is powerful;
  - **person/self** ("bon garçon/bonne fille") is not very effective.
  According to a secondary source, feedback on task and process has an average effect of 0.65 and feedback only on the self, 0.15. **These numbers were not confirmed in the original article.**
  — [AITSL Spotlight: Feedback](https://www.aitsl.edu.au/docs/default-source/research-evidence/spotlight/feedback.pdf); [BERA blog](https://www.bera.ac.uk/blog/how-to-optimise-the-use-of-hattie-and-timperleys-feedback-levels-for-student-learning)
- On "growth mindset":
  - Sisk et al. (2018): the meta-analysis of interventions (k = 43, N = 57,155) found a weak overall effect, about d = 0.08 — [MSU Today](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2018/05/study-finds-popular-growth-mindset-educational-interventions-arent-very-effective); [PDF Sisk et al.](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf)
  - Macnamara & Burgoyne (2022/2023): the effect drops to d ≈ 0.02 in high-quality studies.
  - Burnette et al. (2023) dispute this: d = 0.14 in target (at-risk) groups with faithful implementation and d = 0.04 in the others. The controversy remains open.
  — [MDPI *Journal of Intelligence* 11(6):104](https://mdpi.com/2079-3200/11/6/104); [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/3959161)
- The EEF guide on teacher feedback ("Teacher Feedback to Improve Pupil Learning") exists, but the page returned 403 and **could not be read** — [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/feedback)

### Inferences (phrases for the app, in Canadian French)
- **After a correct answer**, name the strategy or the effort, not the person:
  - « Tu as compté par bonds de 10, bonne stratégie! »
  - « Tu as relu ta phrase avant de valider : c'est ça, travailler comme une pro. »
  - Avoid « Tu es trop intelligente! » or « Tu es la meilleure! ».
- **After a mistake**, give task or process feedback and offer another try, without judgement:
  - « Presque! Regarde les dizaines encore une fois. »
  - « Essaie une autre façon : dessine les pommes. »
  - Do not use "failure" sounds or animations and do not show an error count in front of the child.
- **Self-regulation**, from time to time:
  - « Comment tu as trouvé la réponse? »
  - « Veux-tu essayer un niveau un peu plus difficile? »
- **Vary and dose the praise.** Gunderson showed that what matters is the type of praise, not the amount. Generic praise at every click loses its informational value, which is consistent with cognitive evaluation theory.
- **Do not turn the app into a "mindset lesson"**: the evidence for explicit interventions is weak. Process praise, built into the feedback, is enough.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full text of Hattie & Timperley (2007) or the EEF guide on feedback (403). The exact EEF recommendations (for example: lay the groundwork for feedback with good initial instruction and make sure the student uses the feedback) **were not verified in this session**.
- There are no specific studies on the wording of feedback **in apps** for children aged 7–10. The phrases above are extrapolations from laboratory and classroom studies.

## 4. Quality of educational apps: the four pillars, UNESCO/OECD and the AAP

### Takeaway
An app is "educational" when it promotes **active, engaged (distraction-free), meaningful and socially interactive** learning, within a clear learning goal (Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015). UNESCO (GEM 2023) warns that the evidence on educational technology is scarce and often industry-funded. The AAP (2026) stopped focusing only on time limits and moved to focusing on quality, context, conversation and shared use with parents.

### Findings cited
- Hirsh-Pasek, Zosh, Golinkoff, Gray, Robb & Kaufman (2015), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 16(1), 3–34:
  - the four pillars are active, engaged, meaningful and socially interactive learning, within the context of a learning goal;
  - an app does not need to have all four pillars, but those that do tend to produce more learning;
  - without a learning goal, an app with all four pillars is entertainment;
  - the framework was designed for ages 0–8.
  — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html); [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/putting-education-in-educational-apps-lessons-from-the-science-of/); [New America](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps/)
- In January 2015 Apple's App Store had about 80,000 "educational" apps, largely untested — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150420123021.htm)
- UNESCO GEM Report 2023, *Technology in education: A tool on whose terms?*:
  - good, impartial evidence is scarce;
  - systematic reviews show a small to medium positive effect, but the research is often funded by technology companies;
  - technology should be used only when it is appropriate, equitable, scalable and sustainable, and it should complement in-person teaching.
  — [INEE](https://inee.org/resources/2023-gem-report-technology-education-tool-whose-terms); [UNESCO IITE](https://iite.unesco.org/news/global-education-monitoring-report-2023-technology-in-education-a-tool-on-whose-terms); [UNESCO shop](https://shop.unesco.org/en/products/gem-report-2023-technology-in-education-2023-a-tool-on-whose-terms)
- AAP, "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" (Munzer et al.; explanatory page updated on 3/6/2026):
  - focus on quality, context and conversation, more than on simple time limits;
  - at ages 6–12, poor use is associated with worse sleep, worse school performance, lower attention control and less physical activity and family time;
  - recommends "quality content that puts the child first", including reading and math content;
  - recommends **co-use**, a shared family tablet instead of a personal tablet, and the *Family Media Plan*.
  — [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Helping-Kids-Thrive-in-a-Digital-World-AAP-Policy-Explained.aspx)
- The AAP defines co-use as being with the child to get to know what they use and says this **is not policing**; with school-age children, it suggests asking permission — [HealthyChildren.org: Why Co-Viewing Is Important](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Why-Co-Viewing-Is-Important-Tips-to-Share-Screen-Time-With-Your-Kids.aspx)
- The AAP's 2016 statement for school age ("Media Use in School-Aged Children and Adolescents") recommends consistent limits on time and type of media, screen-free times (meals) and screen-free places (bedroom). It was partly superseded by the 2026 framing — [AAP summary (PDF)](https://blogcontent.summit-education.com/wp-content/uploads/10.Children-and-Media-Tips-from-the-American-Academy-of-Pediatrics.pdf); [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Helping-Kids-Thrive-in-a-Digital-World-AAP-Policy-Explained.aspx)

### Inferences
- How to check the app against the four pillars:
  - **Active**: the child manipulates, decides, writes; she does not just tap to move forward.
  - **Engaged**: no distractions that compete with the goal, such as long reward animations between rounds.
  - **Meaningful**: linked to her life and to the Québec curriculum (PFEQ).
  - **Socially interactive**: activities to do with the parents and the report as a bridge for conversation.
- Short sessions with a **natural ending** ("le jardin se repose") instead of an invitation to "just one more", in line with the AAP's critique of engagement design.
- A shared tablet or PC and use in the common living space are consistent with the AAP.

### Gaps
- I did not open any **OECD** document (for example, *Digital Education Outlook 2023* or *Educating 21st Century Children*). Nothing from the OECD was verified in these notes.
- I did not open the full text of GEM 2023 or of Hirsh-Pasek (2015). The details come from official summaries (APS, INEE, UNESCO) and popular coverage (New America).
- I did not look for guidance specific to Québec or Canada, such as the Société canadienne de pédiatrie or the MEQ, on screens at school age.

## 5. Progress reports to parents: what to show, how to show it and how to talk with the child

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on "app reports for parents" is scarce. The best indirect sources are the EEF guide on working with parents, which relies on positive communication, practical suggestions and helping the child set goals without doing the task for them, and research on standards-based or competency-based report cards, which parents prefer to traditional report cards, but whose "maîtrise/compréhension" labels confuse. Hence a report per **skill**, with **trend**, a few concrete suggestions and no comparison with other children.

### Findings cited
- EEF, *Working with Parents to Support Children's Learning* (December 2018), with four recommendations:
  - critically review how the school works with parents;
  - provide practical strategies for learning at home, such as shared reading;
  - tailor communication to encourage participation;
  - offer more intensive support when needed.
  — [EEF guidance report](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/supporting-parents); [PDF EEF](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk//32623/1/EEF_Parental_Engagement_Guidance_Report.pdf); [summary of recommendations](https://barnet.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2023-04/EEF-Parental-Engagement-Summary-of-recommendations.pdf)
- Other points from the same guide:
  - for homework, support **routines and good habits** and be **cautious about direct help from parents**, especially with older children;
  - parents can help the child **set goals and manage time and effort**, which helps self-regulation;
  - communication should avoid stigmatizing or blaming families.
  — [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/supporting-parents); [EEF news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-guidance-for-schools-on-engaging-parents/)
- The EEF acknowledges that rigorous evidence on parental engagement is **weaker** than in other areas, such as literacy. The Toolkit estimates a gain of about **+3 months** per year with effective parental engagement — [EEF guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/supporting-parents)
- Swan, Guskey & Jung (2014), *Educational Assessment, Evaluation and Accountability*: 115 parents from one district received traditional report cards and standards-based report cards and **overwhelmingly preferred the standards-based report cards**. The sample is small and local — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11092-014-9191-4); [PDF Bath](https://people.bath.ac.uk/edspd/Weblinks/MA_Ass/Resources/Misc/Swan%20et%20al%202014%20EAEA.pdf)
- Guskey (2004) tested labels from US and Canadian standards-based report cards with parent focus groups. Labels such as "Understanding/Quality" and "Mastery/Proficiency" proved **especially problematic** for parents to understand — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/232599797.pdf)
- Guskey also advocates **keeping the same report card format** from one year to the next, so parents do not have to relearn how to read it — [Corwin Connect](https://corwin-connect.com/?p=16887)
- IES/REL Midwest compiled research on elementary report cards and on communicating results to parents, without assessing its quality — [IES REL Midwest](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/edlabs/regions/midwest/askarel/2020/developing-elementary-student-report-cards.aspx)
- **Social-comparison** praise reduced intrinsic motivation under uncertainty; mastery praise increased it (Corpus & Lepper, citing Henderlong & Lepper) — [Corpus & Lepper 2007](https://www.reed.edu/psychology/motivation/assets/downloads/Corpus_Lepper_2007.pdf)
- Process praise is better than person praise, and the amount of praise predicts nothing (see section 3) — [PMC3655123](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123)

### Inferences (report template for the app)
- **What to show**, per PFEQ skill (for example, "additionner jusqu'à 100", "sons complexes ou/on/an"):
  - mastery status in 3–4 levels with a **plain-language description**, not an abstract label. Example: « réussit seule la plupart du temps » instead of « maîtrise ». This responds to Guskey's (2004) finding.
  - **Trend** relative to the previous week or month (↑ stable ↓), highlighted more than the absolute percentage.
  - Usage time and regularity as **context**, not as a goal.
- **Strengths first.** Then 1–2 skills "under construction", described as a next step and not as a flaw. Close with **one or two concrete suggestions for home**, for example: « Comptez les marches ensemble par bonds de 2 ». This follows the EEF model of practical strategies and blame-free communication.
- **Never compare** with other children, with "the average" or with percentiles; compare her only with her own past self. This is consistent with the harm of social-comparison praise.
- **Frequency**: a short weekly summary (3–5 lines) and a fuller monthly one with trend charts. **I found no evidence comparing weekly with monthly**; this is a design choice.
- **Suggestion for a conversation with the child**:
  - ask what she enjoyed and what she found hard;
  - praise strategies and persistence, citing examples from the report;
  - set **one** small goal for the week together with her, following the EEF on goals and self-regulation;
  - do not use the report as pressure or as the basis for a prize or punishment, so as not to create a performance-contingent reward.
- **Stable format** across weeks and months (Guskey).
- **Show error data only to the parents**, and even then grouped by type ("confond b/d"), because that is actionable. The pedagogical use of error analysis was not verified here.

### Gaps
- **I found no controlled studies** on automatic reports from educational apps to parents, nor any comparison between weekly and monthly frequency. Research on "text messages to parents" (for example, the studies by Bergman and by Kraft & Rogers) may exist, but **was not searched** in this session.
- I did not check research on how parents **interpret progress charts** for children.
- I did not check MEQ (Québec) guidance on communicating results (bulletin unique, competencies), which could align the report's vocabulary with the school's.
