## 1. What the IES/WWC guides recommend (K–3 comprehension 2010; K–3 foundational skills 2016; grades 4–9 interventions 2022)

### Takeaway
The three guides converge: (a) explicitly teach comprehension strategies and text structure; (b) ensure decoding and fluency (daily reading of connected text, repeated reading); (c) ask and answer questions about the text, find the main idea ("gist") and monitor one's own comprehension; (d) create a motivating context. The strongest evidence is for strategies, phonological awareness, decoding and fluency. Discussion and text selection have "minimal" evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **Guide "Improving Reading Comprehension in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade" (Sept. 2010; panel chaired by Timothy Shanahan, with Nell K. Duke, P. David Pearson, Joseph Torgesen and others).** Five recommendations, with the level of evidence:
  1. teach the use of comprehension strategies (**strong**);
  2. teach how to identify and use the text's organizational structure to comprehend, learn and remember (**moderate**);
  3. guide focused, high-quality discussion of the text's meaning (**minimal**);
  4. select texts purposefully (**minimal**);
  5. establish an engaging and motivating context (**moderate**).
  — [WWC Practice Guide 14](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/14)
- **Guide "Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in K–3" (July 2016, revised Dec. 2019; panel chaired by Barbara Foorman).** Four recommendations:
  1. academic language, including inferential and narrative language and vocabulary (**minimal**);
  2. phonological awareness and linking sounds to letters (**strong**);
  3. decode, analyze word parts, write and recognize words (encoding) (**strong**);
  4. read connected text every day for accuracy, fluency and comprehension (**moderate**).
  — [WWC Practice Guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/21)
- **Guide "Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9" (Mar. 2022; panel chaired by Sharon Vaughn, with Margaret McKeown, Michael Kieffer and others).** Four recommendations:
  1. decode complex multisyllabic words (**strong**);
  2. do purposeful fluency activities (**strong**);
  3. routinely use a set of comprehension practices (**strong**), in four parts: 3A build world and word knowledge; 3B provide consistent opportunities to ask and answer questions about the text; 3C teach a routine for extracting the main idea ("gist") from a short passage; 3D teach students to monitor their comprehension while reading;
  4. practise with "stretch texts", texts slightly above the student's level (**moderate**).
  — [WWC Practice Guide 29](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/29)
- The EEF (United Kingdom) rates comprehension strategies at **+6 months** of progress in primary and +7 in secondary, with "high" evidence (141 studies), very low cost and a typical duration of 1 to 3 terms. The UK study base shows smaller effects than the global average. — [EEF Toolkit, Reading comprehension strategies (technical appendix)](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/reading-comprehension-strategies/technical-appendix); [Evidence for Learning (Australian mirror of the Toolkit)](https://evidenceforlearning.org.au/teaching-and-learning-toolkit/reading-comprehension-strategies)

### Inferences
- *Prior knowledge, not verified in this session* (the pages read only give the titles of the recommendations; the details are in the guides' PDFs). From what I remember of the full text of the 2010 guide:
  - recommendation 1 lists the strategies activating prior knowledge/predicting, questioning, visualizing, monitoring/clarifying/"fix-up", inferring and summarizing/retelling, and allows teaching them individually or in combination (for example, reciprocal teaching);
  - recommendation 2 covers story grammar (characters, setting, goal/problem, events, resolution, theme) and informational structures (description, sequence, problem-solution, cause-effect, comparison).
  
  The writer should check this in the guide's PDF before citing.
- For an app, each recommendation becomes a type of exercise:
  - **questions before, during and after reading**, literal and inferential;
  - **"gist" routine**: in one sentence, say who/what and what is most important;
  - **monitoring**: the app asks "tu as compris ce paragraphe?" and offers dépannage strategies;
  - **story maps** (text structure);
  - **repeated reading of short passages** (fluency);
  - **"stretch" texts** with support.

### Gaps
- The WWC page does not show the "how-to steps" (they are in the PDFs, 2.8 to 6.2 MB). I did not extract the PDFs of the North American guides in this session.
- The guides are in English. Transfer to French is direct for comprehension strategies, but not for decoding: French is more transparent than English for reading (see section 4).

## 2. What the EEF reports "Improving Literacy in Key Stage 1" and "Key Stage 2" recommend

### Takeaway
The KS2 report (ages 7 to 11, Melina's range) has 7 recommendations. The 2nd edition keeps those of the 1st (2017) and adds examples:
- develop oral language;
- develop fluency;
- teach comprehension strategies through modelling and supported practice;
- teach writing composition strategies in the same way;
- develop transcription (spelling and handwriting) and sentence construction through extensive practice;
- target teaching based on accurate assessment;
- provide structured support for those who struggle.

The KS1 report (ages 5 to 7) has 8 recommendations and says that spelling should be taught explicitly.

### Cited Findings
- KS2 brings together 7 practical recommendations for students aged about 7 to 11. The 2nd edition keeps those of the 1st (2017) and adds examples and resources. The themes are: oral language; fluency; comprehension strategies "through modelling and supported practice"; writing composition strategies by the same method; transcription and sentence construction with extensive practice, while keeping the student motivated; accurate assessment of needs. — [EEF, Improving Literacy in Key Stage 2](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/literacy-ks2); [EEF news, KS2 2nd edition](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-publishes-updated-key-stage-2-literacy-guidance); [nasen, summary](https://nasen.org.uk/news/new-eef-guidance-literacy-key-stage-2)
- The EEF gives fluency ("speed and fluency") the same importance as accuracy for writing well. — [EEF news, "Speed and fluency as important as accuracy for good writing"](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/speed-and-fluency-as-important-as-accuracy-for-good-writing)
- KS1 (ages 5 to 7, 2nd edition with the same recommendations, plus examples) organizes 8 recommendations into: speaking and listening; reading; writing; assessment and diagnosis; structured individual or small-group interventions. It integrates decoding and comprehension, teaches the student to check their own comprehension and says that spelling should be taught explicitly, focusing on words relevant to the topic or genre being studied. Sources diverge: the 2017 version speaks of 7 recommendations, the 2020 ones of 8. — [EEF, Improving Literacy in Key Stage 1](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/literacy-ks-1); [KS1 2020 PDF](https://greatermanchester-ca.gov.uk/media/4106/literacy_ks1_guidance_report_2020.pdf); [KS1 2017 PDF](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/29344/1/KS1_Literacy_Guidance_2017.pdf); [EEF blog, eight recommendations](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/eef-blog-key-stage-1-literacy/)

### Inferences
- The central EEF point that applies to an app is that strategies are taught by **modelling followed by supported practice, with the support gradually withdrawn** ("supported practice"). In the app, this gives the sequence: the character demonstrates the strategy aloud, the child applies it with hints, then applies it alone.
- The recommendation on transcription and sentence construction supports short, frequent exercises of dictation, **sentence combining** and quick writing, to free working memory for composition.

### Gaps
- The EEF site returned HTTP 403 to the fetch. The exact wording and numbering of the KS2 and KS1 recommendations come from secondary summaries and from the EEF news pages. Check the official PDF before quoting literally.
- I did not confirm whether KS1 deals with handwriting explicitly.

## 3. Evidence on inference training, question types (who/what/where vs. why/how), retelling/summarizing and graphic organizers (ages 7 to 10)

### Takeaway
Teaching inference works: in meta-analysis, a moderate to large effect on inferential comprehension (d ≈ 0.68), a moderate effect on general comprehension (d ≈ 0.58) and a smaller one on literal comprehension (d ≈ 0.28), in both strong and weak readers. Teaching the child to **generate questions** and to **write summaries** also has a consistent effect. Teaching text structure has large effects in K–12 meta-analyses, but smaller and less durable ones at the end of primary.

### Cited Findings
- **Elleman (2017), meta-analysis of 25 inference studies, K–12.** Teaching inference improved general comprehension (d = 0.58), inferential comprehension (d = 0.68) and literal comprehension (d = 0.28), in proficient and struggling readers. — [secondary summary (Shanahan)](https://www.philippinesbasiceducation.us/2017/04/how-should-we-teach-making-inferences.html); [author's Scite profile](https://scite.ai/authors/amy-m-elleman-d3XNV3) (values taken from the article's abstract cited in a library catalogue; full text not read)
- **Rice & Wijekumar (2024), "Inference Skills for Reading: A Meta-Analysis of Instructional Practices".** Moderate, positive effect (g = 0.495) on inference skills and general comprehension, with no significant difference by grade, reading level or multilingualism. — [record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_3065453949)
- **Hall (2016), synthesis on inference with struggling readers.** Large effects on researcher-designed inferential measures, more variable on standardized tests. — [search result: ASHA/LSHSS](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2016_LSHSS-16-0041) (journal attribution not confirmed)
- **Rosenshine, Meister & Chapman (1996), Review of Educational Research 66:181-221.** Teaching students to **generate questions** about the text improved comprehension: median effect of 0.36 on standardized tests and 0.86 on researcher-designed tests. The numbers come from an aggregator; check against the original. — [Formapex, article summary](https://formapex.com/barak-rosenshine/1209-teaching-students-to-generate-questions-a-review-of-the-intervention-studies)
- **Graham & Hebert (2010), "Writing to Read" (Carnegie).** Meta-analysis in which writing about what was read (summaries, notes, responses) improved comprehension by about 0.40 SD (mean of 11 studies). — [Synopsis Writing to Read](https://core-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/documents/asset/uploaded_file/776383/Synopsis_Writing_to_Read.pdf); [LINCS](https://lincs.ed.gov/professional-development/resource-collections/profile-432)
- **Pyle et al. (2017), Reading Research Quarterly.** Teaching (expository) text structure, K–12: mean effect of 0.95 (19 studies, 48 effect sizes). The moderators favour 11 to 20 hours of intervention, **one or two structures taught** and primary students. — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/61fb58ef-54ff-4a0f-a6f3-ae318f6ea0d5); [W&M Literacy Lab](https://education.wm.edu/centers/literacy-lab/translating-research-into-practice/documents/comprehension/teaching-text-structure-boosts-comprehension.pdf)
- **Hebert et al. (2016), J. Educational Psychology.** Expository text structure, grades 1 to 12: ES = 0.96 in students with disabilities or at risk. — [ASHA Evidence Map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/61fb58ef-54ff-4a0f-a6f3-ae318f6ea0d5)
- **Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2020), RRQ, grades 4 to 6.** Smaller immediate effects: recall g = 0.37, summary g = 0.57, comprehension tests g = 0.25. The advantage was not maintained on delayed post-tests. — [article record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2545476694)
- **Distinction between explicit and implicit questions in the Quebec curriculum.** The Progression des apprentissages defines inference as going beyond literal comprehension, "combler les vides laissés par l'auteur" with knowledge of the world. It distinguishes the *inférence logique* (linking pieces of information in the text to create a new one) from the *inférence pragmatique* (deducing what is implied: feeling, time and place, cause/effect, problem/solution). It also separates interpretation from inference. — [MEQ, Progression des apprentissages – Français, langue d'enseignement, primaire (p. 73 and 76)](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Suggested question progression for the app**, following the order of the Quebec program (section 5):
  1. localized explicit (who, what, where, when);
  2. scattered explicit (putting two pieces of information together);
  3. referents of pronouns and substitutes ("il" = qui?), which Quebec already treats as inference in the 1er cycle;
  4. pragmatic inference (character's feeling, cause/effect, "why");
  5. inference combining text and knowledge of the world;
  6. summary (essential vs. secondary, 2e cycle).
- **Retelling and summarizing:** in the 1er cycle, do a "rappel" supported by story grammar (beginning, middle, end; character, problem, solution). In the 2e cycle, summarize by separating the essential from the secondary.
- **Graphic organizers:** the strongest evidence is for text-structure maps, teaching few structures at a time. I did not find a meta-analysis specific to graphic organizers for ages 7–10 in this search.
- **Asking for justification** ("Quel indice dans le texte te le dit?") brings together inference and justified interpretation, which the Quebec program assesses.

### Gaps
- I did not find a meta-analysis isolating the effect of "why/how" questions versus "who/what" questions in children aged 7 to 10. The evidence is indirect (inference training and question generation).
- I did not find a recent meta-analysis specific to graphic organizers in this age range.
- Almost all the evidence on inference and text structure comes from studies in English. Transfer to French is plausible, since it involves comprehension processes rather than code, but it has not been verified.

## 4. Spelling: evidence-based teaching for French (lexical vs. grammatical spelling, agreement, morphology and word families, dictation)

### Takeaway
In French, the difficulty of writing lies much more in **grammatical spelling** (marks that are written but not heard: -s, -e, -nt) than in lexical spelling. The order of mastery is stable: noun plural (-s) → adjective plural → verb agreement (-nt), which is the latest. Gender is harder than number. **Explicit teaching, with rules and exercises**, greatly accelerates this mastery. Metacognitive dictations (dictée 0 faute, phrase dictée du jour), studied in Quebec, improve grammatical spelling, especially among weak students, but the evidence comes from the same team that created the method. A general meta-analysis (in English) shows that teaching spelling formally works (d ≈ 0.54) and also improves reading.

### Cited Findings
- **CSEN (France), synthesis "Rationaliser l'orthographe du français pour mieux l'enseigner"** (Sprenger-Charolles, Abeillé, Cerquiglini; June 2024, revised version in 2025):
  - **Transparency:** French is less transparent than Spanish and more than English, but its spelling makes **writing much harder than reading**, because many morphological marks are not heard ("elle est jolie", "les mouches volent").
  - **Where the errors are:** most are in grammatical spelling. The agreement of the past participle with *avoir* and a preceding object is mastered by fewer than 20% of students at the end of primary.
  - **Consistency:** mots irréguliers are read less well and above all written less well than réguliers. "Onze" has 100% consistency for reading and only 44–45% for writing.
  - **Silent letters:** about 70% of the ~15 000 nouns in Manulex end in a silent letter. Final -s marks number in 96% of cases. The -t is a morphological mark (candidat → candidate) in about 50% of cases, which justifies teaching **word families** to find the silent letter.
  
  — [CSEN, spelling synthesis 2025 (PDF)](https://www.csen.education.gouv.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8eb4a522-f5fe-4524-b3f3-a52fb82d9907-CSENV2Syntheseorthographe2025web.pdf)
- **Same synthesis, on number and gender marks:**
  - comprehension of these marks in reading comes before production;
  - the noun's -s is mastered earlier and better than the adjective's -s, and the verbal -nt is the latest;
  - gender (-e) is more problematic than number;
  - agreements in the verb group cause the most errors, even in adults (proximity errors, such as "Je vous disez").
  
  From CP to CE2, the acquisition of number marks is **earlier and faster when the rules are introduced explicitly and with exercises** than through mere exposure (Fayol, Grimaud & Jacquier, 2013): the plural already appears in grade 1 and correct answers increase in all categories. — [CSEN 2025, p. 21–22](https://www.csen.education.gouv.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8eb4a522-f5fe-4524-b3f3-a52fb82d9907-CSENV2Syntheseorthographe2025web.pdf)
- **Same synthesis, on the past participle:** from CE1 to CE2 (≈ ages 7–8), the correct choice among -é/-ée/-és/-ées stays around 50% and only improves from CM1 onward. The CSEN suggests simplifying the teaching at this age: with *être*, it agrees with the subject; with *avoir*, it stays invariable. — [CSEN 2025, p. 22–24](https://www.csen.education.gouv.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/8eb4a522-f5fe-4524-b3f3-a52fb82d9907-CSENV2Syntheseorthographe2025web.pdf)
- **Graham & Santangelo (2014), Reading and Writing 27(9):1703–1743**, 53 studies, K–12:
  - formal spelling instruction vs. none: ES = 0.54;
  - vs. informal approaches ("spelling is caught"): 0.43;
  - gains maintained over time (0.53);
  - transfer to word reading (0.44) and phonological awareness (0.51);
  - correct spelling within compositions (0.94), but overall composition quality not significant (0.19).
  
  These numbers come from secondary summaries; check against the article. — [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11145-014-9517-0.pdf?pdf=button); [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/does-spelling-instruction-make-students-better-spellers-readers-and); [Texas LD Center](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=356)
- **Metacognitive dictations in Quebec (Nadeau & Fisher).** The dictée 0 faute and the phrase dictée du jour were practised from October to May in 41 classes, from grade 3 of primary to Secondary 3. Result: positive impact on grammatical spelling, with greater progress among weak students. The dictée 0 faute derives from the dictée négociée (Arabyan, 1989). In Quebec, it incorporates the **syntactic manipulations of the "grammaire nouvelle"** as a test of analysis: the student justifies each agreement aloud, and the teacher models. — [FRQ, Nadeau 2014 report](https://frq.gouv.qc.ca/app/uploads/2021/06/pt_nadeaum_rapport-2014_dictee-impact-orthographique.pdf); [FRQ, summary](https://frq.gouv.qc.ca/pt_nadeaum_resume-2014_dictee-impact-orthographique); [Cogis, Fisher & Nadeau, "Quand la dictée devient un dispositif d'apprentissage"](https://didacfran.univ-rouen.fr/sites/didacfran.univ-rouen.fr/files/ressources/article_de_daniele_cogis_carole_fisher_et_marie_nadeaua0_quand_la_dictee_devient_un_dispositif_du2019apprentissage.pdf)
- **Arseneau & Nadeau (2018), minority francophone context.** Metacognitive dictations had a positive and significant effect on mastery of grammatical spelling. Caveat: a 2015 article cites a skeptical inspector, according to whom end-of-year progress was identical with or without preparation of the dictation, and there is no strong independent replication. — [CJAL/Érudit 2018](https://erudit.org/fr/revues/cjal/2018-v21-n2-cjal04434/1058464ar.pdf); [Repères/OpenEdition](https://journals.openedition.org/reperes/742)
- **Éduscol CE1 guide (France, 2019), according to académie summaries.** Dictation and copying serve to consolidate decoding and memorize spelling. Dictation starts from syllables and words with graphemes already studied, correction must be **immediate**, and dictation also serves to assess and remediate. The guide also deals with fluency, reading aloud and "fonctionnement de la langue au service de la compréhension". — [Éduscol, CE1 guide (PDF)](https://cache.media.eduscol.education.fr/file/Cycle_2/17/5/Livre_Lecture-ecriture_2019_CE1_web_1173175.pdf); [synthesis, circ. Périgueux](https://andrezieux-nord.circo.ac-lyon.fr/spip/IMG/pdf/synthese-guide-ce1_circo_perigueux.pdf); [Éduscol, foundational guides](https://eduscol.education.gouv.fr/6681/les-guides-fondamentaux-pour-l-enseignement)
- **Quebec program (2026), on what "learning the spelling of a word" means:** giving it meaning, knowing **all its forms** (ami → amie, amis) and its class, so as to know how it behaves in agreements. — [MEQ, Programme actualisé, Annexe 5](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)

### Inferences
- **For an app in French, the evidence points to:**
  1. short, frequent dictation, with immediate correction;
  2. a word always presented with its inflected forms and with its family (pour la lettre muette: "grand → grande, grandir");
  3. explicit agreement training following the natural order (déterminant+nom → adjective in the GN → subject-verb), always with the justification question: "Quel mot commande l'accord? Pourquoi -s / -nt?";
  4. an individual version of the dictée 0 faute: before submitting, the child "asks for help" about a doubtful agreement and the app returns the syntactic manipulation ("remplace par il/ils", "encadre par c'est… qui"), not the answer.
- Lexical spelling exercises based on English evidence (spelling lists, word sorts) transfer to French. However, the biggest gain in French lies in the silent grammatical marks, which research in English barely covers.

### Gaps
- I did not find in this session an official CSEN document solely on "comment enseigner l'orthographe / la dictée". The CSEN synthesis deals mainly with the rationalization of spelling.
- The Éduscol CE1 guide was read only through secondary summaries. The official catalogue page requires a login.
- I did not find a French-specific quantitative meta-analysis on dictation.

## 5. Quebec MEQ: PFEQ Français, langue d'enseignement (primary), Progression des apprentissages, 1er vs. 2e cycle, and the new 2027-2028 program

### Takeaway
Quebec is in transition:
- **2026-2027:** the old program applies (PFEQ 2001 + Progression des apprentissages 2009/2011, with four competencies: lire, écrire, apprécier des œuvres littéraires, communiquer oralement).
- **2027-2028:** the **updated program** (approved in summer 2026) becomes mandatory. It has three competencies: Communiquer oralement, Lire des textes variés, Écrire des textes variés. Reading is divided into decoding, comprehension, interpretation, reaction and critical judgment. The **Liste orthographique** becomes prescribed, with a defined number of words per year.

For Melina (2e année, end of the 1er cycle), the expectations are:
- **Reading:** decode with some automaticity; read by word groups; find grouped explicit information; infer referents of pronouns and substitutes; do a "rappel"; detect her own "bris de compréhension".
- **Writing:** determiner+noun agreement in simple cases; memorized verbs in the present; most of the words on the 1er cycle list (200 words in 1re année and 300 in 2e).

- **Components of "Lire des textes variés":**
  - Décodage: identifies words "avec exactitude et automaticité";
  - Compréhension: identifies the genre; gives meaning to words and expressions; gives meaning to explicit information; **infers implicit information**; builds the overall meaning; identifies bris de compréhension;
  - Interprétation: attributes, explores and justifies meanings;
  - Réaction: identifies and justifies emotions and effects;
  - Jugement critique: criteria, comparison of texts, justification.
  
  — [Programme actualisé, p. 33](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)
- **End of the 1er cycle (reading):**
  - reads varied, short, illustrated and simple texts;
  - decodes "avec une certaine automaticité" and reads by word groups, respecting end-of-sentence punctuation;
  - **relève des informations explicites regroupées**;
  - **infère quelques éléments implicites notamment en associant les mots qui en remplacent d'autres à leur référent**;
  - builds the overall meaning **"en effectuant un rappel"**;
  - detects her own bris de compréhension and remedies them;
  - reacts and justifies the reaction;
  - makes a critical judgment based on the paratext and the illustrations.
  
  — [Programme actualisé, p. 34](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)
- **End of the 2e cycle (reading):**
  - more developed and complex texts;
  - reads without hesitation, respecting punctuation and sometimes with intonation;
  - **regroupe des informations explicites dispersées**;
  - **infère en combinant des informations du texte pour déduire une information sous-entendue et en faisant des liens entre les causes et les effets**;
  - **résume en distinguant l'essentiel des idées secondaires**;
  - critical judgment with criteria, compares texts, confronts their point of view with that of others and justifies with examples and extracts from the text.
  
  In the 3e cycle: a summary that groups several ideas into a more general idea, plural interpretation, reading "au rythme de la parole". — [Programme actualisé, p. 34](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)

  - 2.2 infer the meaning of an unknown word from contextual, morphological (base, prefix, suffix, gender and number marks) and syntactic clues;

**Updated program: writing**
- **End of the 1er cycle (writing):**
  - short and simple texts;
  - generates, selects and organizes ideas;
  - simple sentences with the necessary words in the right order, delimited by a capital letter and a period;
  - **writes memorized verbs in the present indicative and makes determiner and noun agreements in simple cases**;
  - **gets most of the words on the 1er cycle list right**;
  - writes unknown words "de façon phonologiquement acceptable";
  - uses wall and visual dictionaries.
  - **End of the 2e cycle:** varied sentences; appropriate punctuation in enumerations and dialogues; **agreement of the verbs studied in the different tenses of the indicative and of the adjective in the noun group in simple cases**; words from the 2e cycle list and earlier ones; monolingual dictionary and conjugation tables.
  
  — [Programme actualisé, p. 37](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/programmes/PFEQ-Programme-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)
- **Components of "Écrire des textes variés":**
  - Idéation: genre, generating and organizing ideas;
  - Énonciation: written syntax, progression of information, substitutes, dominant verb tense, vocabulary, register;

**Program in force (Progression des apprentissages 2009/2011)**
- **Reading strategies** (gestion de la compréhension):
  - preparation: survol, reading intention, structure;
  - sentence comprehension: delimiting the sentence, reading by word groups, finding the important information, fixed expressions;
  - text comprehension: formulating and readjusting hypotheses, anticipating what comes next, **identifying what the substitutes refer to**, relationship markers, graphic, lexical and semantic clues, regrouping scattered information, **inferring the implicit (logical and pragmatic inference)**;
  - dépannage: rereading, adjusting speed, rephrasing, consulting tools.
  - **Reading in four stages:** comprehension (including "extraire d'un texte des informations explicites / implicites"), justified interpretation "en s'appuyant sur des extraits du texte (preuves)", reaction and appreciation.
  
  — [Progression des apprentissages, p. 71–76](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)
- **Agreement in the Progression in force:** "Dès la 2e année, l'élève commence à travailler l'accord de l'adjectif avec le nom dans des cas simples", and this work continues until 4e année without mentioning the attribute function. Also from 2e année, the student learns to make the attribute adjective agree after *être* intuitively, without naming the function. — [Progression des apprentissages, footnotes, Grammaire de la phrase/Accords section](https://cdn-contenu.quebec.ca/cdn-contenu/education/pfeq/primaire/progressions-apprentissages/PFEQ-progression-apprentissages-francais-langue-enseignement-primaire.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Suggested alignment for Melina (2e année, transition to the 2e cycle in 2027-2028, already under the new program):**
  - consolidate the 1er cycle level: grouped explicit questions, pronoun referents, rappel, self-detection of bris, determiner+noun, 1re and 2e année list (≈ 500 words);
  - move on to the 2e cycle: scattered explicit, cause-effect inference and inference by combining clues, essential/secondary summary, adjective in the GN, subject-verb in the tenses studied, monolingual dictionary.
- **"Going beyond" the curriculum safely:**
  1. always ask for the proof in the text ("preuve"), in the 1er cycle as well;
  2. teach the WWC 2022 "gist" routine;
  3. train the child to generate her own questions;
  4. use morphological spelling (word families and silent letters) earlier than planned;
  5. do repeated reading for fluency, since the decoding criterion includes automaticity and pace.
- **App word bank:** the official list can be consulted on Usito (according to the MEQ page). The 300 words "of choice" legitimize including words linked to the child's interests.
